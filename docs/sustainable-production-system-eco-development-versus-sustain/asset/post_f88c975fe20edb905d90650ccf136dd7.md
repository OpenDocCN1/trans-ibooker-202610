
To continue this approach to the systemization of social interactions, and to return to economics, it is possible to mention the *plural economy* paradigm developed by Laville (1998), Evers and Laville (2004), as well as by Coraggio (2007). Their approach formalizes and connects different “places” of understanding of the economic functions considered by the *social economy*.

Considering a plural economy makes it possible to highlight the fact that market economies (private entrepreneurial sphere) and non-market economies (notably the public economy sphere) are dependent on relations between people (notably the popular economy sphere). In other words, they only exist within the social sphere. This is also highlighted by Passet (1979) through his approach to the three spheres in the field of ecological economics (Figure 1.5). Social relationships occur first in the family and then in society. Without them, the agents of the monetary economy would not exist. For Laville (1998), “the link precedes the goods”.

By being aware of the limitations of analogy, community psychology and social economy can further echo the perspective of ecological economists.

As we have seen, community psychology invites us to take into account a memory load. This resonates as the critical threshold below which is needed to maintain oneself in order to allow the emergence and maintenance of a property that is not deductible from the elementary composition of the social system: the deployment of communities within the different spaces of relationships.

Social economy refers to the fact that the maintenance of the conditions for the viability of functional interrelationships between elements of a system (the link) must not be overshadowed by the evaluation of the utility of the services, resulting from these interrelationships (the goods).

A critique of the relationship between neoclassical economics and social systems can thus be conducted, in a way that is quite similar to that of its relationship to ecological systems. And the framework of the plural economy is particularly suited to this criticism. Is the logic behind the redistribution of monetary wealth generated within the entrepreneurial sphere not close to that underlying Hartwick’s 1977 compensation proposal? And just as fragile?

Effective redistribution can, of course, influence the achievement of social objectives set at the individual level – relating in particular to *primary social goods* (Rawls 1971) and *social capabilities* (Sen 1992). But the provision of this liquidity does not guarantee the quality of social cohesion within the sphere of popular economy. According to Giraud and Renouard (2010), massive money inflows into a social fabric can, on the contrary, create inequalities and invisible damage even for specific indicators of the social fabric.

For Servet (2007), it is *reciprocity* that allows social connection. And the role of this reciprocity, complementary to that of the market, can be deteriorated when the market is dominant. This reciprocity could also be described as a functional social interaction. It is the result of a community emergence whose continuity must refer to the consideration of *critical thresholds*. Redistribution can also be described as a functional social interaction – an economic interaction within the social sphere – if we consider that the economy, as a mode of recognizing reality, only exists within the social sphere.

This is what the theory of *exosomatization* developed by Georgescu-Roegen (1971) following Alfred Lotka and now mobilized by Stiegler (2016) invites us to do. For these authors, technologies are part of an *exorganological* reality; in other words, machines are *exorganisms* – external organs – whose production within biochemical living organisms emanates primarily from a human decision within social living organisms.

If we continue in this logic, the entire economy – even the industrial economy – would only be a combination of the ecological and the social realities. The economy as such would only exist because of the need to
maintain the relationship between ecology and society. This interpretation is close to what the concept of eco-development proposes.

And if we continue the reasoning made possible by the theory of exosomatization, it is not the notions of natural capital and “human” capital that are based on a metaphor of economic capital. It is economic capital that represents the result of the interpenetration of ecological and social systems throughout human evolution.

Let us return here to the notion of the *labor factor* which, integrated by neoclassicals into the equation of global productivity, refers for them to the activation of an overall capacity considered as homogeneous and fungible. This capacity is represented by a number following a calculation based on a representation of some of the qualities of the social system. While this approach satisfies the needs of the analyst in a neoliberal industrial economy, it negates the dynamic, detailed and localized nature of people’s individual capacities, for example in terms of skills and health.

The notion of human capital, if associated with a homogeneous, non-specific – disindividualized – understanding and only oriented toward the availability of the labor factor considered by neoclassicals, therefore seems ineffective. Mobilizing the notion of *social capital* is, in our opinion, preferable. This notion insists – more than on the availability of activatable capacities for work as understood by neoclassicals – on the capacities that people have, thanks to their singularities to develop functional links.

This choice does not necessarily imply totally abandoning the analytical object that neoclassics describe by the notion of human capital, but allows this object to be included in an evaluation located within a space of social interrelationships. It makes it possible to include in economic practice the weighting of qualitative objectives for the *ex ante* maintenance of social systems and to ensure that this weighting is not overshadowed by that of quantitative redistribution objectives.

## 1.2.2. Spaces and temporalities of productive phenomena

### 1.2.2.1. Wealth, values, market and applied ethics

The sustainable development economy must be a *political economy*, in the sense of Polanyi (1944), i.e. an economy of the re-embedding of the
economy in the social and democratic relationship. This re-embedding conditions the comparison of the objectives of sustainability of social and ecological systems, as well as the resilience of their common emergence – the economy – with objectives of valorization, i.e. value recognition.

For Crozet (2004), economic calculation must be approached “not as a single and intangible reference, but as a support for the different stages of the political process”. According to Bourg (2009), a process framed by the consideration of certain fundamental values related to sustainable development must be added to market-based neoclassical valuation.

The political economy of sustainable development, if we wish to give an overview of it through the opposites it articulates, can then be described by the relationship between two approaches to “value recognition”: applied ethics and the market.

Applied ethics is a discipline oriented toward reflection in situations; its practice leads to prioritizing the value systems carried by individuals and allows the evaluation of actions envisaged in a concrete contextualized situation (Létourneau and Moreault 2006). It is an adaptation approach, which offers the ability to discern the specificities of issues associated with different scales of perception of reality and different scales of decision making. Ethics refers to three levels: the universal, the particular (which relates to several) and the singular (which relates to only one). It thus embraces the emerging phenomenon of political decision making as a whole, by strengthening capacity, both in institutional matters and in data collection and management (ibid.).

In our opinion, the term market describes the emerging property of a social system, constituted around the institutionalization of a set of methods and practices that give rise to a homogeneous space of representations, shared by the actors of this space. And the use of these methods allows the individuals who make up the market communities to produce universal and definitive prices, i.e. social representations, at any time and in a recursive way.
