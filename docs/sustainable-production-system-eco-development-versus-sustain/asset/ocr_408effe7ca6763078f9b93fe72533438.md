For Sachs (1981, p. 139), “Development is a whole. The cultural, social, economic, institutional, political and ecological dimensions must be addressed in their interrelationships through an integrated policy”. According to him, it is a question of thinking “of a set of normative criteria and questions rather than a rigid model” (Sachs 1990, p. 3), and of solutions “involving simultaneous respect for social, cultural, ecological, environmental, territorial, economic, political and international indicators” (Sachs 1997, pp. 84–85).

This requirement for a multi-indicator analytical perspective, which is at the heart of Sachs’ conception of the representation of development, is a defense against a neoliberal capture of sustainable development.

Such a capture would lead to a predominance of integration by the market, to solutions through factorization, thus to the fusion of the realities described by the indicators, and to an equation compatible with the logic of global productivity.

On the other hand, simultaneously meeting the requirements described by several indicators implies much more than having the expertise applicable to a level of reality. This requires knowledge adapted to the simultaneous mobilization of several methods and tools dedicated to the recognition of the specificities of phenomena registered in different realities, as well as to the way in which the information resulting from this recognition is put under tension.

Such an undertaking does not therefore imply rejecting any neoclassical practice. It consists of anchoring any neoclassical practice, for example an analysis of decoupling, in a broader socioeconomic approach that addresses the same subject but allows it to be addressed within a robust overall framework. In other words, it is a path of recognition aimed at placing neoclassical approximations – caused, for example, by the use of comparative growth rates – in a state of structured uncertainty.

In economic evaluation practices, this type of approach is often associated with multi-criteria decision-support methods (Roy 1985; Roy 1991; Roy and Vincke 1981).
