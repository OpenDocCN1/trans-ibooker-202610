Theories of *co-evolution* (Norgaard 1984, 1988, 1992; Hodgson 2010) address the comparative dynamics of the relationships between the economy, society and the environment. More generally, a set of systems is in co-evolution when a change in part of one of the systems causes multiple changes in the feedback between these systems (Faucheux and Noël 1995).

Linked to these theories, the concept of *resilience* (Holling 1973) is useful in identifying critical thresholds and functions within ecological and socioeconomic systems. This concept can be considered as “the ability of a system to absorb shocks, sources of dysfunction, and to reorganize itself while changing so as to maintain essentially the same function, structure, identity and feedback” (Walker et al. 2004).

It is often associated with the *adaptive cycle* model (Holling 1995), as well as with the *panarchy* model (Holling et al. 2002), which proposes a spatial and temporal interweaving of descriptive adaptive cycles of socioecological systems. In a panarchical representation, it is the interaction between phenomena occurring at different scales that should be the focus of attention (Levin 1992).
