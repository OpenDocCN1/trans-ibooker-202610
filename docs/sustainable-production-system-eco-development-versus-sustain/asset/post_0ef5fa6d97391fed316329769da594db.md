*Ecological economics* offers a very different understanding of what natural capital is, how it is composed of and therefore how to value it. For ecological economists, natural capital does not have the same standard properties of substitutability as those lent by neoclassicals to economic and labor capital (Faucheux and Noël 1995). In their view, to argue otherwise would be to deny the originality of the physical characteristics that determine the interrelationships between natural elements, and their inclusion in the biochemical cycles from which society receives services (*ibid.*).

It is the complementarity of the components of natural capital that must be emphasized (Pearce and Warford 1993). The total substitution of one of these elements by a technology could only lead to a global disturbance in the ecosystem and by repercussion in the economy (Costanza and Daly 1992; Barbier and Markandya 1990).

The approach of ecological economists thus leads us to consider that natural capital is structurally more fundamental than economic capital. It has an irreplaceable endowment character, since it is not productive by human societies (O’Connor 1993). It is an integrated whole that responds in a nonlinear way to the disruption of ecosystem functions, including their self-purification and regeneration functions. Above certain thresholds, called *saturation thresholds* – or *critical thresholds* – some of these functions, those that ensure natural balances, are deteriorated. A portion of natural capital is then referred to as *critical natural capital* (Ciriacy-Wantrup 1952; Bishop 1978).

The economic valuation of ecosystem services therefore only makes sense if it does not overshadow the approach to maintaining ecosystem functions, i.e. the dynamic qualities and interactions of biochemical elements and cycles. For ecological economists, the aim is to ensure preventive management of natural capital, i.e. to work on the *ex ante* maintenance of ecosystems, rather than simply agreeing to compensate *ex post* for the impacts of human activities.

For Hueting et al. (1992) and Richard (2012), the valuation of *natural capital* is not primarily related to a question of the *utility* of ecosystem services, but rather to the *functionality* of ecosystems.

The term *functionality* is a “safeguard”. It forces us not to separate the analysis of the utility of a service from that of the functions that allow it to exist, i.e. to base the economic analysis of ecosystems on the functional links between the elements and the biochemical cycles that drive them. The only rigorous way to consider this analysis is to consider that if ecosystems obviously do not have a *price*, in the sense of the market economy, their maintenance, i.e. the actions dedicated to their preservation or renewal, generates a *cost* for the actors who agree to devote themselves to them.

This maintenance cost must be accounted for in order to induce the *depreciation* of economic assets whose use impacts ecosystems (Godard 2010, p. 60; Richard 2012). In other words, if, unlike an ecosystem, an ecosystem service can have a price, the price of that service will reflect the sustainable anchorage of the activity that depends on it only if it includes the provision of funds necessary for the systematic maintenance of the ecosystem functions that underpin the existence of that service (Hueting 1980; Ekins 2000; Ekins and Simon 2000; Godard 2010).

In short, ecological economists refuse to subject the environment to neoclassical economic standards and reasoning. Instead, they propose to integrate the economy into the biosphere by respecting the cycles, and therefore the temporalities of the latter (Georgescu-Roegen 1971, 1979, 1995; Passet 1979, 1985). In this respect, the ecological economy converges with eco-development. However, it differs from it in its purpose – the economic sphere in its relations with the ecological sphere, not “development” – and in the fact that it does not always mention, expressly, a social purpose 21.

Practicing the ecological economy does not mean excluding the social sphere: the notions of ecosystem service and natural capital are social representations, and the institutionalization of ex-ante ecosystem management systems is a social action. On the contrary, the ecological economy implies making the social reality the place for the recognition of emergence phenomena whose qualification forms the field of economics in the living world.

The *emergence* phenomenon is one of the characteristics of a biological system, in the sense of Bertalanffy’s *General System Theory* (1968): at the global level of a set, properties not deductible from its elementary properties appear and these properties can be explained by a threshold effect.

What the economic actor considers to be an ecosystem service is therefore nothing more than the emerging property of an ecosystem. And this property is not deductible from the composition of the ecosystem in biochemical elements. Its existence is explained by the relationship between these elements within cycles and functions whose viability is only possible below certain levels of disruption. The critical thresholds mentioned above, those beyond which the entire ecosystem ceases to be viable, are the paroxysm.

The ecosystem service, which is none other than the economic utility removed from an ecosystem function, corresponds as we have seen to the utilitarian reading of an ecosystem emergence. However, in that it is an interpretation of ecosystem realities, this service also results from an emerging property of another system: the social system through which the functional character of ecosystem cycles is recognized, from which the considered utility emerges.

This property is not deductible from the elementary composition of the social system. It requires the anchorage of people in recognition communities that make up the cycles of social life.

More generally, this invites us to conceive of the economy as a utilitarian reading of socially determined and ecologically anchored realities.

## 1.2.1.2. Social economy and exosomatization

*Social systems* can be understood by many disciplines. Two of them are particularly useful to propose, in the social field, an analogy with the emergence phenomenon that gives rise to the ownership of ecosystem function.

*Community psychology* (Bronfenbrenner 1979; Saïas 2011) makes it possible to approach a social system in that it involves people in interaction and thus allows intersubjectivity and the formation of the knowledge necessary to recognize a level of reality.

*Social economy* promotes the recognition of the functional nature of intersubjectivity and knowledge formation to objectify other functions in other realities – notably ecosystem-based ones – but also in the social realities of work.

In community psychology, the notion of “social” refers “to material spaces (territories, institutional arrangements, settings) and symbolic spaces (relational fields, sociocultural contexts constituting the mental space of groups and individuals, therapeutic space) where a social dimension can be identified” (Saïas 2011). While the notion of “community” is part of a theoretical construct where “social identity is forged and a sense of belonging and positive or conflictual interactions are developed with members of one’s group and those of other groups” (Jodelet 2009).

Jovchelovitch (2007) provides a framework for objectifying social relations that combines a *memory load* with symbolic and material spaces. This memory load produces social relationships between individuals, but also within and between groups. It conditions the emergence of “scientific communities” (Latour 1987), i.e. networks of actors, which can be formalized as direct stakeholders of an entity, as well as the emergence of less formal “knowledge communities” (Dupouet et al. 2006), which create knowledge and disseminate it via a network, organized or virtual, *ultimately* generating a common identity that is consolidated by the production of shared social standards (Saïas 2011).

Community psychology is also marked by Lewin’s (1951) work, which describes *behavior* as a function of the interaction between the person and his/her environment, and by Bronfenbrenner’s (1979, 2001) work, whose so-called *ecological* approach leads us to consider the individual in his/her multiple environments.

Bronfenbrenner conceives different levels of understanding of the person’s interaction with his/her social environment: from the *ontosystem* (individual and his/her own characteristics); through the *microsystem* (community entity closest to the individual, i.e. family and close friends); the *mesosystem* (interaction between the different microsystems); and to the *macrosystem*, which encompasses other systems and constitutes a set of values, traditions and beliefs that forms the subject’s culture.
