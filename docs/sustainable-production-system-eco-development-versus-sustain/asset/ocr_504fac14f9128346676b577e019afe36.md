Anything that does not directly relate to the industrial value chain and the markets in which it is embedded is left out, considered as an externality, an external effect. This term of *external* effect confirms a dualism between industrial entities linked by markets, on the one hand, and the territories – physical and social – in which they operate, on the other hand.

From the point of view of an industrial entity in a neoliberal economy, a territory is not the “rest of the world” – an expression dedicated to qualifying other sectors of economic activity. From the point of view of the industrial and neoliberal economy, a territory is a “beyond” – or rather an unthought – of the economy; while it is necessary to bring back to the economy a mathematical image of the collateral effects of economic activities on certain phenomena that take place in this beyond, these phenomena are not intrinsically taken into account in the way the economy is viewed.

In other words, in a neoclassical approach, the market impacts society and ecosystems, and these influences can be calculated, possibly adjusted – which gives a certain acceptability to the market economy. But considering that society and ecosystems are the foundations of the market, or more so, that the market is none other than an emergence of economic and social phenomena, is not considered. The paradox is that these phenomena, which underlie the existence of the market – i.e. the place where the calculation leading to the formation of the market *price* takes place – can be qualified in their manifestations but cannot be calculated in their essence.

Analysts of *ecological economics* (Georgescu-Roegen 1971; Daly 1977; Passet 1979) and the *social economy* (Godin 1871; Laville 1997; Draperi 2005) reverse the neoclassical perspective. They demonstrate by way of two very different fields, but through a similar intellectual process, that internalization can guarantee neither the preservation of ecosystems nor that of social groups.

The approach that brings them together while distinguishing them from neoclassicals is simple: it is not a question of trying to correct impacts in a static and atomized vision of reality, but of effectively preserving capacities in a dynamic and systemic vision of the emergence phenomena that give rise to them. It is therefore necessary to renew the understanding of the living conditions and conservation modalities of the elements, relationships and functions that characterize the capacities described by two metaphors of capital: *natural capital* (Schumacher 1973) and *social capital* (Bourdieu 1980).
