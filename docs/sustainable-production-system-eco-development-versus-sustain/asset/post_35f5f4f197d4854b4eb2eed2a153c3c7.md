## Figure 6.2. Informative and cognitive balance sheet of a cooperative transaction situation guided by a triple heritage identity

The *triple heritage identity* introduced in the previous chapter thus constitutes a guide, a guideline – or “safeguard” – which makes it possible to support the cooperative transaction process toward the production of “communicating” contracts – referring to the fact that such contracts may help other actors in the territory to represent the performances described by the co-contractors.

This approach also makes it possible to territorialize the non-monetary indicators and meta-information associated with a PSS contract, while opening up to the territory their interpretation in terms of functional performance and possible service contracts in other valuation localities.

A contract is therefore no longer just a frontier object between use value and exchange value; it is a *media* that shows a performance. It is an integral part of the territorial IS, embodying and propagating its institutional renewal, facilitating both the recognition of the multifunctional productions with which it is associated and the solvency of the business models of the actors who carry these productions. The contract structures the territorial heritage.

This analysis of the influence of information and cognitive heritage on the joint achievement of collective territorial sustainability objectives and the
solvency objectives of each individual’s activities should be completed by a dynamic approach. The aim is to ensure that value analysis is part of the continuity of production processes, i.e. over time, which also implies going beyond the static version of the informative and cognitive balance sheet.

Our approach will consist of modeling the relationships between, on the one hand, the collective representations of the processes of maintenance of the sustainability of the territorial system (maintenance of social, ecological, economic, governance and propagation functions), and, on the other hand, the monetary costs of activities whose performance contributes to this maintenance.

### 6.1.1.1.1. Designing the processes of the functional ecosystem

This approach first involves collectively thinking about the conditions necessary for the processes of maintenance of the territorial system: five processes if we consider the social, ecological, economic, governance and propagation of the model, whose maintenance conditions will be described by five directories of performance indicators (one directory per process).

Key milestones are as follows:

- Determine the dependency relationships between descriptive indicators of the same process, for example, “How does the maintenance of one ecosystem function affect the status of another ecosystem function? AND are the selected indicators relevant to describe this influence?”
- Determine how the descriptive indicators of one process and those of another process can be used to capture the relationships between the processes under consideration, for example, “How is the maintenance of an ecosystem function influenced by the maintenance of an economic capacity? AND are the selected indicators relevant to describe this influence?”

The relevance of an indicator cannot be determined by reading the indicator alone; it is always a question of comparing the systems of representation – i.e. the couples [indicator – meta-information] – associated with each of the indicators.

### 6.1.1.1.2. Moving from collective to individual representation

The actors who together envisage the sustainability of the territorial system through the collective qualification of maintenance processes,
objectives and performance indicators must be able to extricate themselves from this context of representation.

These actors also represent, formally or informally, how this maintenance will affect their own activities. This projection, formalized by indicators specific to their profession and linked to the local anchorage of their activity, would lead them to consider indicators and meta-information that are distinct, for the most part, from those that describe the maintenance processes of the territorial system.

In order to establish a relationship between these two sets of indicators and meta-information, we will consider that the contributions of an actor to the processes of maintenance of the territorial system can be qualified by specific indicators, which describe how the actor’s own activity influences the realization of these processes.

At this stage, a framework for analyzing the informative and cognitive balance of a cooperation situation in order to maintain the sustainability of the territorial functional ecosystem would be developed (see Figure 6.3).

### 6.1.1.1.3. Entering the analysis of the representations in a time frame

The informative and cognitive assessment gives access to an instant picture – fixed at the time of the closing of the accounting period – of the relationships between collective representation capacities and individual representation capacities. A long-term perspective is necessary to reflect the fact that each of the actors is part of a cooperative transaction process (see section 3.1.2.2.1) that animates the governance group of the territorial functional ecosystem.

In other words, individual and collective representations evolve together when defining the maintenance processes of this functional ecosystem. And the set of actors organized and structured to ensure this maintenance must therefore be distinguished from another set, made up of the same actors, but organized and structured to incorporate this maintenance into the development of each other’s professions.

### 6.1.1.1.4. Determining collective costs from the costs of contributing to the collective

The distinction between the specific organizations and structures of the same set of actors makes it possible to compare the costs of each actor’s
activities with the operating costs of the processes of maintaining the sustainability of the territory. It is indeed a single and same dynamic: the collective processes of maintenance, and the activities specific to each of the actors, can only be distinguished because of a choice of perspective – micro- or mesoeconomic¹.

The integration of microeconomic costs provides an interpretation of the overall level of costs of territorial processes whose evolution, analyzed jointly with that of the territorial informative and cognitive balance sheet, must, in our opinion, feed a renewal of the relationship between political economy and local innovation, as well as a re-foundation of the evaluation of wealth produced by the actors of a territory.

## 6.1.1.2. Modeling joint productions

A dynamic system can be modeled using a state vector and a *transition function*, and must therefore establish the *boundary between* an “interior” and an “exterior” to the system (Daucé 2002).

The definition of the state vector, although complex, represents *a priori* the least problematic part of DMCS approach. It is a question of jointly describing, at a given moment, the state of the informative and cognitive heritage available to the system in question, the performance of this system, and the costs of the processes that drive it. As for the problem of the border between the interior and exterior of the system, we will approach it in a rather trivial way, considering as “exterior” the entities disconnected from the territorial IS that are the subject of the analysis.

The DMCS will be approached in a neo-Ricardian approach, described as *joint productions* (Sraffa 1960; Pasinetti 1975).

A *joint production* model makes it possible to mathematically transcribe how the co-evolution of a process system (the processes of maintenance of the territorial functional ecosystem) mobilizes and renews a set of resources (the representations of the actors who define and animate these processes).
