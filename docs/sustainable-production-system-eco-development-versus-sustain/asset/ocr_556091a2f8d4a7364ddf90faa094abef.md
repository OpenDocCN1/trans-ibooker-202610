# 1.2.1.1. Ecological economy and emergence

Two approaches to *natural capital* can be considered. They focus on issues related to the possibility of substituting this natural capital with economic capital. This neoclassical idea, defended in particular by Hartwick (1977) in the principle of compensation mentioned above, gave rise to the current of *environmental economics* (Stiglitz 1974; Dasgupta and Heal 1979). It is firmly rejected by the supporters of *ecological economics* movement, who thus distinguish themselves from neoclassical thinking, of which the environmental economy is a variation.

The distinction, or rather the split, between these two currents that compete for the same object of analysis – natural capital – is often addressed in terms of *low* and *high sustainability*. Examining the concept of *ecosystem services* (Costanza et al. 1997; Daily 1997) provides a better understanding of what separates them.

The UN *Millennium Ecosystem Assessment* (2005) distinguishes between several categories of ecosystem services: cultural services¹⁷, supply services¹⁸, regulatory services¹⁹ and support services²⁰.

The neoclassical *environmental economy* integrates these different services into the market economy through methods specific to each category, for example, the willingness to pay for access to cultural services, or the cost of raw materials for supply services. Regulatory and support services, outside the scope of direct utility for the neoliberal industrial economy, are only considered in a related and partial way through possible internalization and compensation mechanisms.
