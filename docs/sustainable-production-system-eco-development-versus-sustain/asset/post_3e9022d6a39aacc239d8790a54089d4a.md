This situation is not inevitable. Other relationships between economics and the practice of information and currency are possible. Some may be frightened by our Marxian approach of *incorporating a collective work of representation* when analyzing the value of productive phenomena, or by an institutionalist vision in which *money is a social relationship*. Let us invite them to carry out a similar process with more *mainstream*, more “orthodox” tools of inspiration.

Organizational management commonly refers to congruence, i.e. the *strategic alignment* of ISs (Liavari 1992; Henderson and Venkatraman 1993; Bergeron et al. 2001, 2004). And the notion of *co-alignment* of IS governance and activity governance (Bergeron et al. 2004) would thus influence a *co-variation* between performance, business models and IS (Venkatraman 1989; Bergeron et al. 2001). For Henderson and Venkatraman (1999), a link should be established between the number, power and types of technologies; systemic skills to support the entity’s strategy; and governance of availability and IS capabilities (Bounfour and Epinette 2006).

Our DMCS approach indicates how to measure a co-variation between the collective performance of the processes of maintaining the sustainability of the territory, the business models that contribute to this maintenance and the ISs. To do this, it is not the number, power and type of technologies that we have referred to when we compare them to governance and IS capabilities. It is to the diversity, the importance and the consistency of the representations mobilized by the actors – both in the exercise of the profession specific to each one and in their relationship to the common horizon – that drives the governance of the territory: the search for sustainability.

The evaluation of the representation capacities offered by the territorial IS is carried out by establishing the *informative and cognitive balance sheet* following the measurement of the *triple heritage identity*, which is a “signature” of the local anchorage of this IS. This approach, which very directly feeds strategic alignment, is also a prerequisite for determining the level of *micro–meso–macro co-amortization* of assets in the *negentropic decoupling balance sheet*.

Based on an approach proposed by Parker and Benson (1988), Bounfour and Epinette (2006, p. 27) suggest assessing the (financial) *value* of a new IS.

*Value of a new information system =*

*Traditional advantages – traditional costs + link value + acceleration value + restructuring value + innovation value.*

Their method consists of extending a *discounted cost–benefit* analysis to “*value linking* or acceleration values, resulting for example from benefits arising from the combination of resources between activities or functions; restructuring values, associated for example with changes in organizational modes in a department, department or function; innovation values, resulting from changes in procedures and methods of conducting activities, following the introduction of the new system” (Bounfour and Epinette 2006, p. 27).

What is this *value linking*? And to what would we associate these values of connection, restructuring, innovation and acceleration, if we were to base the expression on the DMCS approach? It is certainly in the *territorial proximities* of Colletis *et al.* (2005).

In our opinion, the *link value* expresses the measurement of a state of *institutional proximity* conferred by a new IS – whether it is an intra- or interorganizational territory. It is considered in the *connectivity* dimension – one of the dimensions of the analytical space within which the *adaptive cycle* that we have mobilized to describe a *cooperative transaction* evolves.

The *restructuring value* would express the measure of the renewal of the relationship to *organizational proximity* that results, within this new IS, from the adaptation of indicator and meta-information repositories following a cooperative transaction. It is to be expressed in the *resilience* dimension of the adaptive cycle space.

The *value of innovation* is considered in the third of the dimensions of the evolutionary space that we have associated with the adaptive cycle – the analytical dimension of the relationship between the *materiality* of information. This value is related to an IS performance in terms of transformation of the capacity to interpret the territorial anchorage of *homogeneous production units* with regard to contributions, non-market uses and contractualized services in which the entities they represent are involved.

It is here that the relationship of *spatial proximities* to the space of recognition of values is recast.

We then quite naturally consider the *acceleration value* as relative to the concretization of the *territory* in the sense of Colletis et al. (2005). Spatial, organizational and institutional proximity are only one potential of this territory which, it should be recalled, must “be revealed in a situation of coordination, ranging from the resolution of a problem encountered by an actor located in time and space to a territorial development project bringing together the time horizons of actors involved in this triple proximity” (*ibid.*).

*Institutional proximity* between actors is both the outcome and the condition for *political deliberations* that drive the release of the informative and cognitive “resources” necessary for the *concerted specialization* of actors. These deliberations are therefore functionally linked to the *cooperative transactions* that accompany the co-implementation of *PSS-type* offers, and guide the *industrial structuring* of the territory – thus renewing both the relationship to *organizational proximity* and *spatial proximity*. They make it possible to establish the relationship between the geography of needs and activity products, i.e. on the one hand, the *mapping of the physical anchoring of the matter-energy flow*, and, on the other hand, the *territorial inscription of controversies maps* relating to the impact and control of this flow.

This *situation of territorial coordination* between political and economic activities, of the re-embedding of the economy in the social and political spheres – therefore of *political economy* in the sense of Polanyi – may or may not be covered by a formal contract. In all cases, it leads to the description of new actors, objects and productive phenomena identified as strategic for sustainable development. The changes in the procedures and methods of conducting activities, to which Bounfour and Epinette associate an innovative value of the new IS, are, in our opinion, more broadly part of an *institutionalization value*. They reflect the ability to practice a new integration of the micro-, meso- and macroaccounting and extra-financial systems, i.e. the territorial capacity for economic *Communication*.

## Conclusion

Our simple, basic problem is that the current assessment of *decoupling* between GDP growth and ecosystem and social group degradation is based on a structural, institutionalized dissipation of economic information and on the political-media inhibition of social and ecological information.

The *dynamic modeling of cost systems* (DMCS) approach for sustainable territories management proposed in our doctoral thesis (Morlat 2016) and refined in this book aims to measure the linkage, restructuring and innovation values conferred by the *use* of a new territorial information system. These measures promote the assessment of the strength of cohesion between the ecological, sociotechnical and socioeconomic components of the territorial system, i.e. the level of *negentropy* associated with a territorial situation, with the *state* of a territory.

However, these static measures – relating to a state of the territory – are not sufficient. Let us not be under any illusions. Socioeconomic evaluations are constantly being questioned – not biased by vocation as neoclassical evaluations are, but fluctuating by nature, tinged with circumstances, and obviously not free from interpretation bias. This is why we have insisted so much on the fact that designing and implementing the collective solution of a problem – in this case the collective activity of maintaining the sustainability of the territory – is an activity *located* in time and territory.
