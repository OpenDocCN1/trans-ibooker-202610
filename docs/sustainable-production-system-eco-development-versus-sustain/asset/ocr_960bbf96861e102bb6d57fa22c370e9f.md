Continuing the comparison of applied ethics and the market, in order to analyze the interfaces between the realities addressed by these two methods, makes it possible to identify dangerous practices that are conducive to exceeding critical thresholds within the politico-economic system. And qualifying such thresholds implies addressing the constraints between the subsystems that the political economy is trying to reconcile by reintegrating the economic into the democratic system.

For de Courcelles (2009), the difficulty of ethics consists of articulating the levels of the singular, the particular and the universal – with their possible contradictions – to define an evaluation base that is both readable and respectful of identities and responsibilities.

Sustainable development, in the sense of a strong sustainability apprehended at the universal level, is then a horizon toward which we must strive at the level of the individual through different communities of belonging – with contrasting codes, customs and beliefs – within which individuals with their own subjectivities and singular interests interact.

De Courcelles adds that it is important to distinguish between ethics and morality. Ethics is a questioning in and for action, which mobilizes an evolving system of “values” because it depends on the set of actors involved in the decision. On the other hand, morality refers to an action already regulated by norms derived from a fixed and uncircumstantiated value system.

Moreover, the “golden rule” of ethics (“do not do to others what you would not want them to do to you”) does not imply acting only out of altruism or in the general interest, but also corresponds to the correctness and justice of behavior; sustainable development therefore does not imply renouncing the notion of wealth, but possibly redefining this notion (ibid.).
