
Theories of *co-evolution* (Norgaard 1984, 1988, 1992; Hodgson 2010) address the comparative dynamics of the relationships between the economy, society and the environment. More generally, a set of systems is in co-evolution when a change in part of one of the systems causes multiple changes in the feedback between these systems (Faucheux and Noël 1995).

Linked to these theories, the concept of *resilience* (Holling 1973) is useful in identifying critical thresholds and functions within ecological and socioeconomic systems. This concept can be considered as “the ability of a system to absorb shocks, sources of dysfunction, and to reorganize itself while changing so as to maintain essentially the same function, structure, identity and feedback” (Walker et al. 2004).

It is often associated with the *adaptive cycle* model (Holling 1995), as well as with the *panarchy* model (Holling et al. 2002), which proposes a spatial and temporal interweaving of descriptive adaptive cycles of socioecological systems. In a panarchical representation, it is the interaction between phenomena occurring at different scales that should be the focus of attention (Levin 1992).

This model makes it possible to partially illustrate the relationships between the levels of the universal, the particular and the singular. It could also contribute to addressing the phenomena of synchronizing transindividuation and diachronizing co-individuation considered by Stiegler (2006), and thus to addressing the relationships between wealth and values.

Negotiations around the value of productive phenomena are carried out at an intermediate level between that of the individual who makes judgments about the composition of this value in the short term and that of the society that transforms the representation of wealth in the long term.

In a market economy in the neoclassical sense24, i.e. one that removes all rationality other than that attached to the quest for financial profit per market transaction – as is the case, for example, in the GAFAM economy25 – wealth and exchange value merge. This economy thus institutes at the universal level the value of exchange as the only standard of wealth, i.e. of the good.

If nothing else imposes itself on it, the pre-eminence of this representation of wealth captures transindividuation. As a result, negotiations at the individual level, aimed at qualifying the value of productive phenomena, may tend to be conducted only to structure the exchange value that can be associated with the useful emergence of ecological and socioeconomic functions.

People’s social value systems are only mobilized to induce them to express their consent to give or receive an exchange value during transactions that may concern any phenomenon of reality.

A market economy in the strictly neoclassical sense would therefore not only make the dimensions of representation of reality (particularly social, ecological and economic) fungible. It would erase the differences between collective spaces of perception of the qualities socially attributed to this reality (levels of the universal, the particular and the singular), which would lead to homogenizing any social group of actors into a set of agents of the market economy.

In a neoliberal industrial economy, the situation is different. This economy also has as its horizon the universality of the market and the exchange value as a standard of wealth, but there remains at the level of the individual an autonomous space of representation, that of production, where the use value intervenes.

Value judgments then put local tension on exchange and use in a social group that is no longer necessarily limited to market agents, but includes actors in the industrial economy who extend the scope of objectification of utility to the act of physical production. Individuals bring together at the level of the singular and the particular *knowledge* whose usefulness for development implies reconsidering the foundations of the “labor factor”.

An interface between two economic worlds then remains materialized by the relationship to the physical dimension of the productive apparatus. The particularities of this apparatus impose a distance from exchange by mixing in negotiations around the value concerns related to socioecological and economic non-market costs specific to the mode of production.

This interface structures spaces of representation that allow the existence of diversified socioeconomic communities, thus likely to open the field of a political economy, by crossing the views on the value of productive phenomena.

Let us then assume here that the diversity of communities led to consider the value of productions results in a diversity of views on the phenomenon of work, and that it is not homogeneous but composite – it is a *plural work*, of which we distinguish five components here:
- bio-physical-chemical phenomena related to the production and reproduction functions of living organisms are the result of *ecosystem-based work*;
- community and political phenomena related to the production and reproduction functions of life in society are the result of *social work*;
- psychosociological phenomena linked to the production and reproduction functions of the capacities for the recognition of socioecological phenomena are the result of *economic work*;
- physical and numerical phenomena related to the transformation functions of energy and matter are the result of *industrial work*;
- capitalization phenomena linked to the functions of market transactions and monetarization are the result of *financial work*.

The representation of value therefore derives from a negotiated interpretation of the ways in which these different productive phenomena contribute to wealth – understood in the sense of a sustainable co-evolution between ecological and social systems.

## 1.3. Conclusion

In a sustainable development political economy, ecosystem work is to be considered as necessary for *primary production*. It makes possible the existence of the social system as an area of intersubjective representation, in which work is to be considered in what is necessary for a *secondary production* that is the production of the social system as a trans-subjective reality: groups able to communicate and in particular to formulate needs.

The economic system, as a reality within the social sphere, would then correspond to the more or less rapid production and spread of new ways of valuing the responses to these needs. And the associated field of representation approaches economic work, in its essence, as a work of recognition of social and ecological productions, within the social sphere and in a perspective of responding to social need.

This economic recognition work is necessary for *tertiary production*: that of the modalities of responding to social need and their qualification in terms of use value. It leads to a new field of representation of work: that of industry – physical and digital – the place of a *quaternary production* that is a response to social need by combining ecosystemic and economic work.

The field of physical and digital industry leads to the field of finance in which the work to be considered is that necessary for a *quinternary production*: that of representing the exchange value of the combined products of the five components of work.
