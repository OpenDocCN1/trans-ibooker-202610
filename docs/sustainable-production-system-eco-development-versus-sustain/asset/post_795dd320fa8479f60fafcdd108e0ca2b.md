Continuing the comparison of applied ethics and the market, in order to analyze the interfaces between the realities addressed by these two methods, makes it possible to identify dangerous practices that are conducive to exceeding critical thresholds within the politico-economic system. And qualifying such thresholds implies addressing the constraints between the subsystems that the political economy is trying to reconcile by reintegrating the economic into the democratic system.

For de Courcelles (2009), the difficulty of ethics consists of articulating the levels of the singular, the particular and the universal – with their possible contradictions – to define an evaluation base that is both readable and respectful of identities and responsibilities.

Sustainable development, in the sense of a strong sustainability apprehended at the universal level, is then a horizon toward which we must strive at the level of the individual through different communities of belonging – with contrasting codes, customs and beliefs – within which individuals with their own subjectivities and singular interests interact.

De Courcelles adds that it is important to distinguish between ethics and morality. Ethics is a questioning in and for action, which mobilizes an evolving system of “values” because it depends on the set of actors involved in the decision. On the other hand, morality refers to an action already regulated by norms derived from a fixed and uncircumstantiated value system.

Moreover, the “golden rule” of ethics (“do not do to others what you would not want them to do to you”) does not imply acting only out of altruism or in the general interest, but also corresponds to the correctness and justice of behavior; sustainable development therefore does not imply renouncing the notion of wealth, but possibly redefining this notion (ibid.).

As well as redefining it, it is the recharacterization of the place of the notion of wealth that should be taken as a risk. Producing the analytical framework that will make it possible to approach the political economy by comparing ethical and market practices involves clarifying the relationships between “wealth”, economic “value” and social “values”.

*Wealth* can be considered as a whole corresponding to what the good is at the *universal level* of a given social group. In a sustainable political economy of development, we believe that the notion of wealth must correspond to a situation of sustainable co-evolution between ecological and social systems – the latter being considered in the broad sense, i.e. by including economic communities. And the collective formulation of what this wealth is induces an intersubjective framework for representing what is important and what is crucial, i.e. a capacity whose improvement can be associated with *progress*. The latter term here is understood in the sense of a progression toward sustainable freedom from constraints linked to a set of critical thresholds associated with ecological and socioeconomic systems.

*Value* is a social representation, a composite artifact decision support for action that is structured at the *level of the particular*, i.e. a subset of the social group that has represented wealth at the level of the universal system. Value is an intersubjective consensus resulting from (value) judgments about how an activity or phenomenon promotes access to wealth, i.e. contributes to the joint removal of critical thresholds associated with ecological and socioeconomic systems.

Value is thus the result of a non-aggregate and negotiated integration of different components (of use, non-use and exchange), which takes place when a phenomenon collectively perceived as productive is qualified as it allows access paths to wealth. A productive phenomenon therefore does not produce value, and it has value in that it is considered productive within a group, which leads to assuming and claiming the character of social, spatial and temporal relativity of economic value.

*Plural social value* systems, rooted in people’s cognition, i.e. at the *level of the individual* of the social group that represented wealth, are mobilized when making the judgments that come into play during negotiations on the value of productive phenomena. These systems can be reorganized by these negotiations, whose processes and terms influence – and are influenced by – collective representations of wealth at the universal level.

## 1.2.2.2. Political economy and plural work

The critical thresholds whose attainment would result in the loss of the power of value recognition enjoyed by the political economy are fundamentally related to the weakening of the social functions that allow interactions between community spaces of representation.

The difficulty in articulating singular, particular and universal, mentioned by de Courcelles (2009), is no stranger to this problem.

The representation of wealth at the universal level is directly influenced by the influence exerted from the singular level by the mobilization of plural social value systems during the development and renewal of the political process. At the level of the individual, the interactions generated by the political process resonate with the dynamics of these social systems, leading to a phenomenon of creative differentiation of individuality and shared knowledge, which influences the redefinition of wealth at the universal level.

Stiegler (2006) describes as *individuation* the phenomenon corresponding to the “psychological and social biological formation of the individual who is still incomplete”²³. Stiegler calls for a policy based on a re-articulation between psychic and collective individuations and a technique – *technique* referring here to a concrete and effective environment linking the “I” and the “we”. In Stiegler’s study, the *transindividual* is what produces the social and through which the diachronizing co-individual of the “I” generates the synchronizing transindividual of a “we”³.

Interactions between individuals generate singularity, rhythms specific to each one, but also a group in which they constitute and nourish their individualities together through a process that can be approached intrinsically and according to a particular temporality. As a social institution, as well as a technique producing its environment within this institution, the economy – political, industrial and market – is thus the combination of a synchronizing transindividuation and a diachronizing co-individuation.

The inclusion of a social group in a synchronism of the relationship to wealth at the universal level of this group is inseparable from the diachronic negotiations around value, conducted by different subgroups considering various productive phenomena at different time and territory scales.

The synchronism of the relationship to wealth, as it is addressed in the democratic process for example, is directly affected by the diachronic influences of negotiations around the value of productive phenomena. During the democratic process, people collectively identify themselves and co-evolve the plural social value systems that will be mobilized during negotiations around the value of productive phenomena, negotiations that, in turn, influence the political process.

Political economy is an economy of the re-embedding of the economy in the social and democratic relationship – that is, in a relationship with the constraints of reality. It must take into account multiple levels (singular, particular and universal) and different temporal representations of wealth and value. This is all the more necessary and delicate when the reality in question is recognized as the result of systemic phenomena, functions and emergences that must be addressed both independently and in their interactions.
