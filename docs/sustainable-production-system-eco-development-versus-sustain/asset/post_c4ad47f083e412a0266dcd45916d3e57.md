For Sachs (1981, p. 139), “Development is a whole. The cultural, social, economic, institutional, political and ecological dimensions must be addressed in their interrelationships through an integrated policy”. According to him, it is a question of thinking “of a set of normative criteria and questions rather than a rigid model” (Sachs 1990, p. 3), and of solutions “involving simultaneous respect for social, cultural, ecological, environmental, territorial, economic, political and international indicators” (Sachs 1997, pp. 84–85).

This requirement for a multi-indicator analytical perspective, which is at the heart of Sachs’ conception of the representation of development, is a defense against a neoliberal capture of sustainable development.

Such a capture would lead to a predominance of integration by the market, to solutions through factorization, thus to the fusion of the realities described by the indicators, and to an equation compatible with the logic of global productivity.

On the other hand, simultaneously meeting the requirements described by several indicators implies much more than having the expertise applicable to a level of reality. This requires knowledge adapted to the simultaneous mobilization of several methods and tools dedicated to the recognition of the specificities of phenomena registered in different realities, as well as to the way in which the information resulting from this recognition is put under tension.

Such an undertaking does not therefore imply rejecting any neoclassical practice. It consists of anchoring any neoclassical practice, for example an analysis of decoupling, in a broader socioeconomic approach that addresses the same subject but allows it to be addressed within a robust overall framework. In other words, it is a path of recognition aimed at placing neoclassical approximations – caused, for example, by the use of comparative growth rates – in a state of structured uncertainty.

In economic evaluation practices, this type of approach is often associated with multi-criteria decision-support methods (Roy 1985; Roy 1991; Roy and Vincke 1981).

The recognition path proposed by these methods consists of a nonlinear and recursive path consisting of four steps: (i) structuring of the decision problem by associating decision criteria with issues; (ii) articulating and modelling preferences; (iii) aggregating the alternative evaluations (preferences); and (iv) making recommendations (Guitouni and Martel 1998).

A multi-criteria analysis can be used to support a mathematical calculation associated with the valuation of ecosystem services to the economy, or the determination of a political price aimed, for example, at introducing a tax on polluting activities. But more fundamentally, it is intended to provide representations to inform the decision. Monetary representation, when it intervenes, is thus considered as a tool for the application of the decision – which remains the result of social representations.

## 1.2. Systemic approach to the economy

The neoclassical economy is an economy that aspires to induce intelligible reality. The doctrine of global factor productivity, which has become a paradigm, leads analysts who comply with it to consider as productive only those phenomena that can be described by mathematical equations – nowadays implemented by numerical algorithms.

Anything that does not directly relate to the industrial value chain and the markets in which it is embedded is left out, considered as an externality, an external effect. This term of *external* effect confirms a dualism between industrial entities linked by markets, on the one hand, and the territories – physical and social – in which they operate, on the other hand.

From the point of view of an industrial entity in a neoliberal economy, a territory is not the “rest of the world” – an expression dedicated to qualifying other sectors of economic activity. From the point of view of the industrial and neoliberal economy, a territory is a “beyond” – or rather an unthought – of the economy; while it is necessary to bring back to the economy a mathematical image of the collateral effects of economic activities on certain phenomena that take place in this beyond, these phenomena are not intrinsically taken into account in the way the economy is viewed.

In other words, in a neoclassical approach, the market impacts society and ecosystems, and these influences can be calculated, possibly adjusted – which gives a certain acceptability to the market economy. But considering that society and ecosystems are the foundations of the market, or more so, that the market is none other than an emergence of economic and social phenomena, is not considered. The paradox is that these phenomena, which underlie the existence of the market – i.e. the place where the calculation leading to the formation of the market *price* takes place – can be qualified in their manifestations but cannot be calculated in their essence.

Analysts of *ecological economics* (Georgescu-Roegen 1971; Daly 1977; Passet 1979) and the *social economy* (Godin 1871; Laville 1997; Draperi 2005) reverse the neoclassical perspective. They demonstrate by way of two very different fields, but through a similar intellectual process, that internalization can guarantee neither the preservation of ecosystems nor that of social groups.

The approach that brings them together while distinguishing them from neoclassicals is simple: it is not a question of trying to correct impacts in a static and atomized vision of reality, but of effectively preserving capacities in a dynamic and systemic vision of the emergence phenomena that give rise to them. It is therefore necessary to renew the understanding of the living conditions and conservation modalities of the elements, relationships and functions that characterize the capacities described by two metaphors of capital: *natural capital* (Schumacher 1973) and *social capital* (Bourdieu 1980).

### 1.2.1. Nature and structure of productive capital

#### 1.2.1.1. Ecological economy and emergence

Two approaches to *natural capital* can be considered. They focus on issues related to the possibility of substituting this natural capital with economic capital. This neoclassical idea, defended in particular by Hartwick (1977) in the principle of compensation mentioned above, gave rise to the current of *environmental economics* (Stiglitz 1974; Dasgupta and Heal 1979). It is firmly rejected by the supporters of *ecological economics* movement, who thus distinguish themselves from neoclassical thinking, of which the environmental economy is a variation.

The distinction, or rather the split, between these two currents that compete for the same object of analysis – natural capital – is often addressed in terms of *low* and *high sustainability*. Examining the concept of *ecosystem services* (Costanza et al. 1997; Daily 1997) provides a better understanding of what separates them.

The UN *Millennium Ecosystem Assessment* (2005) distinguishes between several categories of ecosystem services: cultural services¹⁷, supply services¹⁸, regulatory services¹⁹ and support services²⁰.

The neoclassical *environmental economy* integrates these different services into the market economy through methods specific to each category, for example, the willingness to pay for access to cultural services, or the cost of raw materials for supply services. Regulatory and support services, outside the scope of direct utility for the neoliberal industrial economy, are only considered in a related and partial way through possible internalization and compensation mechanisms.
