The comparison of information relating to different levels of reality is intrinsically an approach that handles uncertainty. It implies that disciplines communicate, that their representatives are aware that they are not mobilizing the same objects and that they are concerned about building new, interdisciplinary objects, made of approximations whose use will be limited to a particular approach. This approach requires attention to the conditions of production of interdisciplinary objects. It is based on the collective recognition of an act of interpretation, and on the contextualization of this interpretation, prior to any mathematical representation.

On the other hand, when a discipline shapes an interdisciplinary object ex nihilo, assigns it a permanent scientific vocation, and imposes through this object a level of representation that silences disciplines related to other realities, there is a distortion of reality.

For Nicolescu (2011), “two levels of Reality are different if, by passing from one to the other, there is a break in laws and a break in fundamental concepts (such as, for example, causality). No one has succeeded in finding a mathematical formalism that allows for a rigorous transition from one world to another”.

The simplification of representations of reality that underlies neoclassical evaluation is not, however, problematic in itself. The problems begin where simplification for operability gives way to reductionism, where the consequences on evaluation mark the threshold of acceptability that separates the chosen approximation from the distortion suffered.

The use of a mathematical formalism is therefore obviously not to be prohibited in economics when one wishes to put into tension descriptive information from several levels of reality. However, it is absolutely crucial to ensure that the approximation inherent in this calculation does not impose on political actors a distortion of representations of reality. The “polluter pays” principle, for example, is only useful in terms of producer responsibility and public awareness if the payment of the amount due by the polluter does not
lead to the illusion of effective compensation for environmental damage¹⁶, or to neglecting the social effects caused by such damage.

## 1.1.2.2. Neglected socioeconomic direction

The omnipresent uncertainty and the necessary approximation must not lead to setting aside the ambition of absolute decoupling in order to be satisfied with the search for decoupling that would only relate to the economic value associated with the production of human activities. This would be subversion. The success of a decoupling strategy is not so much the number of sources of uncertainty and modes of approximation, as the effect of the combination of approximations and uncertainties. The issue is therefore, on the one hand, to know where the uncertainties lie and, on the other hand, to determine how approximations affect representational capacities and perspectives for action.

The neoclassical proposal to reinvest savings in a “repair” of natural capital – assuming that such a repair is possible, we will come back to this point – would imply a trans-subjective knowledge of ecological and economic realities, but also of the social realities that influence the representation of the ecological and economic ones.

Such knowledge is inaccessible to the human mind, even collective. On the other hand, intersubjective recognition of the existence of the dynamics that drive the ecological, economic and social spheres is possible, and this is the path that must be followed in the construction of a decoupling strategy.

Decoupling, as a collective action and as a necessary evaluation for this action, is part of a path of recognition, through several realities. This journey begins with the recognition of the social realities by which the recognition of the economic and ecological realities will be carried out. It continues with the representation of the interrelationships between economic and ecological realities, before returning to the social one – both as a social body in interrelation with the economic and ecological realities, and as a place of interpretation and decision.

Even neoclassical analysts follow a – limited – path of knowledge when they read reality through the equations of their models. The impetus for a market offset strategy comes from a social body – this is the case, for example, with the social action of institutionalizing a CO₂ market – while the recognition of the effect of this strategy is also achieved, as a recognition action, in a social reality.

The particularity of the social action of recognition carried out by neoclassical analysts is based on two things. First of all, on the fact that this action mobilizes mathematical equations to recognize, which does not make it anything other than a social action of recognition. This central point will be developed throughout this book. It leads to the question of the respective places taken in the analysis by the calculation and by the description of what counts, and then this is part of the answer; this particularity lies in the fact that this action only recognizes what the equations allow us to approach from reality. This situation results in a strong dependence on the “analytical path”, i.e. the transposition of the same equation – from the same reading grid – from one situation to another, possibly adapting the variables to be considered to the circumstances of the recognition.

However, neoclassical economists should not be subjected to unfounded accusations. Biases driven by an analytical routine can also manifest themselves in socioeconomic approaches – i.e. based on social representations and possibly leading, but only later, to the use of calculation tools. The practical culture mobilized by socioeconomists, however, makes these biases exceptions and mistakes, while the biases introduced by neoclassical economists derive directly from the operational – mathematical – declination of what they consider to be principles and laws.

If economics is a science, it is a science of recognition through action before it is a science of knowledge for action. The wandering of the neoclassical economy is due to this confusion of genres between knowledge and recognition, and therefore between reality and model. To confess this inability of the neoclassical economy to think through action would introduce the need to recognize not only an omnipresent uncertainty, but an irreducible relativity. This relativity is unacceptable to neoclassical economists. It is incompatible with the idea of “hard science”, because it leads to breaking the normativity imposed “all other things being equal” and to always seeing relations with the “rest of the world” as part of the evaluation.

In other words, the action of formalizing a hypothesis, like the production of a model, is the result of work on shared representations, i.e. recognized by actors as compatible with each other and with a given reality. Modeling therefore implies both recognizing several realities, and recognizing oneself as a producer of interdisciplinary concepts, methods and tools designed to subjectively describe the relationships between these realities.

Mobilizing representations that exist in one of the economic realities (example of the market) as the main support for action at other levels of reality (example of the internalization of the economy’s impacts on the environment), without knowledge of these realities, is therefore not rigorous. In addition, beyond scientific rigor, there is the question of the meaning given to economic representation, and in particular its influence on political action, i.e. the question of the ideological impact of economic practices. These shape social representations.

It is most likely this dimension that most amputates the contribution of the neoclassical economy to the emergence of a sustainable mode of development. Development can just as easily be understood as collective postindustrial revolution progress, as the hope for material and social progress generated, and as the global expansion of a capitalist society (Comeliau 1999; Gendron and Reveret 2000).

The political object of sustainable development, if captured by neoclassical practice, can thus refer to the idea of perpetuation – of sustainable maintenance – of the use of economic representations of the ecological and social realities.

As a result, the concept of sustainable development remains highly criticized. Diemer (2013) reports very strong positions, particularly from Latouche, who evokes an “alibi concept”, “hell, paved with good intentions” (1994, p. 80), or an “oxymoron” (2003); Georgescu-Roegen (1995) who evokes a “sweet lullaby”; Godard (2005) who associates sustainable development with a “chimera or mystification”; and Boisvert et al. (2009) who evokes an “avatar” of globalization.

However, this concept of sustainable development is well established. It is not a question of throwing it into oblivion but of clarifying and completing it, and for that reason understanding why it remains hindered by a certain number of paradoxes, in order to act on its propagation.
