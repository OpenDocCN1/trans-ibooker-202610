## 9.6 Phenotypic Ontology of Humans in Rare Disorders

Ontologies have contributed considerably to translational studies and the rare disorders’ genetic diagnosis. Genome sequencing has facilitated finding several new disorder-related genes [19]. In many subjects, the diagnosis might alter medical management and household counseling [20]. Variant-derived analysis can distinguish a disorder-triggering variant across several variants in a typical genome or exome. Recognizing the entire medical asset for such data necessitates extra information regarding phenotype anomalies and disorders. Ontologies offer a computerized-analyzing model, which reframes the molecular data when assessing phenotype presentation. The Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) facilitates intense phenotyping whereby computerized phenotype patterns of human disorders connect closely related terminologies in the hierarchy and adjusted against unique phenotype anomalies [21]. There are many terminologies in a subtype hierarchy that encrypts unique terms for differential diagnosis in accordance with the repetition of the term over each disorder in the HPO repository.

The HPO enables a significantly greater comprehensive representation of medical phenotypes [22]. It is configured for computerized examination through connecting to ontologies of biological variables such as gene function, anatomy, biochemistry, and others and computerized disorders’ descriptions [21]. For instance, neutropenia is naturally described via three ontologies’ terminologies including neutrophil (cell types), blood (anatomy), and reduced count (variable). ## 9.7 Annotations and Ontology Integration

HPO is a basic comprehensive domain prototype has been for rare disorders so far, and we have previously provided a large corpus of HPO disease record profiles to OMIM, Orphanet and DECIPHER [24]. With the latest advances in personal medicine, it is becoming increasingly important to create a basis for phenotype-driven genome analysis and other translational research in other medical fields. As a result, we have extended our work to common phenotypes of human disease using a text-breaking strategy [25] to analyze PubMed Corpus 2014, which has enabled us to conclude 132,620 comments on 3145 common diseases [25]. These notes were validated against a manually cured subset of perturbations and the results of experiments showed a total accuracy of 67%. We showed biologically beneficial phenotypic similarity between specific diseases that share one or more associated genetic variants (“Comprehensive Association Study [GWAS] hit”), as well as phenotypic overlaps between a specific and unusual disease linked to the same genes [25]. Some tools have already been incorporated for genotype-phenotype data in the field of complex diseases and via systematic research studies (GWAS), such as GWAS Central [26] and GWASdb [27], which are likely to be adopted throughout the future for phenomenon-wide association studies with electronic health records.

The efficiency of computational search algorithms within and across species may be enhanced if a detailed list of phenotypic functions is recorded. It is fair to say that the individual commenting on the set of comments constitutes a challenge against all existing phenotype profiles. Therefore, the set of phenotypes selected for the document must be as concise as possible and represent the most common and important observable phenotypes. The Monarch strategy developed a reporting demand meter that tests the width and depth of the phenotype annotation profile in conjunction with all selected phenotypes of humans and model organisms using a five-star rating system for a given individual, with the aim of helping the annotator build a comment profile that is specific enough to exclude related diseases and identify model organisms.
