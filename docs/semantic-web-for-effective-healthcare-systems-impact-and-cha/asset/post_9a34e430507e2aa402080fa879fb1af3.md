## 7.1 Historical Discourse of Surgical Robots

To err is an evolutionary trait of human beings. While it is true that dedicated and single-focused attention towards acquiring a particular skill can significantly reduce the margin of error, the possibility of elimination of all possible errors is a statistical lie. It is important to realize that this lack of guarantee for the elimination of all errors might not hinder or even significantly affect our daily lives and most of the professions, however, fields like surgery and medicine must fail to turn a blind eye to this fact. When the question reduces to saving another person’s life, while dealing with a system as convoluted and complicated as the human body, the chances of committing an unforeseen error is increased drastically, significantly hindering possible routes toward focused attention and dilution of error margins. It is to be noted that surgery, in its most simplistic form, is just a set of many instructions that are to be conducted in a step-wise fashion given a particular observation or inference is made regarding the individual’s anatomy. This reductionism of the complex nature of 21st-century studies begs to ask us the question that—Can it be automated? [5].

Human beings are creators of machines and machines are the cornerstones of automation. They work on algorithms and are unhindered to the stochastic and transient changes of the environment around them, making them most suitable to conduct activities with the significantly complex instruction set and seemingly high stakes involved. In the context of surgery done with a robotic modality, Puma 560 stands at the entrance of the race. The history of robotic surgery has been of a complex nature. Many significant strides were made from a multi-axial focus to direct the attention of organizations, individuals, and institutions towards the positive aspects and the required need for implementation of robotic surgery as a new normal in the medical infrastructure.

Being a brainchild of Kwoh et al., Puma 560 was the surgical robot of its time. It was equipped with a state-of-the-art CT scanner, probe guide, and an instruction set of such complicated nature that it allowed Puma 560 to conduct varied surgical invasions—from neurological biopsies by Kwoh to transurethral resection by Davies [10].

The successor of Puma 560 was the extremely non-ubiquitous surgical system of integrated nature developed by the Burgar and Paul, named as—Robodoc. The singular use-case of Robodoc was the conduction of prosthetic hip replacement.
