## 2.2.4.1 Pros and Cons of Semantic Web

### Cons

All things considered, there is an enormous number of website owners who are not aware of the Semantic Web’s significance. Because of this unawareness, they do not feel motivated. Since it is hard to expect them to include the metadata manually into OWL (Ontology Web Language) semantic markups.

There’s likewise another substantial reason content writers are worried about in regard to the Semantic Web, particularly the owners of those sites which rely upon advertising profit. They believe that there is less profit in creating a semantic website for a specific purpose rather than a simple website or general search engine, since the client will simply look through anything on the program and the outcomes—such as significant dates, normal measurable data, or verifiable information—may appear immediately, making an ordinary connection like indexed lists obsolete, eliminating the need to open websites by any stretch of the imagination. In that way, supporting this is nearly impossible for site owners.

The use and management of personal information could become an issue since it will be more important than ever to establish strong privacy protections. Fundamentally, all that you post online could be exposed because the ability of content generators (or essentially everybody) will be decreased, since it would be where they post the content, which will determine whether to share the data indiscriminately or not.

### Pros

The end users will be the ones profiting the most from this since they will see an improvement in the manner they collaborate with machines, look for data, and explore the Internet.

Users are allowed to communicate with the machine the same way they communicate with other users. There is no requirement to understand or use irrelevant words or expressions; rather, we can use expressions we use regularly.

The machine can answer like the human with the end user through the web; it would seem that the machine is talking with you and attempts to comprehend your need. If you look for or ask anything, it will no longer return the list of links, yet it will return to you the basic answer and give you the data taken from numerous other sites. This is the base of the Semantic Web: the list of links would not work since you are not requesting links; however, you need the outcome in a basic structure in case you request something [11, 12] (g, h).

There is no doubt that the not-too-distant future will see the rise of innovations able to respond to people’s inquiries in a precise and natural manner, even though these days we want to get natural reactions. Machines will be ready to take part in a natural communication system with people more than ever. This may help make computers increasingly adapted to human needs, and possibly, this all-inclusive use of the Semantic Web may create another transformation in the manner we communicate.

## Health Level Seven (HL7)

Health Level Seven International, also known as HL7 [18], founded the Special Interest Group on Vocabulary in 1996. In 1998 this was elevated to a Technical Committee (TC) [5–7]. The HL7 standard is not revenue-driven; it is an American National Standards Institute certified standards-making association. This standard is primarily committed to providing a comprehensive framework and related standards for the exchange, integration, sharing, and collection of electronic healthcare data. This supports clinical practice and the organization, management, and evaluation of healthcare services organizations [8].

Many different types of computer systems or software are used by hospitals and healthcare providers for different types of functionality like billing, patients’ records, and tracking. These systems should communicate with one another when they like to get new information, or when they like to retrieve data; anyway, not all do in that capacity.

Such a huge number of standards, rules, and approaches are specified by HL7 International, by which healthcare systems can communicate with each other. Such guidelines or information standards are a set of rules that allow data to be shared and handled identically and consistently. These data standards are intended to allow healthcare organizations to easily share clinical data [9]. The basic operation of a database management system is to store and manage data. This can be used for corporate and organizational purposes, such as hospitals, private medical centers, clinics, and offices. An effective health management system must have all the related information of its patients, medical records, departments, and doctors. Theoretically, this capacity to exchange information should relieve the pressure by reducing the tendency for clinical care to be geographically isolated and highly variable [10].

## 2.2.4.2 Impact on Patient

The outlook of patients is essentially not the same as that of providers and experts, as they are consumers of the service as opposed to suppliers. Since they are at the medical clinic, they are likely also generally restless, terrified, in pain, or in any case not in their optimal state.

In healthcare technology, product innovation tools are included to support patients: devices like appointment reminders, waiting areas, information displays, and electronic patient records. These might be used alongside clinicians, at the clinic, or somewhere else, either in preparation for their visit or afterward.

These fundamental devices improve the efficiency of the patient’s objectives. These days, no one wants to invest the energy and time filling out forms, whether manual or digital. Patients need to retrieve their data in 5 to 10 minutes, rapidly, simply, and straightforwardly.

Today patients do not just have data about themselves but also have more information to bring to specialists. They can obtain healthcare-applicable data like the nutritional value they consume, food logs, sleep logs, and genetic information in many cases through this. They can better take care of themselves. Through the framework provided by the clinic or any third party, they can also monitor their health records, narratives, prescriptions, and other data. However, while many patients currently have this information, they are not always sure how to manage it, or if and how their provider may use it.

The Semantic Web can support the individual patient, but beyond personal health, it can help by giving a boundless amount of data of varying degrees of legitimacy accessible to the patient about side effects, diagnoses, and medicines. Many patients need or want to have more control over their health, particularly where so many alternatives are accessible, as they have greater availability into the healthcare space. A lot of data on patient care, education, and support are accessible. Most health sites have traditionally been information-based, and the Net (Internet) is, for instance, the best way to find a support group for any illness. It is also a tremendous source of details on individual diseases, no matter how obscure, and is increasingly being used by patients as a way of finding the best hospitals and specialists.

A growing blend of principles, innovations, and social practices layered on top of the best data distribution and sharing mechanism in existence—the World Wide Web.

The vision of a Semantic Web for accelerated research critically relies upon the holders of biological and clinical information making that information accessible in a reusable structure. Frequently, the effort that goes into preparing and serving this information will not directly benefit the provider. Rather, researchers are rewarded for producing scientific discoveries and writing about them, clinicians for helping sick patients, and pharmaceutical companies for developing safe, effective medications. There are also privacy risks associated with sharing personal data. Sensitive patient information must be obtained with appropriate consent and with sensitivity to those privacy issues. It is an open question how to structure incentives to make these holders of valuable data believe the push is in their best interest.

If the research community decided today that it was motivated to publish information semantically, we do not yet have sufficient numbers of skilled data workers. Data modeling, even without the expectation of interoperability, is a hard learned skill, and the challenge is considerably amplified when the goal is to share data for unforeseen uses. We have to build up and populate another discipline, a blend of interdisciplinary skills that incorporate strong understanding of biomedicine, software engineering, reasoning, and the social studies of science and computing.
