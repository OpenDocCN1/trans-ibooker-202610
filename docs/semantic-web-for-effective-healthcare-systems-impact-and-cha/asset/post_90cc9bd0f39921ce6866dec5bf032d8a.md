Current research on automated surveillance methods involves extracting structured information from clinical narratives using NLP pipelines that can deal with synonyms but not similar terms. The challenge here is not the technology per se but the data. Automated surveillance algorithms are blind to data related to emerging diseases such as influenza H1N1 which broke out in 2009. Lin et al. [45] develop a real-time surveillance pipeline for disease statistics by combining word embedding with a convolutional neural network instead of traditional NLP-based approaches.

Systems-of-Systems (SOS) Engineering is one of the sub-fields of Systems Engineering. SOS is a composition of systems consisting of independent, distributed and evolving constituent systems acting jointly toward a common goal by exploiting the synergies between them. Power grid technology, transportation, production and military enterprises are some areas where examples of working SOS systems are available. Nielsen et al. [46] review the current state in model-based techniques in SOS engineering.

A breakout session [47] held at the National Center for Research Resources Conference on the Future of Telehealth recognized telehealth as an SOS based on scale and complexity. The session participants identified key topics on telehealth and discussed the known barriers from the points of view of (1) the patient, (2) the system, and (3) the data. From the perspective of data, barriers identified hindering data collection, compilation, and transmission between healthcare providers, and accessibility of data for research purposes were (a) lack of data standards, and (b) issues around data security, privacy, and trust.

Application of semantic technologies to resource-constrained IoT-based systems is difficult because of the distributed and heterogeneous nature of resources. Ryu et al. [48] present an integrated semantic service platform (ISSP) to create ontological models in various IoT-based service domains of a smart city. This ISSP method attempts to address problem areas in providing integrated semantic services in IoT systems such as semantic discovery, dynamic semantic representation, and semantic data repository. Smart home environments come with many heterogeneous sensors for monitoring health and environmental parameters in users with special needs at home. Alirezaie et al. [49] present an IoT infrastructure system, E-care@home, which gathers measurements from heterogeneous sources using ontologies and integrates the data to enable semantic interpretation of activity-based and event-based context recognition in home environment. Huang et al. [50] present a semantic approach based on ontology for risk recognition and rule reasoning for the expression of context for decision support to provide safety services in a smart home neighborhood.

The realm of digital epidemiology offers methods for application of IT in digital disease detection for supporting infectious disease monitoring and surveillance or for understanding attitudes and concerns about infectious diseases. In their supplement paper, McClure et al. [51] set out to examine the transformations brought by advances in Information and Communications Technology. They draw attention to functional and technical challenges posed by internet-based research and social media usage in epidemiology and healthcare. In his paper [52], Denecke highlights the ethical issues for consideration during integration of digital epidemiology with existing practices. Ioppolo et al. [53] question the readiness of the society to host smart technologies for remote connectedness to allow continuous monitoring and gathering of data for deriving actionable outcomes. After a brief discussion of the links between technological innovation and the healthcare sector, they discuss viewpoints of change needed to drive future research.

## 13.5  Conclusion

Healthcare does not have a universally accepted, clinically fit for purpose, semantically inter-operable standard language. The need of the hour to advance semantic computing in healthcare is not yet another approach to merge medical vocabularies. Merging existing terminologies into a common vocabulary will have inherent clinically significant differences. Despite all recent advances in ML/AI, NLP, and automated data processing technologies, a comprehensive vocabulary system that provides for all clinical and administrative data as per local health needs, at the same time engaging with consumers, will remain a dream for the foreseeable future.

Different stakeholders must come together to revisit existing fragmented upstream, midstream, and downstream care protocols. As care becomes more holistic and encompassing, redundancies, and idiosyncrasies in individual-level “health needs” and community-level “health determinants” will ease. This will pave the way for lightweight data exchange solutions that will make it possible to exchange data in a meaningful way by creating a shared vocabulary for the interplay between pertinent clinical and administrative data along the health continuum.

The direction this era of data economy will take for healthcare depends on how raw data and products associated with data are shared. For the data economy to succeed in the current and future environments, we need to harness data from all known sources in all its sizes and forms. In their viewpoint paper, Tang et al. [54] drive home this exact fact and envision emergence of economic cluster effects within the health data economy, whose potential is maximized by the sharing of data products. Legal and ethical restrictions will continue to limit sharing of sensitive data across organizational boundaries. National and International regulations such as the EU’s GDPR levy strict requirements to protect sensitive personal data. Novel emergent approaches to use data residing in their original repositories, such as the Personal Health Train initiative [55], brings immense potential to circumvent the need to transfer data.

To put it succinctly, standards for data and data interoperability are no longer necessary and sufficient. The primary focus is the development of necessary and sufficient standards for knowledge and knowledge interoperability.
