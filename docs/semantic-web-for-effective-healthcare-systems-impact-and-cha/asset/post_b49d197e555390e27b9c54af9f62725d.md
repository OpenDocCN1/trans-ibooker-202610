## 11.2 Ontology of Biomedicine

An ontology formally describes domain knowledge as a set of concepts and relations between these concepts. Thesauri, terminologies, classifications, and coding systems are commonly used. Ontologies play a major role in biomedical applications across a broad variety of applications, including integration of data, information, and decision support. Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine—SNOMED CT, International Classification of Diseases (ICD), Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes (LOINC), Gene Ontology (GO), Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), RxNorm, Foundational Model of Anatomy (FMA), and Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) are the most popular biomedical ontologies in the field of pharmacy. Each ontology has its own purpose and scope. Interoperability barriers between heterogeneous data thus occur.

Different frameworks or applications use varied terms among multiple users and systems. In 1986, the National Library of Medicine (NLM) developed the UMLS to overcome these barriers, and it is constantly being updated. There are three sources of knowledge: Metathesaurus, Semantic Network, and SPECIALIST Lexicon. The UMLS (2016AA) includes more than 3.25 million concepts (CUIs), 22 million links, and 12 million atoms (AUIs) from over 150 sources. A major objective of the UMLS is to establish mappings between different bio-ontologies. This is done by assigning a concept unique identifier (CUI) to terms from various source ontologies that have similar meanings in the Metathesaurus. The mapping of these vocabularies helps computer systems to exchange information among various data sources. In addition, the rich relationships in the UMLS provide a good foundation for reasoning about clinical knowledge [8]. Different UMLS implementations include ontology integration, natural language processing, and useful collections of clinical quality measures (CQMs).

Since US researchers Michael Cox and David Ellsworth at the National Aeronautics & Space Administration (NASA) first proposed the challenge of big data in 1997 [13], the possibility that vast amounts of information are being generated rapidly around the world has had an impact in all fields of natural science, social science, and engineering [14]. Precision medicine is another therapeutic concept and clinical paradigm that relies on the integration of knowledge from multiple sources [16], including multi-omics, electronic health records, and even mobile devices, also regarded as “personalized medicine.”

The heterogeneity of these biological data has significantly impeded the harmonization of knowledge and integration of precision medicine. A powerful and effective clinical decision system thus includes advanced analysis, knowledge representation, and reasoning. Ontology plays a vital role in linking and applying consistent clinical knowledge. First, ontology provides a terminology mechanism to reduce the heterogeneity of knowledge and enables interoperable information within biomedical data systems. Through the combination of biomedical ontology terms and metadata representation, a unique identifier (ID) with its corresponding label is encoded. For example, HPO terms may be used to normalize the signs and symptoms of a patient. DOID can be used to represent diseases. Regarding metadata standardization, Ontology for Biomedical Investigations (OBI) [17] provides an accurate description of the metadata schema. It not only illustrates the metadata concepts but also reveals the relationships between them. The semantic heterogeneity of biomedical data systems can therefore be reduced to achieve interoperability and automated integration of knowledge; the analysis pipeline and extensible reasoning based on information retrieval strategies can be realized without any problems.

Ontology may help to form the precise and useful representation necessary for precision medicine. Representations depict entities from domains of interest, such as diseases, phenotypes, drugs, and exposures, by naming the entities in each domain and providing computational definitions of various levels of abstraction [18]. The key relation in ontology is the “is a” association, used to construct a fundamental hierarchical system. Beyond that, ontology also specifies other logical relations between entities, for example, the “has phenotype” (RO_0002200) relationship, which is often used to represent a disease inhering in an organism, where the organism has a phenotype. ## Community-Based Development of Ontology

With the proliferation of ontologies, heterogeneity and redundancy have become big challenges in ontology development. A typical approach to resolve these issues is to create mappings based on synonymous relationships. However, this methodology introduces new errors and chaos into ontology. Thus the OBO Foundry, as a widely accepted community of ontology builders, promotes a different method of constructing a network of biomedical ontologies based on shared principles.

## 11.3 Supervised Learning

While AI is a broad field, the work introduced here focuses on supervised learning and even more specifically on classification. It can very well be extended to handle open, ordinal, or structured outputs. The methods presented here can also be applied to reinforcement learning, as well as other forms of AI, such as unsupervised techniques.

The problem is to learn a model M: X → Y as a mapping that assigns classes from Y to objects from X. A variety of such methods have been developed in the field, among them supervised learning approaches, for example, Support Vector Machines [9], Logistic Regression, or Non-Negative Matrix Factorization. Regardless of the specific approach, the ultimate goal is to build models that maximize prediction quality (or minimize a loss function). A problem under consideration is how models can be enhanced when an external structure is known, i.e., hierarchical relationships between class labels or non-IS-A connections among attributes.

It can be seen that ongoing work on neural networks, especially deep learning, can be used as a representation learning paradigm in model structures. In deep learning, various hierarchical models are considered and can be based on hierarchies. Early and subsequent studies on learning systems and the use of background knowledge involved sophisticated inductive logic programming (ILP) and multi-level learning techniques.
