Some solutions to these challenges have been researched and experimented with. The RDF Mapping Language (RML) is the formalization of specification for mapping various heterogeneous knowledge sources and serialization to the RDF Knowledge Graph. Mapping knowledge from different format to RDF model is a complicated task as it involves uniformity, modularity, interoperability and extensibility. RML is an extension of relational database to RDF (R2RML) which is a W3C recommendation [98–100]. Instance Matching (IM) is another technique used to interlink different knowledge bases to retrieve relevant and reliable knowledge for user applications [96]. RDF Knowledge Graph Visualization during the graph compilation process can help extract relevant knowledge while textual document processing and is an effective tool for efficient and effective retrieval [101]. A solution proposed for faster discoverability and efficient integration of knowledge is content-based union and complement metrics from among the knowledge subsets [102]. Centralized storage architectures are also seen as efficient knowledge bases that are cost-effective while being robust [93]. More recent research has been in the use of Artificial Intelligence techniques like Machine Learning for the predictive analysis of the knowledge bases. RDF Frames is a proposal to connect RDF Knowledge Graphs with Machine Learning algorithms [92].

# 10.4.4 Information Retrieval Using SPARQL

SPARQL (Simple Protocol and RDF Query Language²) is a formalized specification to query RDF data to retrieve and manipulate knowledge. It is deployed by Knowledgebase systems to process and produce relevant knowledge from the knowledge base. After the standardization of RDF, many query languages came up as personal, academic and commercial endeavors. In early 2004, W3C established the RDF Data Access Working Group and in late 2004, the working group came up with a draft of the SPARQL specification. Although the SPARQL specification became operational by then, the official W3C specification of the standard SPARQL 1.0 was announced only in 2008. After the expiration of the RDF Data Access Working Group, the SPARQL Working Group was formed 2009 and in late 2009 they came up Drafts of the SPARQL 1.1, which, after many revisions and improvisations became official W3C Recommendations in March of 2013 [103].
