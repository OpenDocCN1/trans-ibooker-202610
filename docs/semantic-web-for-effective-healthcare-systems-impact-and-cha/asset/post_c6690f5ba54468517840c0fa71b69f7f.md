## Precision Medicine in the Context of Ontology

Rehab A. Rayan¹* and Imran Zafar²

¹Department of Epidemiology, High Institute of Public Health, Alexandria University, Alexandria, Egypt

²Department of Bioinformatics and Computational Biology, Virtual University of Pakistan, Lahore, Pakistan

### Abstract

Data volumes in the field of precision medicine (PM) increase exponentially as biomedical technology develops. The disseminated information contains valuable knowledge about the location of the related biomedical units and their semantic relationships. To this end, a model must be established to represent information as ontology in order to formally reflect the relationships between diseases, phenotypes, genes, mutations, drugs and so on, and to achieve effective integration of heterogeneous data. Ontologies are systematic descriptions of knowledge that can combine and examine substantial quantities of heterogeneous data, enabling accurate analysis of a patient. The application of ontologies in healthcare is largely directed at describing and reorganizing medical terminologies. Health professionals have advanced their technical expressions and vocabulary to support storing and communicating generic medical knowledge and patient-specific information proficiently. These terminologies, optimized for human processing, are marked by a substantial quantity of inherent knowledge. Health information systems should deliver complicated and comprehensive medical concepts without ambiguities, which is a challenging business demanding a thorough investigation of the construction and the concepts of these terminologies. However, it could be realized by building medical domain ontologies for describing these information systems. The principal aim of this chapter is to explain ontologies and their application in computational reasoning to promote an accurate classification of patients’ diagnosis and managing care, and for translational research.

### 9.1 Introduction

Precision medicine (PM) is a way to treat and prevent diseases that aims to maximize effectiveness by taking into account variation in genes, environmental factors [1]. The PM network also connects patients, carers, clinical laboratories and prospective investigations [2]. With the rapid expansion of personalized medicine, the amounts of heterogeneous clinical data and scientific information are exponentially growing, where valuable knowledge such as functional biomedical units and their semantic relations are inaccessible. One crucial question is how to know and use the knowledge to assist PM. By incorporating, classifying and standardizing these biomedical tools from different resources and establishing relationships between them, terminologies, classifications and ontologies may provide varying degrees of complexity in computational formats, enabling enormous data collections to be analyzed and a patient to be properly categorized [3]. Construction of these knowledge models is necessary for PM studies, and appropriate.

Ontology is a powerful technique for representing pragmatically and understanding biomedicine. In the same contexts, precision or personalized medicine is a novel model in the concept of ontologies that ensures the outstanding confidence for individualized patient care with incremental manners. The framework of ontologies is emerging to improve patients’ diagnosis and treatment via providing easy tools and methods of medical decision support system-level for multiple reasoning mechanisms in precision medicine. To deal with these large datasets or data is heterogeneous in nature and collected from individual patients, here we acquired suitable methods to identify the basic knowledge in which every patient potentially different, to handle specifically changing or issues like different signs and symptoms from diseases. The ontological information is worth mentioning since the scientific information and clinical data are heterogeneous or hard to manipulate. Based on classification approaches in the domain of ontologies, it provides the facility for handling disease mechanisms in several patients correlating top-level information [4].

Terminologies have a long history of recalling information, such as searching for documents or database records that meet certain criteria [5]. Many of the most relevant medical knowledge retrieval tools include Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) for PubMed Indexing and Search; RxNorm [6], a standardized and renamed medication terminology; and UMLS (Unified Medical Language System), which contains over 100 clinical terminologies and coding systems [7]. These tools, which contain common names and synonyms and cross-reference lists, shape the search and indexing data and are often used in EHRs and public databases. Ontologies differ from vocabularies in that ontologies explain the relationships between concepts in a way that enables rational logical reasoning to draw conclusions from similar statements [8]. For example, if ontology classifies “virus” as an infectious agent and classifies “infectious meningitis” as a form of infectious meningitis, it will conclude that “viral meningitis” is a subclass of “infectious meningitis.” Aristotle created a conceptual taxonomy that in some way mimics modern bioontologies [9]. Researchers have recently used the term “ontology” to indicate a computational description that explains the cognitive aspects of comprehension. The ontology contains a set of concepts (terms) and synonyms thereof as well as concise logical definitions that describe the formal relations between concepts.

Using concise syntax in ontology can maintain logical coherence, even with hundreds or thousands of concepts across multiple domains, allowing computational processes to define facts that are implicit but not specifically specified in original data. Therefore, ontologies can help leverage the latent knowledge of clinical big data by encoding data with “computable” semantics, allowing machine learning and other algorithms to meet challenges in the analysis of high-capacity multimodal data by combining it with clinical significance. Ontologies can also be used in conjunction with the use of natural languages in extremely clear textual concepts, such as those found in clinical commentaries [10], and improve knowledge extraction from EHRs and other sources. Ontologies will also enable the integration of basic scientific data such as animal model data and general information like associations between genetic variations and diseases, enabling patient identification based on data collection far beyond the EHR and enabling new clinical understanding [11]. The integration of huge amounts of data and the cheap high-capacity computer offers the ability to gain therapeutic and diagnostic knowledge for excellent interaction analysis, significant descriptive analysis and inferential.
