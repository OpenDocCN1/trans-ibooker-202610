Some of the common rare diseases usually found are thalassemia, hemophilia, sickle-cell anemia, autoimmune diseases, primary immunodeficiency in children, lysosomal storage disorders such as Pompe disease, Gaucher’s disease, Hirschsprung disease, cystic fibrosis, hemangiomas, and some forms of muscular dystrophies [3].

In the United States, the Rare Diseases Act of the year 2002 defines a rare disease formally according to its occurrence frequency, as “any disease or condition that affects fewer than 200,000 people in the United States,” usually 1 in every 1500 people. This definition is principally similar to that of the Orphan Drug Act, 1983, which was a federal law written to inspire and encourage research into rare diseases and probable cures [4]. In Japan, the legally accepted definition of a rare disease is one that affects less than 50,000 patients in Japan or having a frequency of about 1 in every 2,500 people [4].

On the other hand, the European Commission on Public Health has defined rare diseases as “life-threatening or chronically debilitating diseases which are of such low prevalence that special combined efforts are needed to address them” [4]. The term low prevalence has later been defined as usually meaning less than 1 in every 2,000 people. Statistically, the diseases which are rare, but not life-threatening, frequently debilitating, or ineffectively treated, have been excluded from their definition [4]. The World Health Organization defines a rare disease as all pathological conditions affecting 0.65–1 out of every 1000 inhabitants. These figures clearly relate to the size of the population of these countries, but even modifying for that, the definitions differ from about one to eight in 10,000 [8].

## 12.3 Characteristics of Rare Diseases (RDs)

Rare diseases are very challenging within the scope of diagnosis. Due to a lack of proper information, early detection of rare diseases becomes a major challenge. To avoid any sort of complications, proper information and characteristics of rare diseases have become a top priority. Characterization helps to identify the types of rare diseases and its classification becomes easier. This would ease the physician to diagnose and proceed relatively smoothly.

Following are some of the common characteristics of rare diseases [5]:

- 1. Among various etiologies, RDs may result from a genetic disorder, pathogenic agents, toxic agents, allergies, or other possible causes. Rare diseases can be acute or chronic and can affect anyone at any age [10].

- 2. Though there are numerous causes for rare diseases, many of them are thought to be genetic, which either can be passed from generation to generation or can randomly occur in a person, which can become first to be diagnosed in the family.

- 3. The symptoms might range from severe to very severe or may be chronic and degenerative, leading to lethal conditions.

- 4. Some rare diseases prove to be inevitably fatal given currently available treatments.

- 5. The diseases are not easy to manage; both patients and families undergo struggles for treatment. Therefore, they can be extremely painful, resulting in psychological stress. As there is no proper cure and treatment, there is no hope of getting over the disease and living a normal healthy life.

- 6. It risks the quality of life, and as most of them are incurable, they increase the mortality chances, being unusual for treatment [5].

The characteristics of rare diseases depend on distinct factors. Several factors such as disease history, clinical research, available disease monitoring, treatment evaluation, etc. are often considered for the characterization of rare diseases. Moreover, geographical factors (geographic area, geographic country, geographic international, etc.) also play a major role [9, 10]. There might be diseases rare in one part of the world but common in another part. For example, tuberculosis, a rare disease, is common in less economically developed countries but rare in developed countries. Thus the range of rarity may change from rare to extremely rare or rare to common, and vice versa.

## 12.4 Types of Rare Diseases

Generally, types of rare diseases are studied based on their cause: genetic, non-genetic, pathogenic, toxic, or other factors. There are still some rare diseases with not known, unspecified, or insufficient facts. There are few rare diseases with obscure causes such as Gorham’s disease. It is a very rare disease that has been named and characterized for decades but is now under idiopathic diseases. Likewise, some rare diseases have more than one cause like hepatitis or herpes virus with multiple causes. Hepatitis can be inherited or caused by sexual contact or contact with the virus through food or drinks. Based on causes, some types of rare diseases are listed below.

### 12.4.1 Genetic Causes

Rare diseases can be caused by alterations in single or multiple genes. According to experts, the rarest of diseases are genetic in origin, more than 80 percent [10]. Generally, a genetic disease is classified into two types: single-gene disorder (monogenic), where a specific gene is mutated or permanently altered, and multiple-gene disorders, where more than one gene is altered. This mutation can be due to failure of repair mechanism, environmental factors, or other reasons. These are inherited depending on their inheritance pattern, i.e. autosomal dominant or recessive, X-linked or Y-linked. Depending on this pattern, rare diseases can be inherited by two carriers (both affected parents) or by a single carrier (single affected parent) from generation to generation. Following are some examples of inherited rare diseases listed below:

- I) Von Hippel–Lindau syndrome (VHL) [11, 12] affects numerous organ systems in the body by the formation of cysts and benign tumors with potency to transform into malignant forms, affecting various body parts like kidneys, uterus, pancreas, and nervous system. It is caused by a mutation in a specific tumor suppressor gene (chromosome 3) and is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. Studies show that 80% of cases are due to heredity and the rest are due to different mutations [12].

- II) Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) [11] is a genetic disease inherited by an autosomal recessive or X-linked pattern. The affected person loses resistance to various infectious diseases. According to reports, there is a mutation on the X chromosome and defects on chromosome 7 (CYBB gene) and chromosome 16 (NCF1 gene). Mutations can interfere with the WBC’s ability to produce oxygen compounds that are essential to kill harmful pathogens. On the contrary, the immune system can produce granulomas in various parts of the body, thereby isolating bacteria [12].

Some metabolic disorders caused by malfunction or deficiency of a specific enzyme are monogenic. For example, lysosomal storage disorders result in numerous enzyme deficiencies, causing metabolic disorders like Hurler syndrome (alpha-L-iduronidase deficiency), Tay–Sachs disease (Hex-A deficiency), Fabry disease (alpha-galactosidase A deficiency), Gaucher disease, etc. These are caused as a result of an alteration in a specific gene [11, 13, 14].

### 12.4.2 Non-Genetic Causes

Some diseases are a result of genetic mutations but are not inherited. Mutations in the genes can be due to environmental factors, for example, toxic substances due to smoking. Klippel–Trenaunay syndrome [14] is a rare disease that is a result of genetic mutation in the PIK3CA gene but is nonhereditary. These mutations are responsible for developing tissue overgrowth in the body [16].

### 12.4.3 Pathogenic Causes (Infectious Agents)

Numerous pathogens like bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites cause disease more often. Among the estimated statistics, there are several rare diseases caused by infectious agents. As these diseases are rare, many public health experts and non-profit organizations have encouraged people to raise awareness through intensive campaigns and the development of affordable treatments [9]. Many diseases caused by pathogens can also be fatal due to insufficient information and unaffordable treatments. Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (Rickettsia bacterium), Whipple disease (*Tropheryma whipplei* or *Tropheryma whippelii*), Legionnaires’ disease (bacteria Legionella), Hansen’s disease (also known as leprosy) (*Mycobacterium leprae*) are some fatal diseases caused by pathogenic agents. Likewise, some viral diseases are Hantavirus pulmonary syndrome, rabies, Zika virus infection, and monkeypox virus infection [13, 14].
