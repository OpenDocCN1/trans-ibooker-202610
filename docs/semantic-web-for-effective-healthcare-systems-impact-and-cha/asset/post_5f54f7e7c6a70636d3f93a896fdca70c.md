## 7.5 Transoral Robotic Surgery

Robotic automation is now becoming an essentially inclusive part of many fields of surgery. One of the notable examples being tonsillectomy. Recently, a new automatic method has been implemented for the same—TORS (Transoral Robotic Surgery) Radical tonsillectomy. Developed primarily for the treatment and management of carcinoma of tonsillar nature, TORS offers the surgeons various advantages. Nonrobotic procedures limit the surgical access and therefore restrict Transoral resections’ indications of malignant neoplasms to a restricted area of the tonsillar fossa. TORS, on the other hand, uses the endoscopic optics of high-quality for increased instrument mobility of surgical robot which further facilitates operative and wide-reaching Transoral resection of tonsillar carcinoma. This is done concurrently with decreasing the added indisposition often associated with open surgical processes [13–20]. Today, the use of TORS is not only limited to tonsillectomy. It’s also being used in similar arenas of supraglottic partial laryngectomy, canine model of supraglottic laryngectomy, advanced oropharyngeal carcinoma, and head and neck cancer surgery as well.

## 7.6 Pancreatoduodenectomy

The pancreas is an organ that holds a crucial role in the digestion and regulation of blood sugar. There are mainly three diseases that strike the pancreas—Diabetes, Pancreatitis, and Pancreatic Cancer.

Standing boldly at the fourth position of the foremost root of mortality in the USA is pancreatic cancer [11, 26–30]. The odds of survival further fall low, when it is seen statistically that a very small percentage of diagnosed patients (over 50,000 in the year 2019) is operable [12]. These call for the need for automation of the same process and early diagnosis of the same given that pancreatoduodenectomy is itself a challenging procedure for the best of the surgeons. Today, as the debate over the advantages between robotic PD and laparoscopic or “open” PD continues, it has been 10 years since surgeons have started using the Da Vinci systems while considering pancreatoduodenectomy [2]. A recent study on the literature written about the pancreatoduodenectomy shows that while RPD (Robotic PD) offers theoretical advantages over laparoscopic PD by improved instrument dexterity, better ergonomics, and 3D visualizations, it practically stands at par with the laparoscopic method [4]. However, this does not deny the fact that the advantages it offers outweigh its other aspects.

Currently, it is safe to say that robotic-assisted PD is better suited to patients. This is based on the general observed rate of faster recovery post-operatively alongside the reduced blood loss during the surgery [21]. This also calls attention to the fact that a more comprehensive RCT is badly needed to help us draw more tactful conclusions regarding robotic PD and its manifestations on the patients’ signs post-operation.

## 7.8 Rectal Tumor Surgery

A recent paper published drew an evaluation between the laparoscopic and robotic procedures for rectal surgery. The parameters taken into consideration included short-term outcomes, perioperative data, and extent of compliance to ERAS protocol (Enhanced Recovery After Surgery). Though the paper suggested a difference of 2.4% in the extent of compliance between the laparoscopic group and the robotic group, robotic surgery was found to be responsible for shorter LOS, lower conversion rates, and fewer postoperative surgery complications when contrasted to laparoscopic surgery. There is still a lot to be done in robotic surgery to increase its compliance with ERAS protocol in rectal tumor surgery.

## 7.9 Robotic Lung Cancer Surgery

For a field that is highly dependent on video-assisted thoracic surgery, robotic surgery placed a big upgrade. Robotic surgery has few features similar to VATS, which help the surgeons easily adapt to the newer system without losing their ease of usage and skill precision.

Proper port placements are crucial when it comes to maximizing the advantages of robotic surgery. When it boils down to the cruciality behind a particular mechanism, the utility of the overall goal often outweighs the cost for minor adjustments. The corporate-giant Zeus, which stands as the front face in the race of robotic surgery, designated a special research team that was assigned the task to chart out the positions and anatomical coordinates for the placement of ports. Various strategies were taken into consideration; these included posterolateral, anterolateral, and mediolateral approaches. Finally, after careful conduction of a series of experiments on dummy bodies, it was found that the anterolateral approach must be designated for designing and finding physical markers on the human body for port placement.

Later, studies were conducted by the team at Zeus Research Labs and other independent organizations working in the field of robotic surgery, in the lieu of identifying any ports in the procedure which stood excess and can be removed, hence aligning with the principles of minimally invasive surgery. On the conduction of thousands of experiments and years of research, it was concluded that the posterior port can be removed from the protocol for robotic surgery. The payoff of this reduction was the minimization of the total port count and the optimization of the gap between adjacent robotic arms. However, this payoff is not localized to the reduction of some mathematical quantities.
