Because of the complexities of medical terminologies and the fact that a medical term can mean differently in different contexts, lay people easily misunderstand terms commonly used by physicians. Cosic et al. [24] tested how well patients understand terminology commonly used with them in inpatient and emergency settings of an orthopedic department. Two key findings from this study are that patients (1) do not completely understand healthcare terms commonly used by the professionals, and (2) were ignorant of this fact that they have a poor understanding of orthopedic terms. The findings of this study highlight the seriousness of having clear terminology in use to enable (a) effective communication between healthcare professionals and patients, (b) transparent and informed dialog with patient to get consent to care, and (c) patient satisfaction regarding health outcomes.

## 13.3 Semantic Analytics and Cognitive Computing: Recent Trends

From the early nineteenth century, statistical techniques have contributed in bringing about notable transformations to healthcare. The advances in design, engineering, and analytics in recent decades offer much improved capabilities and disruptive solutions to age-old problems. “Data Normalization,” “Semantic Analytics,” “Cognitive Computing,” “eHealth Literacy” are recent industry buzzwords for the evolving interdisciplinary field in healthcare big data analytics to bring positive shifts in clinical decision support systems and related technologies using semantically normalized medical terminology.

Although there is much progress in knowledge and NLP techniques, the results are not yet significantly satisfactory to use free text without any constraints in clinical documentation. This section presents select research trends from this growing interdisciplinary field to draw the attention of the reader to the complexities of the underlying research problems that makes finding a practical solution difficult and elusive.

### 13.3.1 Semantic Data Analysis

Since the late twentieth century, informaticists and data scientists are tirelessly exploring novel solutions to extract, standardize, and analyze health data locked as narrative free-text in health data repositories. Advances in cognitive computing tools offer exciting opportunities in integrating NLP techniques with everyday clinical care.

For the medical informatics generalist, Nadkarni et al. [25] offer an overview and tutorial of NLP and design of modern NLP systems. Starting with the historical evolution of NLP, the authors describe key NLP sub-problems and draw attention of the reader to selected medical NLP efforts. Velupillai et al. [26] review advances in clinical NLP used to analyze clinical texts semantically. [27] investigate suitability of different types of NLP tasks and their applications when supported by machine learning algorithms. They also explore the needed properties that must be present in the text data used for training the model. [28] show a way of addressing the annotation obstacle in medical procedure classification by building a neural network-based processing pipeline for query matching. In particular, they show how adding related concepts to the original input of the semantic network improves semantic matching.

### 13.3.3 Semantic Applications

Clinical models (CMs) drive documentation of clinical examinations, screenings or measurements in primary care settings and are defined locally to suit various clinical workflows. **Gøeg et al.** [40] hierarchically cluster CMs based on SNOMED-CT and semantic similarity estimation with average and best-match-average aggregation techniques. Dendrograms enable visualization of the hierarchical clusters and comparison of the results of the two aggregation techniques. In secondary care settings, care plans in clinical pathways (CPs) are of greater use when adapted to personalize the treatment experience. A care plan is too generic to provide value in its standardized form. **Wang et al.** [41] customize CPs using a data-driven approach for semantic analysis and reasoning of historical clinical data in the hospital. The authors represent hospital-specific terms by adding local ontologies to the CPs. Hospital-specific clinical data undergoes semantic transformation for storage in a semantic data model and disease-specific treatment procedures include physicians’ frequently used medical interventions. “To standardize, or not to standardize” remains an elusive question in clinical practices where the care needs of patients vary by the healthcare environment. The challenge is to take local customizations and reach the summit point where information models and terminologies are globally standardized using semantic interoperability.

Decision making requires actionable information, synthesized from smart data. **Panahiazar et al.** [42] use semantic web technology for integrating and re-using heterogeneous resources for knowledge discovery. They standardize data by annotating unstructured and semi-structured data with the ontology concepts. Web agents use the annotated data to interpret knowledge from resources such as images, text, and XML files. Highlighting the challenges in using big data, the authors present practical use cases to emphasize the need for a semantic data-driven environment. **Jovanovik et al.** [43] develop a set of reusable methods and tools to transform heterogeneous dataset of drug products available from independent and distributed sources into a comprehensive Linked Drug Data dataset. Drug data is collected from the official national drug registries of twenty-three countries using an automated system which then cleans the data, aligning it for transformation into 5-star Linked Data. The consolidated Linked Data dataset is then published on the Web for common use.
