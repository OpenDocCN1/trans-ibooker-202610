## Abstract

The clinical decisions are driven by all the available information of the medical and patient history. The issue currently faced is the provision of medical information that is based on evidence as soon as its requirement in delivering the best possible care at that time. This can be done by making the “evidence based actionable content” available to the healthcare professional at that time so as he can choose the best possible decision at that time. The actionable content gives clinical guidance in terms of recommendations that can be queried, link to relevant context, and actionable items. To discover actionable content in healthcare domain, health analytics needs a sequence of three types of analytics: descriptive, predictive and prescriptive. These analytics helps to retrieve coherent meaningful information that can represent actionable content. Traditional machine learning techniques help to transform descriptive analytics into predictive analytics. However, these techniques are data-hungry and in healthcare domain, data comes from multiple heterogeneous sources. These heterogeneity issues are addressed using semantic technology i.e. ontology. This chapter discusses the usability of ontology and proposed ontology-driven three layer architecture to extract actionable content from multiple heterogeneous information sources in healthcare domain.

**Keywords:** Analytics, descriptive analytics, predictive analytics, prescriptive analytics, ontology, actionable content

## 5.1 Introduction

Healthcare data is collected and analyzed to improve the quality of health of patients, reduce costs of treatment, and also to generate meaningful insights for patients and researchers. The clinical decisions taken by the healthcare system are driven by all the available information on the medical data and also patient history for specific kinds of illnesses. This requires validation by medical practitioners [34]. The best care can, however, be guaranteed by providing evidence-based medical information. For this Health professionals need “actionable content.”

The question remains as to how the actionable content should be included in the practice and be made efficient for use by aiding in clinical decision making and administrative capability. As an example, suppose an oncologist has an appointment scheduled with a 44-year-old premenopausal woman.

After some primer examination, it was discovered that the woman has an early stage of breast cancer. It was found that it is triple-negative (ER, PR Her-2 negative), and the woman has a history of breast cancer, in her family. Although this was crucial in diagnosis, she has not gone through genetic testing for a BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation that would affirm (or dismiss) an inclination to breast or ovarian malignancy.

At the time when the appointment between the patient and the oncologist is going on, details about the options in treatment regimens, health insurance plans for the patient, altering clinical traits to be administered to the patient should be made available, which is just at the click of a button. In other words, the workflow should be generated. The challenges in the processing are multi-fold. Not only the information should be made available, but it has to be filtered and processed for actionable content. There is a need for high-quality data and good metadata which is valid and comparable with other resources. Also, Artificial Intelligence (AI) and clinical decision making play an important role in this scenario. The AI model with ontologies will help to access information from multiple sources and transform it into a stepwise procedure. This would influence doctors or medical practitioners in the decision-making process.

“Actionable content is meaningful actions obtained by analyzing any type of raw data. They help organizations make well-informed decisions and they are obtained as a result of data analytics.” Not all insights are actionable though. The goal of this chapter is to present the use of ontology in the extraction of actionable content in the healthcare domain. This can be achieved by initially providing a sketch of the emerging discipline of

health analytics and put forth ontology as one of the tools for extracting actionable content.

The entire chapter is structured as follows. Section 5.2 renders the various definitions of Actionable content with its descriptions. Section 5.3 focuses on the stages of health analytics and Section 5.4 focuses specifically on how ontology can be used for prescriptive health analytics.

## 5.2 Actionable Content

Actionable intelligence/knowledge/Content “is not only relevant to the world of practice, but it is also the knowledge that people use to create that world” [5, 14]. There was a wide gap between the output of data mining algorithms and the effect of this output in terms of actionable output. This gap was because of the academic objectives and the business goals between identified patterns and deliverables. This entailed the requirement of active knowledge that had to be identified and delivered in the form of an action plan.

### 5.2.1 Actionable Content in Theory

This conceptualization of the term Actionable content started in [6] Cleveland in 2001 when a term Action plan was suggested. Then in [7, 10, 11] 2010 Cao et al., presented a conventional view of Actionable Knowledge Discovery (AKD). It was elucidated from the system and decision-making perspectives. Actionable knowledge discovery was defined as a “closed optimization problem”¹. AKD is designed to deliver business rules which are operable and seamlessly integrated with business processes and systems. The six dimensions of AKD which are described as an optimization process are as follows. This is a generic structure that is applicable in any domain. According to [8, 9] AKD is a five dimensional optimization process.

```
AKD ::=optimization(problem, environment, model, decision, data)
```
