Ontologies differ from vocabulary in that ontologies explain the relationships between concepts in a way that enables rational logical reasoning to draw conclusions from similar statements [8]. For example, if ontology classifies “virus” as an infectious agent and classifies “infectious meningitis” as a form of infectious meningitis, it will conclude that “viral meningitis” is a subclass of “infectious meningitis.” Aristotle created a conceptual taxonomy that in some way mimics modern bioontologies [9]. Researchers have recently used the term “ontology” to indicate a computational description that explains the cognitive aspects of comprehension. The ontology contains a set of concepts (terms) and synonyms thereof as well as concise logical definitions that describe the formal relations between concepts.

Using concise syntax in ontology can maintain logical coherence, even with hundreds or thousands of concepts across multiple domains, allowing computational processes to define facts that are implicit but not specifically specified in original data. Therefore, Ontologies can help leverage the latent knowledge of clinical big data by encoding data with “computable” semiconductors, allowing machine learning and other algorithms to meet challenges in the analysis of high-capacity multimodal data by combining it with clinical significance. Ontologies can also be used in conjunction with the use of natural languages in extremely clear textual concepts, such as those found in clinical commentaries [10], and improve knowledge extraction from EHRs and other sources. Ontologies will also enable the integration of basic scientific data such as animal model data and general information like associations between genetic variations and diseases, enabling patient identification based on data collection far beyond the EHR and enabling new clinical understanding [11]. The integration of huge amounts of data and the cheap high-capacity computer offers the ability to gain therapeutic and diagnostic knowledge for excellent interaction analysis, significant descriptive analysis and inferential.

Carl Linné (1707–1778), who classified the diseases into 11 classes, 37 orders, and 325 species, developed the first advanced medical hierarchy that could be known as a real unit of disease (nosology). While the classification of Linnaeus retains certain errors from a scientific perspective, such as the notion that leprosy can be caught by consuming herring worms, its classification lays the foundation for the research that eventually led to the first edition of ICD, 1893. After its development as a cause of death, ICD has developed enormously but it continues to be intended as a guideline for epidemiology, health management and billing, not as a measurement of the patient as a biological substance.
