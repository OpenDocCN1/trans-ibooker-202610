## 10.4.2 Knowledge Graphs

Graph-based knowledge representation has two advantages especially for knowledge engineers and experts who design and build the knowledge base of Knowledgebase system. The visualization of objects in a graph makes it easily understood and comfortable to handle. The reason why semantic networks are a popular form of knowledge representation is precisely due to its graph-like feature. Moreover, the reasoning mechanism in graph structures is understood without much complexity due to the structure-preserving mapping feature of graph or graph homomorphism. The homomorphic feature of the graph allows the graphs to be broken into smaller components for modelling purposes which can be easily visualized. Also, the same graphic form is used in both the computational and interface level circumventing the need for transforming the knowledge base components for the user interface. The other advantage of graph-based knowledge representation is at the level of computation. The homomorphism of graphs allows the basic graphs to be anchored on to the combinatorics. The homomorphism unifies the results of complexity with the algorithm in many domains. The graphic representation used instead of logical formulas can also contribute to newer algorithm ideas. Moreover, labeled graphs are more suitable for knowledge acquisition processes in building a knowledge base [79].

Knowledge graphs can be defined as the knowledge base of a Knowledgebase system in which the knowledge is represented using a graph structure where the different knowledge concepts are the nodes of the graph and its edges are the relationship between the concepts. The building of the knowledge graph involves all the technologies that are used in the creation of the knowledge base since the knowledge graph is itself the knowledge base [80–83]. There are numerous definitions for knowledge graphs ranging from precise technical definition to more generic definitions [84, 85]. There are conceptual and empirical definitions of Knowledge Graphs [86]. The type of the graph of knowledge graphs may vary depending on the model of the knowledge representation. The term “Knowledge Graph” was probably used by E.W. Schneider in 1972 [87] and later in 1982 Cornelis Hoede of the University of Twente and F.N. Stokman of the University of Groningen used it as a discrete mathematical concept [88]. Currently, knowledge graphs are vital to many tech companies especially those that work with Big Data on the internet like Microsoft, Google, Facebook, IBM, eBay, Yahoo, and the like [89, 90].

The generation of a Knowledge Graph involves the process of acquiring knowledge in which the editing, mapping and annotation are done, the storage of the acquired knowledge, the curation of the knowledge by assessment, cleaning and enrichment of the knowledge and the deployment of the knowledge in a Knowledgebase system [80]. The curation or the refinement process is crucial to the deployment of the knowledge base and the efficiency of the system. Knowledge acquisition can be done either by refining existing Knowledge Graphs or by crowd editing or by extracting knowledge from large semi-structured knowledge bases or using information extraction process from unstructured or semi-structured sources. Whichever process of knowledge acquisition is deployed, the result will be imperfect and unreliable unless an effective process of curation is used. Two main aims of the curation process are the graph completion by which the missing knowledge is added to complete the graph and error detection to ensure that the graph is without misinformation and reliable. Although the process of knowledge creation and knowledge refinement may be similar, they are deployed separately since the refinement can work simultaneously on different graphs and the refinement process will give a clearer understanding of the graph that is built [80, 91].

## 10.4.3 RDF Knowledge Graphs

RDF Stores are usually used to store knowledge graphs and therefore Knowledge Graphs with RDF knowledge model are referred to as RDF Knowledge Graphs. RDF Knowledge Graphs can be defined as Knowledge Graphs in which knowledge is represented in the form of triples consisting subject, predicate and object [92–95]. RDF Knowledge Graph can also be defined as labelled multi-digraph in which the set of nodes could be either URIs or literals, and the corresponding edges are URIs representing the predicates [93–96].

There are several challenges in deploying RDF Knowledge Graphs. Understanding and encapsulating the concepts from a variety of heterogeneous knowledge sources is an important challenge. The users need to understand the entities in the RDF Knowledge Graphs so that the knowledge graph can be deployed directly to user applications. Another challenge is in exploiting the knowledge available in the RDF Knowledge Graph. It requires efficient retrieval techniques. Linking various knowledge bases represented using RDF Knowledge Graph is also a challenge since it requires effectively choosing the right knowledge bases for the solution that is sought by the application. Similarly, efficient and effective retrieval of relevant knowledge from huge knowledge bases for which automatic query generation could give insights is another big challenge in deploying RDF Knowledge Graphs [97]. Some solutions to these challenges have been researched and experimented with. The RDF Mapping Language (RML) is the formalization of specification for mapping various heterogeneous knowledge sources and serialization to the RDF Knowledge Graph. Mapping knowledge from different format to RDF model is a complicated task as it involves uniformity, modularity, interoperability and extensibility. RML is an extension of relational database to RDF (R2RML) which is a W3C recommendation [98–100]. Instance Matching (IM) is another technique used to interlink different knowledge bases to retrieve relevant and reliable knowledge for user applications [96]. RDF Knowledge Graph Visualization during the graph compilation process can help extract relevant knowledge while textual document processing and is an effective tool for efficient and effective retrieval [101]. A solution proposed for faster discoverability and efficient integration of knowledge is content-based union and complement metrics from among the knowledge subsets [102]. Centralized storage architectures are also seen as efficient knowledge bases that are cost-effective while being robust [93]. More recent research has been in the use of Artificial Intelligence techniques like Machine Learning for the predictive analysis of the knowledge bases. RDF Frames is a proposal to connect RDF Knowledge Graphs with Machine Learning algorithms [92].

## 10.4.4 Information Retrieval Using SPARQL

SPARQL (Simple Protocol and RDF Query Language²) is a formalized specification to query RDF data to retrieve and manipulate knowledge. It is deployed by Knowledgebase systems to process and produce relevant knowledge from the knowledge base. After the standardization of RDF, many query languages came up as personal, academic and commercial endeavors. In early 2004, W3C established the RDF Data Access Working Group and in late 2004, the working group came up with a draft of the SPARQL specification. Although the SPARQL specification became operational by then, the official W3C specification of the standard SPARQL 1.0 was announced only in 2008. After the expiration of the RDF Data Access Working Group, the SPARQL Working Group was formed 2009 and in late 2009 they came up Drafts of the SPARQL 1.1, which, after many revisions and improvisations became official W3C Recommendations in March of 2013 [103].
