Minimally invasive surgery was first performed in 1987. It was a laparoscopic cholecystectomy. As the list of procedures performed laparoscopically grew at an ever-increasing rate, so did the technological advancement and general refinement of the skill of surgeons. Minimally invasive surgery was an idea that the masses immediately bought upon its conception. MIS offered the public and surgeons a great number of advantages. Minimally Invasive Surgery opened a world of smaller incisions, reduced infection risks, and shorter stays in the hospital. It also enables the option to significantly reduce convalescence depending on need.

Though MIS attracted the masses and provided a simplistic system for surgery, it involved a lot of technical and mechanical difficulties. The deficit of haptic feedback, dexterity, and eye-hand coordination posed major difficulties for the surgeons. An example of the above being the movement of laparoscopic instruments in the opposite direction of interest to reach it. This nullified the coordination between hand and eye. This situation is also referred to as the “fulcrum effect.”

There are 7 degrees of freedom in the human hand-wrist, this refers to the fact that the hand can independently move and rotate in a total of seven unique directions. However, the current laparoscopic instruments have only 4 degrees of freedom which again posed difficulties to the surgeons. These instruments also lack the sense of touch which makes procedures regarding manipulation of tissue visualization-dependent. Also, the physiologic tremors that were generated in a surgeon’s hand while the operation was now transmitted readily across rigid instruments’ length.

These limitations accounted for the reason why performing MIS with automated laparoscopic instruments makes the anastomoses and delicate dissections difficult if not impossible. The burning necessity to overcome such limitations laid the founding stones towards the conception of surgical robots.
