Thus, it is not possible to prove $P(e)$ through reasoning by structural induction over the expression $e$. This property cannot be proved by induction over the structure of expressions and requires an induction over the “size” of the computations involved in evaluation. Considering the evaluation function $\lfloor \_ \rfloor$, this can be done using the least fixpoint theory for continuous functions, as described in section 4.7.1. The property will be proved here using big-step operational semantics, which allows us to reason by induction over the evaluation trees; this is a way to consider the “size” of the computations involved in evaluation.

## 3.4.2. Induction over evaluation trees

Our aim here is to prove a property $P$ which states that if an expression is evaluated as a value, then this value is unique. Formally, within the framework of big-step operational semantics, we wish to prove the following proposition:

### PROPOSITION 3.1.– Determinism:

\[
\forall e \in \mathbf{Exp}_2 \ \forall Env \in \mathbf{E} \ \forall v, v' \in \mathbb{V} \ (Env \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \text{ and } Env \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v') \Rightarrow v = v'
\]

The set of evaluation (or inference) trees for the expressions in $\mathbf{Exp}_2$ can be defined inductively, which means that we can reason by induction over these trees, i.e. over the “size” of the computations carried out during evaluations. More generally, given an inference system $\mathbb{S}$, i.e. a set of inference rules of the form

\[
(R) \frac{e_1 \cdots e_n}{e}
\]

the set $\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{S}$ of elements $e$ that can be inferred (sometimes called the set of theorems of $\mathbb{S}$) is defined inductively as follows:

- for every axiom in $\mathbb{S}$, i.e. for every rule of the form $(R)-$, the conclusion $e$ belongs to $\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{S}$;
- if $e_1, \cdots, e_n$ are elements in $\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{S}$, and if

\[
(R) \frac{e_1 \cdots e_n}{e}
\]

is a rule in $\mathbb{S}$, then \( e \in \mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{S}} \). Indeed if \( e_1, \cdots, e_n \) are elements in \( \mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{S}} \), then there exist trees \( \nabla_1, \cdots, \nabla_n \) for each of these elements, and we can therefore construct the following tree:

\[
\frac{ \begin{array}{c} \nabla_1 \quad \cdots \quad \nabla_n \\ e_1 \quad \cdots \quad e_n \end{array} }{ e } (R)
\]

Proving a property \( P \) over the elements of \( \mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{S}} \) comes down to proving that this property holds for all elements concluding an axiom in $\mathbb{S}$ and proving, for each rule

\[
(R) \frac{ e_1 \cdots e_n }{ e }
\]

in $\mathbb{S}$ if all \( e_1, \cdots, e_n \) satisfy the property \( P \), then \( e \) also satisfies the property \( P \). This results in an inductive reasoning scheme built on the inference tree of \( e \). In the case of evaluation trees for the expressions in \( \mathbf{Exp}_2 \), this enables us to reason by induction over the computations carried out during the evaluation.

Using this technique, and taking \( \mathbb{S}_{\mathbf{Exp}_2} \) to be the inference system defining the big-step operational semantics of the expressions of \( \mathbf{Exp}_2 \), proposition 3.1 can be proved by considering the following equivalent formulation:

\[
\forall \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \in \mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbf{Exp}_2}} \quad \underbrace{(\forall v' \in \mathbb{V} \text{ Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v' \in \mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbf{Exp}_2}} \Rightarrow v = v')}_{P(\text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v)} \quad \text{[3.7]}
\]

PROOF. Let \( P \) be the property to prove and let us show that this property is satisfied by all evaluation trees \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \in \mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbf{Exp}_2}} \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_1 \), then \( e = v \) is a constant in \( \mathbf{K} \), and since \( F_1 \) is the only rule that can be used to evaluate a constant, \( v = v' \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_2 \), then \( e = \text{op } e_1 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1 \), \( v_1 \in \mathbf{V} \) and \( v = \text{op}_\mathbf{V} v_1 \). By induction hypothesis, if \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v'_1 \), then \( v'_1 = v_1 \in \mathbf{V} \) and since \( F_2 \) is the only applicable rule for evaluating \( e \), we obtain \( v' = \text{op}_\mathbf{V} v'_1 = \text{op}_\mathbf{V} v_1 = v \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_3 \), then \( e = \text{op } e_1 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1 \), \( v_1 \in \mathbf{E} \) and \( v = v_1 \). By induction hypothesis, if \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v'_1 \), then \( v'_1 = v_1 \in \mathbf{E} \). \( F_3 \) is the only applicable rule for evaluating \( e \), and we obtain \( v' = v'_1 = v_1 = v \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_4 \), then \( e = e_1 \text{ op } e_2 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_2 \rightsquigarrow v_2 \), \( v_1, v_2 \in \mathbf{V} \) and \( v = v_1 \text{ op}_\mathbf{V} v_2 \). By induction hypothesis, if \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v'_1 \), then \( v'_1 = v_1 \in \mathbf{V} \) and if \( \text{Env} \vdash e_2 \rightsquigarrow v'_2 \), then \( v'_2 = v_2 \in \mathbf{V} \). Rule \( F_4 \) is the only applicable rule for evaluating \( e \) and we obtain \( v' = v'_1 \text{ op}_\mathbf{V} v'_2 = v_1 \text{ op}_\mathbf{V} v_2 = v \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_5 \), then \( e = e_1 \text{ op } e_2 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1 \), \( v_1 \in \mathbf{I} \) and \( v = v_1 \). By induction hypothesis, if \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v'_1 \), then \( v'_1 = v_1 \in \mathbf{I} \). Rule \( F_5 \) is the only applicable rule for evaluating \( e \) and we obtain \( v' = v'_1 = v_1 = v \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_6 \), then \( e = e_1 \text{ op } e_2 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_2 \rightsquigarrow v_2 \), \( v_1 \in \mathbf{V} \), \( v_2 \in \mathbf{I} \) and \( v = v_2 \). By induction hypothesis, if \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v'_1 \), then \( v'_1 = v_1 \in \mathbf{V} \) and if \( \text{Env} \vdash e_2 \rightsquigarrow v'_2 \), then \( v'_2 = v_2 \in \mathbf{I} \). Rule \( F_6 \) is the only applicable rule for evaluating \( e \) and we obtain \( v' = v'_2 = v_2 = v \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_7 \) (respectively, \( F_8 \)), then \( e \) is the conditional expression if \( e_1 \) then \( e_2 \) else \( e_3 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow \text{true} \) (respectively, \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow \text{false} \)) and \( \text{Env} \vdash e_2 \rightsquigarrow v \) (respectively, \( \text{Env} \vdash e_3 \rightsquigarrow v \)). By induction hypothesis, if:

\[
\text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1' \quad \text{then} \quad v_1' = \text{true} \quad (\text{respectively } v_1' = \text{false})
\]

and if:

\[
\text{Env} \vdash e_2 \rightsquigarrow v' \quad (\text{respectively, } \text{Env} \vdash e_3 \rightsquigarrow v') \quad \text{then} \quad v' = v
\]

Rule \( F_7 \) (respectively, \( F_8 \)) is the only applicable rule for evaluating \( e \) and we obtain \( v' = v \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_9 \) (respectively, \( F_{10} \)), then \( e = \text{if } e_1 \text{ then } e_2 \text{ else } e_3 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1 \), \( v_1 \in \mathbf{V} \backslash \mathbf{IB} \) (respectively, \( v_1 \in \mathbf{IE} \)) and \( v = \text{ErrT} \) (respectively, \( v = v_1 \)). By induction hypothesis, if \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1' \), then \( v_1' = v_1 \). Rule \( F_9 \) (respectively, \( F_{10} \)) is the only applicable rule for evaluating \( e \) and we obtain \( v' = v \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_{11} \) (respectively, \( F_{12} \)), then \( e = x \) is a variable in \( \mathbf{X} \) such that \( x \in \text{dom}(\text{Env}) \) (respectively, \( x \notin \text{dom}(\text{Env}) \)) and \( v = \text{Env}(x) \) (respectively, \( v = \text{ErrU} \)). Rule \( F_{11} \) (respectively, \( F_{12} \)) is the only applicable rule for evaluating \( e \) and we obtain \( v' = v \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_{13} \), then \( e = \text{let } x = e_1 \text{ in } e_2 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1 \), \( (x, v_1) \oplus \text{Env} \vdash e_2 \rightsquigarrow v_2 \), \( v_1 \in \mathbf{V} \) and \( v = v_2 \). By induction hypothesis, if \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1' \), then \( v_1' = v_1 \in \mathbf{V} \) and if \( (x, v_1) \oplus \text{Env} \vdash e_2 \rightsquigarrow v_2' \), then \( v_2' = v_2 \). Rule \( F_{13} \) is the only applicable rule for evaluating \( e \), and since \( v_1' = v_1 \), then \( (x, v_1') \oplus \text{Env} = (x, v_1) \oplus \text{Env} \) and we obtain \( v' = v_2' = v_2 = v \).

If \( \text{Env} \vdash e \rightsquigarrow v \) has been obtained using rule \( F_{14} \), then \( e = \text{let } x = e_1 \text{ in } e_2 \), \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1 \), \( v_1 \in \mathbf{IE} \) and \( v = v_1 \). By induction hypothesis, if \( \text{Env} \vdash e_1 \rightsquigarrow v_1' \), then \( v_1' = v_1 \in \mathbf{IE} \) and \( F_{14} \) is the only applicable rule for evaluating \( e \) and we obtain \( v' = v_1' = v_1 = v \).
