## £C Payment collection

> The Times of 14 October 1844 reported that the building committee of the Reform Club 'repudiated the claim of Mr Barry [the architect] for his professional charge ... contenting themselves with the comfortable reflection that he ought to be satisfied with the payment of a little more than one-third of the usual professional charge ... A legal tribunal [made an] award to Mr Barry of his full amount of his well-earned claim.'

### Enforcement

Most clients are honourable and pay their designers within a reasonable time. However, some clients may attempt to evade their contractual obligations. As most design practices tend to have relatively few clients at any given time with each paying a high proportion of the practice's turnover, this can be a serious threat to a practice, leading in the most extreme cases to the designer's insolvency. However, there are a number of steps that the designer can take to safeguard his/her position:

### Safeguards

First, the designer needs to take several protective measures before the project has commenced:

- Carry out financial checks that a prospective client can afford the designer's services. The designer's accountant or bank or a credit reference agency may be able to check the client's credit record. Repeat such checks at intervals, particularly when the designer is about to embark on a significant quantum of work.
- Ask colleague practitioners if the prospective client has a reputation for being difficult.
- Negotiate, agree and sign ID/10 (see 'p3 How to use ID/10', page 33) or similar form of appointment.
- If the client is a consumer, explain and expressly agree certain clauses (see the 'negotiated clauses' section in 'p3 How to use ID/10', page 33) before entering into the contract. The designer should write to the client soon after, confirming that he/she had explained these clauses and that the client agreed to them (see 'p3 Model appointment letter to consumer client', page 38).
- Specify in the Schedule of Fees and Expenses that charges will be billed monthly unless the agreement provides that the designer will collect payment in advance of carrying out work on the project.
- Be constantly aware that he/she could be the defendant in an adjudication. The designer's file should always be kept up to date so as to be ready for an adjudication 'ambush'.

### Bill frequently

Second, the designer should ensure that he/she always bills promptly each month and chases accounts if they are not paid soon after. It is important not to have a large sum outstanding because:

- it reduces the practice's available working capital
- it increases the amount of outstanding sums subject to the risk of non-payment
- large sums due become more tempting for a malevolent client to attempt to evade. This often occurs at the end of a job when the client believes he/she has no further need for the designer.

### Warning signs

Third, the designer should be alert to warning signs that the client might not pay if he/she:

- claims that he/she is not happy about something and repeatedly demands changes to work done
- alleges faults in the designer's work
- requests work to be finished before paying
- alleges overcharging
- persistently queries invoice details or amounts or suddenly requests an itemised breakdown of a payment application
- pays late, particularly if uncharacteristic of the client
- sends correspondence that ignores outstanding fees
- objects to previously agreed terms.

> 'What we anticipate seldom occurs; what we least expect generally happens.' Benjamin Disraeli.

When any of these happens, the designer should consider immediately stopping work until this matter is satisfactorily resolved and the client pays all outstanding sums.

### Initial response

When payment is late, the designer should:

- first send a reminder (see ‘£C Specimen payment reminder’, page 264 and model letters, page 263 and 265) and perhaps even a second reminder
- then personally telephone the client to see why he/she is not paying. It is best not to leave this task to a colleague if the designer knows the client personally
- if it appears that the client is genuinely impecunious, the designer can suggest that the client pays in stages by post-dated cheque(s), e.g. half now and half in, say, three months’ time. It is better to obtain a drip feed of payment than achieve the Pyrrhic victory of obtaining a judgment and then being unable to collect payment
- if payment is still not forthcoming, raise the matter with the client and attempt to learn the cause of any genuine concern he/she might have
- if the client criticises the designer’s performance (essentially alleging negligence) try to resolve the issue immediately. The designer should first take a dispassionate look at his/her performance. It is important to be reasonable (but not harshly) self-critical. It is often very useful to take advice from a trusted colleague, as the designer can often be too close to the problem. The designer should do everything to avoid letting ego and pride get in the way of dealing reasonably with the matter. If, on reflection, the designer feels that his/her service might not have been to a reasonable standard, then the matters should be remedied without complaint
- if negligence is alleged, immediately notify his/her professional indemnity insurers (see ‘£C Model letter to professional indemnity insurers’, page 268). Most policies have a term which renders the insurance invalid if the designer does not notify the insurers within a relatively short time of any occurrence that might give rise to a claim under the policy. It is important to ensure that the policy is always available as it can be the principal barrier between the designer and insolvency if a negligence claim succeeds
- consider serving a statutory demand for a debt over £750, either personally by hand or using a process server. This allows the designer to apply to the court after 21 days for a bankruptcy order in the case of an individual, or a winding up order in the case of a company. In the latter case, it is often useful to send the order to a senior director. However, the designer has to be able to prove that the work has been done to the agreed standard and therefore a claim of negligence could successfully defeat this application with the designer paying the costs of both parties.
