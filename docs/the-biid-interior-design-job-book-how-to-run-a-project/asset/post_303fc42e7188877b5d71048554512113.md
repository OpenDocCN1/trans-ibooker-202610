## Validation

'Third-party validation' in the form of articles in professional journals, favourable reviews, letters of recommendation from past clients etc. are often very useful in convincing a client that the practice is suitable for the project they have in mind.

## Website

Practices will also wish to have a website, designed by a professional website designer. This is probably the first point of contact for many potential clients and needs to make a favourable impact within a few seconds. It should be well designed, easy to navigate and regularly updated.

## Project team

If the proposed project will require the services of other consultants, the practice may wish to indicate to the prospective client that it can form a capable team that can carry out the project in a co-ordinated way. In these cases, it may be useful for the proposed design team to make a combined submission to the client, perhaps indicating other projects where members of the team have worked together to satisfactorily deliver a job. Clients like certainty, and therefore it is often useful to indicate who is the lead consultant who will interface with the client.

## Clients

Designers should always remember that clients are often ignorant of matters that the designer deals with on a daily basis and thus believes are universally apparent. The designer should never assume that a potential client understands even the most elementary design matters unless the client is clearly very experienced.

Inexperienced clients often have preconceived ideas of how interior designers go about their work and what services they offer. Television property makeover programmes have distorted the public’s perception of this. It is therefore often useful for the designer to indicate to the client, at this early stage, the general basis on which it carries out its business, the services it will provide and the terms under which it will do so.

Reference to the proposed form of appointment, such as ID/10, is also useful to ensure that the client is aware at the outset what the general contractual relationship will be between the designer and the client.

## Professional tasks

Most importantly, the designer acting in a professional capacity should not indicate that the practice can deliver the project within a particular budget or timescale. At this stage the client’s requirements (the brief) are generally not known in detail and if an independent contractor is ultimately employed, these matters are mostly outside the designer’s control.

## Commercial tasks

If, however, the designer is to act as a contractor, then when the brief and design have been agreed in detail, the designer can contract with the client to deliver the built project to a time and a cost. The designer should remember that this aspect of its work is considered a commercial, not a professional, risk and so may not be covered by the designer’s professional indemnity insurance policy.

## Terms

When the client wishes to know the terms on which the designer is prepared to carry out the services required, for example when the client is seeking competitive fee tenders, steps covered in the following section, ‘P3 Pre-appointment’, should also be followed in making a submission.

## P2 Fee proposals

### Fee quotations

Clients often ask designers by way of a Request for Proposal (RFP) to submit quotations for fees in competition with other practices, in the mistaken belief that this will achieve the best value for money. The designer might attempt to explain to the client that professional services should be competitive on quality, not price (would they choose a surgeon only because he/she charged the lowest fee?) but sometimes it is unavoidable. EU rules require that public work over a certain value be competitively tendered and some commercial clients follow this rule as well.

### Fee calculation

In order to submit a realistic quotation for a lump sum or percentage fee, a practice must have a good idea of what projects cost to design, based on careful time records of previous projects, adjusted for inflation, relative size and complexity. This then should be applied to the project in question, planning in detail how the project will proceed and the specific staff who would be assigned to it. Tasks difficult to estimate, e.g. negotiating a planning consent, should be omitted from the quotation if at all possible and made the subject of time charges.

In difficult economic periods, there can be great pressure on a practice to win a job at all costs (with some practices having been known to submit nil fee bids in order to secure possible future work from large clients) but in doing so there is a great risk of:

- under-resourcing a job to a level that it cannot be carried out to an adequate standard, leading to a dissatisfied client and even a claim for negligence
- reducing the cashflow of the practice to a point where the financial viability of the practice is in jeopardy.

### Negligence

Designers must carry out work on all projects to a reasonable level of competence whatever the level of fee. It is no defence to a negligence claim to argue that the agreed fee was, in retrospect, inadequate.

When fees are squeezed due to competition, it is particularly important to make contractual provision for, and charge for, work additional to that originally requested. It is important to immediately notify clients when circumstances arise (whether due to unforeseen circumstances or client changes) which cause the designer unforeseen expense (see ‘b Notes on client’s instruction’, page 138) which he/she will be charging to the client.

Designers should remember that it is generally better to lose a job if an adequate fee for it cannot be secured than to ‘buy’ a project and live with the financial consequences for the many months or years that the project takes to carry out.
