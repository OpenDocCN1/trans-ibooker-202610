## P1 Notes on emails

### Emails

Emails are now so commonly used as to have become almost the default method of written communication. They have the advantage of immediacy but herein lies a danger. There is a risk that such communications will not be as carefully considered as they should be, bearing in mind that they are part of a professional service.

Just as with a letter, an email should not only transmit its message but also serve as a self-explanatory record for future reference, possibly at a much later date and by people unfamiliar with the project.

It is therefore useful to set it out as if it were a letter, complete with the names and addresses of the sender and the recipient, the project file number, the subject, etc. The text of the letter should be written in complete sentences, and spelling should be checked. Any attachments should be listed by name at the foot of the email as they often are lost when emails are copied or forwarded.

Designers should resist the impulse to immediately hit the ‘send’ button after an email is drafted and should give it the same careful consideration as they would a letter.

### Form of address

It is generally thought that formality should be maintained in correspondence regardless of whom is being addressed (except perhaps the client if the designer is on friendly terms with him/her). Addressing non-professionals, e.g. suppliers, contractors, manufacturers, by their first names in correspondence could indicate that the designer’s relationship with such people is not entirely at arm’s length and so should be avoided.

## P1 Notes on discussion record

### Discussion record

The pertinent details of all informal meetings and telephone conversations should be carefully noted in a discussion record (sometimes called an ‘attendance note’), at the time they happen, and placed in the job file. As with letters, they should not be cryptic but understandable to any third party reading it at some time in the future. Formal meetings involving several participants are best minuted (see ‘k Specimen meeting minutes’, page 213).

The principal reason for writing such a record is to remind the designer (and his/her staff) of key discussions relating to the job but also as a record of matters affecting the designer’s work. It can also serve as a reminder of actions to be taken.

> It is usually too late to write a discussion record when the designer is being sued for negligence.

### Use

Discussion records are written evidence that could be used in the event of a dispute with the client as to contract terms, and may be important in later explaining the designer’s performance. Discussion records written well after the event, based on the designer’s memory, should be recorded as such on the note but are less satisfactory as evidence.

### Client’s instructions

Instructions given by the client in discussions should be confirmed immediately in a client’s instruction (see ‘d Specimen client’s instruction’, page 140). Similarly, verbal instructions given by the designer to a contractor should be confirmed immediately in a designer’s instruction (see ‘k Specimen designer’s instruction’, page 215).

## P1 Notes on document issue

### Document issue

All documents issued externally should be accompanied by a documents issue form to allow the recipient to confirm that he/she has received all the documents sent and as a record in the project file. Copies of the documents sent should also be included in the file.

Designers often think it unnecessary to file copies of draft documents as they will be filed when completed. This is a mistake as it may be important at a later date to establish the state of the document at the time of issue.

Extra pages can be added to the document issue form as necessary.

## P1 Notes on print register

### Print register

The printing of drawings often makes up the major portion of recoverable expenses charged to a client and so it is useful to collect up all printing on projects on a print register, whether the prints are made in-house or by a service bureau, for easy reference when billing the client.

Most designers charge in-house printing at rates comparable with current service bureau rates. For large practices this can sometimes justify the lease or purchase of dedicated printers.

## P1 Notes on time sheets

### Time sheets

Time sheets should be kept by all members of the practice (including directors/partners) working on projects. They can be used to form the basis of charges to a client, for profit analysis, as a basis for calculating fee proposals for future similar jobs and as a basis for staff pay.

Time should be shown to the nearest half hour, and the tasks carried out in this time recorded. Microsoft Excel can provide a convenient method of doing this, and tasks can be shown as notes to any cell by using the ‘reviewing’ function.

It is important that these sheets are filled out contemporaneously (at least on a daily basis) as the case of Munkenbeck v Harold illustrates (see ‘Applicable case law’, page 276). Adjudicators and the courts are not prepared to accept as evidence time sheets that appear to have been reconstructed after the event and that do not schedule the tasks carried out on each day.

Hours spent on projects that are charged on a time basis are collected up each month, multiplied by the relevant hourly rates and included in the next client invoice.
