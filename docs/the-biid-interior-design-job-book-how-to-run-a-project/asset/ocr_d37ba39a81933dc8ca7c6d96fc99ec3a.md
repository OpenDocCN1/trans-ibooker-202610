# # P3 Risk avoidance

There are a number of pitfalls that await the unwary designer as he/she carries out the tasks necessary for any project. Avoiding them is key to having a successful practice. Whilst it is impossible to predict every trap, observing the following rules will decrease the chances of the designer getting into trouble.

# Appointment

Many problems can be avoided if the designer takes care at appointment stage to do the following:

+   - Check prospective clients before accepting a commission (see ‘fC Payment collection; Safeguards’, page 254).
+   - Attempt to obtain a detailed brief from the client. If the client is not clear about his/her detailed requirements, consider commencing the project at work stage A or B.
+   - When proposing on a percentage, or particularly a lump sum, fee basis, ensure that there is a clear brief. If not, do not hesitate to clarify any ill-defined matters with the prospective client.
+   - Attempt to ensure that there is a reasonable budget, although some clients are (unwisely) reluctant to set a budget at the beginning of a project.
+   - Ensure that there is provision in the form of appointment for additional payment for additional work carried out by the designer (e.g. ID/10 clause 5.8).
+   - Ensure that adequate information about the premises involved is supplied to the designer or that the client understands that he/she will have to arrange for it to be prepared (except on the simplest of jobs where the designer might be prepared to carry out a small survey). See ‘c Notes on letter to client re surveys’, page 104.
+   - Explain the proposed role of the designer to the client, particularly if the designer is to act as contractor and/or retailer.
+   - Avoid bespoke forms of appointment as they are almost always prejudicial to the designer. Legal advice should be sought to understand the implications of any unfamiliar terms proposed. In particular look carefully at the ‘duty of care’ clause, and if it is different from clause 2.1 of ID/10 (where the key words are: ‘shall exercise reasonable skill, care and diligence in accordance with the normal standards of the designer’s profession’), ensure that professional indemnity insurers are happy with the revised wording.
+   - Avoid committing to a budget or a timetable when acting as a designer. When acting as contractor, only commit to these after the design has been finalised and agreed with the client.
+   - Never contract to provide services for which he/she is not qualified.
+   - Do not employ consultants directly and ensure that the responsibilities of all consultants are clearly defined.
+   - Do not start work until the form of appointment is agreed and signed by the client. Those expected the part(ies) who actually sign the agreement are those expected; beware a sudden change of name. If two people are involved, e.g. husband and wife, business partners etc. ensure that both sign the form. This will prevent the all too common ploy of one party later claiming that the other had no authority to incur fees.
