# £E Giving evidence in a hearing

> > 'Every time you take any action, imagine how you would explain it to a hostile barrister.' Raymond Cecil RIBA.

# Legal action

Sometimes even the most careful designer will become involved in a legal action, perhaps in support of a claim for fees due or in defence of a negligence claim. It is at this point that a carefully kept project file containing correspondence, records of all meetings, telephone conversations, decisions taken, time sheets and all other aspects of a job becomes of vital importance.

In preliminary negotiations between the parties’ solicitors, a thoroughly evidenced case can help convince the other side’s solicitors that the designer will prevail and may well influence them to recommend that their client settle on terms favourable to the designer.

If the case does go to a hearing, contemporaneous written records (see ‘r1 Specimen discussion record, page 9) are often accepted as compelling evidence by a court. However, records that have clearly been created well after the event that they purport to record can prejudice a case. (See Applicable case law: Munkenbeck v Harold, page 282), in which time sheets, which were evidently prepared well after the time in question, were rejected by the judge as a basis on which an architect could recover the cost of time spent.)

# Preparation

If a designer has to give evidence in a hearing before an adjudicator, arbitrator or judge, it is crucial to prepare carefully. Copies should be made of all evidence that bears on matters relevant to the case and kept in a separate case file.

The designer should make him/herself familiar with this file and with the principles upon which his/her legal team is prosecuting or defending the case. It is worthwhile spending sufficient time to do this as, under ID/10 clause 5.22, the designer will be reimbursed for this time provided his/her legal action is successful or the action (or part of it) is abandoned by the client.

# Witness statement

Well before the hearing, the designer will be asked to sign a witness statement, which will include a declaration that the designer believes the statement to be true. It is very important that such a statement drafted by a solicitor in the designer’s own words but without using jargon. The designer should read the final draft carefully and ensure that it contains no errors whatsoever before signing it.

There is nothing more harmful to a witness’s credibility or detrimental to the chances of a case succeeding than a hostile barrister demonstrating that statements made by a witness are untrue, perhaps because of the evidence of others or even inconsistencies in the witness’s own evidence in the hearing. Anyone doubting this should read the judgment in Fitzroy Robinson v Mentmore Towers (page 282).

# Hearing

When giving evidence in a hearing, the designer will first be asked to give his/her full name, professional address, professional qualifications, how long in practice and any special expertise the designer might claim. It is important not to exaggerate the latter as an expert might be required to meet a higher standard of care than an ordinary practitioner.

# Likely questions

The designer should attempt to discover from his/her legal team what questions are likely to be asked as giving evidence on the witness stand can make the witness feel very exposed.
