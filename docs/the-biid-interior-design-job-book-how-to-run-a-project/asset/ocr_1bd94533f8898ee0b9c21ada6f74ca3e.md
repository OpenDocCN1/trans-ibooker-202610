# Validation

'Third-party validation' in the form of articles in professional journals, favourable reviews, letters of recommendation from past clients etc. are often very useful in convincing a client that the practice is suitable for the project they have in mind.

# Website

Practices will also wish to have a website, designed by a professional website designer. This is probably the first point of contact for many potential clients and needs to make a favourable impact within a few seconds. It should be well designed, easy to navigate and regularly updated.

# Project team

If the proposed project will require the services of other consultants, the practice may wish to indicate to the prospective client that it can form a capable team that can carry out the project in a co-ordinated way. In these cases, it may be useful for the proposed design team to make a combined submission to the client, perhaps indicating other projects where members of the team have worked together to satisfactorily deliver a job. Clients like certainty, and therefore it is often useful to indicate who is the lead consultant who will interface with the client.

# Clients

Designers should always remember that clients are often ignorant of matters that the designer deals with on a daily basis and thus believes are universally apparent. The designer should never assume that a potential client understands even the most elementary design matters unless the client is clearly very experienced.

Inexperienced clients often have preconceived ideas of how interior designers go about their work and what services they offer. Television property makeover programmes have distorted the public’s perception of this. It is therefore often useful for the designer to indicate to the client, at this early stage, the general basis on which it carries out its business, the services it will provide and the terms under which it will do so.

Reference to the proposed form of appointment, such as ID/10, is also useful to ensure that the client is aware at the outset what the general contractual relationship will be between the designer and the client.

# Professional tasks

Most importantly, the designer acting in a professional capacity should not indicate that the practice can deliver the project within a particular budget or timescale. At this stage the client’s requirements (the brief) are generally not known in detail and if an independent contractor is ultimately employed, these matters are mostly outside the designer’s control.

# Commercial tasks

If, however, the designer is to act as a contractor, then when the brief and design have been agreed in detail, the designer can contract with the client to deliver the built project to a time and a cost. The designer should remember that this aspect of its work is considered a commercial, not a professional, risk and so may not be covered by the designer’s professional indemnity insurance policy.
