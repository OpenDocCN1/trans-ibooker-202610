
## Payment collection (continued)

### Take stock

If the above fails, the designer should take stock of the matter. He/she should understand that going further will almost inevitably destroy whatever relationship exists between the designer and the client. Most designers believe that a client who does not pay is not worth having but there can be other considerations, e.g. the negative effect that pursuing a fee claim can have on other interconnected personal or business relationships. The designer should also balance the sums due against the risk of incurring unrecoverable costs. It is sometimes best to abandon small sums due, before proceeding too far.

If the designer wishes to pursue the matter, he/she should first think carefully and ensure that he/she has all the relevant facts. He/she should evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of his/her position, taking legal advice if necessary.

### Communications

It is important to always:

- keep communication simple and factual. Remember that the client and his/her solicitor are not experienced in the designer's field, and technical matters and procedures may need explaining
- act even-handedly and not make threats nor respond in kind to threats from the client and/or his/her solicitors
- avoid communicating in an aggressive or intimidating manner, particularly if the client is a consumer. 'More in sorrow than in anger' is a good rule to observe
- not say or write anything that could be remotely construed as defamatory
- keep to provable facts, referring as far as possible to written evidence
- never give any indication that the unpaid sum will affect the designer's solvency. Such comments will only encourage a malevolent client
- avoid emotion. The designer should avoid discussing the matter on the telephone or in meetings unless certain that he/she can speak dispassionately.

### Forceful response

Having borne the above in mind, if the designer wishes to continue to pursue payment he/she should, after taking legal advice, draft a letter to the client noting the following points:

- Both the designer and the client are bound by the terms of their agreement, ID/10. If the client is a consumer, note that certain terms were explained to the client before he/she entered into the agreement and enclose a copy of the earlier letter confirming this (see 'P3 Model appointment letter to consumer client', page 38).
- List the client's criticisms of the designer's services and against each state the designer's precise and detailed reply, referring to supporting written material in the project file. It is often quite useful to discuss this response with a specialist solicitor.
- Under clause 5.20 of ID/10, the client is not permitted to set off alleged claims of negligence against sums due. The client is free to pursue such a claim but only as separate matter. In the meantime, he/she must pay the sum due.
- Under clause 8.1.2(b) of ID/10, the designer may suspend the services unless payment due is received within 14 days.
- Under clause 5.22 of ID/10, costs of the successful party in a dispute, including all of that party's time spent in recovering any amounts due, will be collected from the unsuccessful party. Therefore the time that the designer has incurred in writing this letter will be charged to the client.
- Under clause 5.21 of ID/10, interest on the unpaid sums after 14 days will be charged to the client at the rate of 8% over base, provided for in the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998. Enclose a further payment application showing all of the above additional charges.
- If full payment is not received within 14 days, the designer will place the matter in the hands of the firm's solicitor with a view to taking the matter to adjudication. (This will discourage the client from imagining that the high costs of litigation or arbitration will inhibit a designer who has been impoverished by non-payment.)

This letter must be self-explanatory and respond as far as possible to all the client's criticisms. The client will probably show this letter to his/her solicitor, and often clients explain only their own position. It is important that, through this letter, the client's solicitor is made aware of the designer's views on the matter. In this sense, the client's solicitor, if competent, is the designer's ally as he/she should point out to the client the strength of the designer's case. This may be the first time that the client has been given an independent assessment of the designer's position.

In addition, it is often useful to enclose a second letter, headed 'without prejudice save as to costs', offering to accept within a short period (say, seven days) a small reduction in the sums due, to settle the matter swiftly. This often provides an incentive for the client to cut his/her losses and dispose of the matter.

The designer would be wise to consider very carefully any letter he/she proposes to send to the client, perhaps reconsidering its tone and wording after he/she has slept on it. The designer should show the letter to his/her solicitor before sending it. Emails should be avoided as they are too immediate.

Even if no negligence has been alleged, the designer should inform his/her insurers, citing any legal advice received as to the likelihood of success. Fee disputes often induce clients to raise spurious claims of negligence which the insurers will wish to deal with.

### Adjudication

If the above is unsuccessful, the designer should take further legal advice and, subject to that advice, proceed to adjudication. The designer should note that, under clause 9.2.2 of ID/10, the adjudicator has the power to award costs to the successful party and that clause 5.22 provides for the costs to be recovered of any claim which is successfully defended or abandoned.

The advantages of adjudication are that it is relatively inexpensive and also fairly quick: the adjudicator has to make a decision within 28 or, if an extension is agreed by the parties, 42 days.

### CFA

If the designer is forced to contemplate employing a solicitor to enforce payment, it may be useful to shop around to find specialist lawyers who will enter into a Conditional Fee Agreement (CFA). Under this arrangement, the solicitor (and sometimes any barrister involved) will not charge if the legal action is unsuccessful. If it is successful, then the lawyer(s) are allowed to add an 'uplift', an increase in their fees of up to 100%. This has several advantages:

- If the action is unsuccessful, the designer does not have to pay his/her lawyers.
- By gauging the solicitors' response to the question of using a CFA, the designer can estimate the true strength of his/her case.
- The solicitor is required to disclose to the other side that a CFA is in place. This serves to warn the client and his/her solicitors that:
  - the designer's lawyers are so confident that the designer's case is so good that they are prepared to stake their fees on it
  - the client has more to lose if he/she is unsuccessful and will have to pay up to double the indemnified costs of the designer (plus, of course, the client's own costs as well)
  - the designer will not be constrained by legal costs.

#### Disadvantages

However, designers should be aware of several considerations when using CFAs:

- Most CFAs provide that the complainant (in this case the designer) is ultimately responsible for paying his/her solicitor both the legal fee and the uplift if the designer's case is successful but the client cannot pay. It is important therefore for the designer to check the client's solvency before taking any action. Designers may believe that the client's property that was the subject of the design project could represent an asset of the client but such assets can be heavily mortgaged or in different ownership.
- Before proceeding with the case, the designer should ascertain the legal fees position if the case is settled on a compromised basis.
- If the designer is unsuccessful, he/she will be responsible for paying the client's legal costs. However, this contingency can sometimes be covered by an After the Event (ATE) insurance policy.

If the designer is contemplating a CFA, it is essential to obtain legal advice from an experienced specialist construction law solicitor before proceeding. If the solicitor is convinced that the case has good prospects of success, often a barrister and an ATE insurer will rely on this and also agree to become involved on a conditional basis.

#### ATE insurance

This type of policy insures against having to pay the other side's legal costs in case the designer loses his/her case. Some policies even allow the cost of the premium for this insurance to be covered under the policy as well and thus payable only if the case is successful. In order to obtain ATE insurance, the designer's solicitors will have to provide insurers with supporting evidence of the case's likelihood of success. Having obtained such insurance provides a strong incentive for the client to settle because the designer is effectively risking nothing by pursuing the case.

#### Jackson Report

In January 2010 Lord Justice Jackson recommended that ATE premiums and success fees should not be recoverable from defendants. The designer should therefore confirm the current position with his/her solicitor.
