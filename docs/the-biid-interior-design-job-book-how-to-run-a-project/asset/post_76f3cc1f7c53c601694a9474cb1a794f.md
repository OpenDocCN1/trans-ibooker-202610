
## K Notes on site visit report

### Frequency

The designer should visit the site at intervals appropriate to the work being carried out at the time and when requested by the contractor. Generally, the interval between site visits is about fortnightly but this can vary greatly.

The designer will have indicated to the client (see the model appointment letters on pages 37-40) the number of site visits he/she estimated when proposing for the project. The designer should not hesitate to carry out more site visits if required by the progress of the works but, if so, should advise the client when the estimated number of site visits allowed for in the appointment is about to be exceeded.

### Inspecting

When inspecting the works the designer should:

- revisit the contractor's programme and prepare a list of the items of work to be inspected that should have been constructed since the last visit
- wear a hard hat, reflective jacket and protective footwear
- compare what has been built with what had been drawn and specified
- not accept any materials or workmanship that are not in compliance with the contract. The designer should require that all unacceptable materials be removed from the site immediately
- not accept any variations proposed by the contractor unless they would improve the design. Most such suggestions are made to save the contractor money
- answer contractor's queries only after returning to the office and researching all relevant information.

### Matters recorded

It is important to record all important matters arising during the visit, particularly those relating to the quality of the work, its progress compared with the contractor's programme and any necessary actions to be taken.

Site visit reports should show:

- the date and time of the visit
- who attended and from what organisation
- any relevant observations on the quality of the works, particularly any defects noted
- the progress of the works compared with the contractor's programme
- actions to be taken.

It is important to write the report so that it can be understood by a third party who had not attended the meeting (e.g. the client) or was not involved in the project at the time, or who might read it well after the meeting has taken place. Additional pages can be added to the site visit report as necessary.

### Photographs

It is very useful to take a large number of digital photographs at each site visit as a record of the progress of the building works and to show details of the construction before it is covered up.

## K Notes on meeting minutes

### Minutes

All telephone conversations, discussions and meetings of any importance should be recorded by the designer, and the record kept on file. These can be in the form of a discussion record (see p1 ‘Specimen discussion record’, page 9) when the conversation is informal but when a meeting involves several participants it is best to formally minute it and send copies to all the participants and other interested parties, particularly the client.

### Matters recorded

Meeting minutes should show:

- the date and time of the meeting
- who attended and from what organisation
- any views or disagreements which might have been expressed
- what was resolved
- actions to be taken.

It is important to write the minutes so that they can be understood by a third party who had not attended the meeting or was not involved in the project at the time, or who might read it well after the meeting has taken place.

Additional pages can be added to the minutes as necessary.

## K Notes on designer's instruction

### Designer's instruction

The designer's instruction to the contractor is an important record of changes made to the project after the construction contract has been entered into. These changes can arise due to unforeseen contingencies or to the employer's change of mind (which are first recorded as a client's instruction – see ‘D Specimen client’s instruction’, page 140).

Designers' instructions often have an effect on the construction cost and programme and, before issuing one, the designer should ascertain these and advise the client.

As explained in the pre-construction letters to the contractor and client (see ‘J Model letter to contractor before construction’, page 198 and ‘J Model letter to client before construction’, page 200), only amendments to work instructed through this form should be taken into account when payment certificates are issued. This is to avoid confusion and to discourage the client from attempting to directly instruct the contractor.

### Additional fees

Additional design work occasioned by such instructions can be charged to the client under clause 5.8.1 of ID/10.
