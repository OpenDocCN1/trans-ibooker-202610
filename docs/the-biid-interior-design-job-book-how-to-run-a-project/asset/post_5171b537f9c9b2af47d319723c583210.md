## Acknowledgements

Following their involvement in the publication of the form of interior designer appointment, ID/10, the authors have frequently been asked to write a guide to its use. As the purpose of this form (as with any appointment agreement) is to define the relationship between client and consultant throughout the course of a project, it seemed that a more useful book would be a manual for running a job, from before the point when a designer contemplates making a proposal to a prospective client until after the job is completed.

Having taken this decision, the principal inspiration was the RIBA's Architect's Job Book, various editions of which have been a key reference work in the authors' practice since it was founded in 1974. The recently published eighth edition, thoroughly revised by Robert Dalziel and Nigel Ostime of 3Dreid, is particularly useful. However, in the authors' opinion, the book is better suited to larger projects, and of course, it is written for architects, not interior designers.

This book therefore hopes to provide interior designers, who more often work on projects under £1m in value, with detailed information on best practice for jobs that are typical in their profession.

David Chappell's Standard Letters in Architectural Practice was also an inspiration although again not strictly applicable as it too is written for architects. However, for designers who frequently manage larger projects, it is very useful.

The authors would like to thank Peter Sharp and Jill Gough of Aon insurers who contributed to the notes and letter to professional indemnity insurers, Chris Westhead, adjudicator, the Rt Hon. Lord Justice Mark Potter QC who advised on giving evidence in a hearing, Dids Macdonald of ACID who advised on copyright and John Gelder of NBS who contributed to the notes on specifications.

The authors particularly wish to thank Colin Jones, a partner specialising in construction law with Hewitsons solicitors in Cambridge, who kindly reviewed the text of this work and made many useful suggestions which we have incorporated.

The opinions expressed in this book are not necessarily those of the professional organisations who have kindly endorsed this book. Any errors of fact are those of the authors alone and they would welcome any corrections from readers.

## Preface

> 'Design is the transformation of existing conditions into preferred ones'
> Sciences of the Artificial, Herbert Simon (Nobel laureate).

The authors have long felt that British interior designers did not have the benefit of books that explained professional practice, whether in running a firm or in running a job. This book seeks to fill the need for the latter.

The practice of interior design, as with architecture, is mostly a thoroughly enjoyable vocation. Otherwise why would so many gifted and talented designers work long hours for which they are generally underpaid. However, the profession can sometimes be very cruel, as the risks of being in practice can be very great indeed for the unwary.

This book is not, unlike most books on interior design, about design, but about practice. By following longstanding procedures in the construction industry, designers will be communicating in the same terms as other members of a project team.

The first duty of a professional is to place the interests of his/her client before his/her own. By following best practice as illustrated in this book, designers will be doing this and at the same time safeguarding themselves against any suggestion of negligence or incompetence. The designer should always remember that he/she must expend their clients' resources with care and that these sums can often be considerable.
