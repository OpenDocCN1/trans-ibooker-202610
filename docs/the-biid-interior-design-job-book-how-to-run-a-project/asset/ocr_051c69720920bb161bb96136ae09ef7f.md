# £C Charging options

# PROFESSIONAL

There are a number of methods that a designer can use to calculate professional fees for design and installation management. Each has its advantages and disadvantages to both designer and client.

# Percentage

This is a traditional method, used by architects and designers for many years. Its advantages are that the amount of the fee bears some relationship to the amount of the design work carried out, particularly when the precise brief is not available at the beginning of the project. Experienced clients are often familiar with this method of fee calculation. Its disadvantages are that the amount of the final fee is not precisely predictable and that clients can sometimes suspect that the designer has an incentive to over-specify in order to drive his/her fee up.

It is important to indicate how interim fees are calculated, generally expressed as percentages of the agreed percentage fee, payable at the completion of each work stage (subject to interim monthly fees).

It is important with this method to have a mechanism to deal with the fair payment for design work when there is a reduction in brief during the course of the project, and ID/10 clause 5.7.2 covers this.

# Lump sum

This method of calculating fees is useful only when the precise nature of work including a detailed brief is known at the beginning. It gives the client some security that the fee will not exceed the lump sum but the designer, when calculating it, will have to allow an amount to cover contingencies.

As above, it is important to indicate how interim fees are calculated – generally expressed as percentages of the agreed lump sum, payable at the completion of each work stage (subject to interim monthly fees).

# Time charge

This method of calculating fees can be said to be the 'fairest' as the designer is paid just for work actually done. The client will probably be used to this method of charging by his/her accountant and/or solicitor. However, the client has no certainty what the final fee will be and can sometimes suspect that the designer has an incentive to spend more time on the project than it warrants.

Time charges are useful when the project has not been sufficiently defined to allow the calculation of the amount of work necessary to carry it out. At the beginning of an ill-defined project, the designer might charge on a time basis to define the scope of the project and the services required, and then charge for subsequent work on a percentage or lump sum basis.

The designer should avoid the imposition of a ceiling which is a one-way bet for the client. To assuage client concerns, the designer could agree to notify client when fees are approaching an agreed level. The submission of interim monthly fee accounts also keeps the client aware of how quickly fees are being incurred.

# Additional work

As projects almost always change and develop as they proceed, due to either unforeseen circumstances or client change, when the basis for fee calculation is either percentage or lump sum a method of calculating any additional design costs (usually a time charge) should be agreed. The designer should ensure that such a time charge rate is adequate and should not be shy in claiming it.
