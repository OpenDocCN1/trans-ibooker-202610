# P3 Pre-appointment stage

# Stage description

The preliminary stage covers the preparation and negotiating of
the designer’s terms of appointment. When a potential client is-
sues a Request for Proposal (RFP) for design services in respect of a
project, the designer should be prepared to submit the documents
shown in ‘P3 How to use ID/10’, page 33.

# Contract form

Professional bodies such as the BIID and the RIBA rightly
recommend that the agreement between the client and the
designer is recorded in writing and signed by both parties. In law, a
verbal agreement can still be treated as a form of contract but it is
highly unadvisable to agree to carry out professional services on
such a basis because:

+   - the wide range of terms necessary for the efficient running of a
+     project and the protection of both the parties will not be covered
+     in detail
+   - there is no objective evidence of the agreed terms (or even if they
+     were ever discussed).

Despite this, many designers fall into the trap of not agreeing a
written design contract and often end up in difficulty as to the
scope of their obligations or in enforcing fee collection as a result
(see ‘5C Payment collection; Safeguards’, page 254).

As discussed below (‘P3 Why use ID/10?’, page 31) the BIID
Agreement for Interior Design Services (ID/10) is now the
construction industry standard form of designer/client agreement
and provides a fair balance between the interests of clients and
designers.

Designers should not just refer to ID/10 in correspondence but
should send a copy to the prospective client unless the designer is
quite certain that the client is familiar with this form of contract,
perhaps from a previous project carried out by the designer. If the
client is a consumer then the designer should individually
negotiate certain clauses with him/her (see ‘P3 How to use ID/10;
Negotiated clauses’, page 33).

# Services and brief

It should never be assumed that a ‘normal’ interior design service
is to be performed. Inexperienced clients may not understand
what this involves and some clients may want a partial service.
Most jobs are prototypes. Therefore the designer should discover
from the client:

+   - his/her brief, as far as it is known at this stage
+   - the client's view of the interior design services necessary to
+     properly respond to this brief.

This is particularly important when competitive fee tenders are
being sought. In such cases, the designer’s fee quotation should
respond precisely to the client’s stated requirements in order to be
competitive.
