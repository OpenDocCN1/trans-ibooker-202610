## Applicable case law

The following precis of selected legal cases, which touch on some of the issues covered in this book, are not intended to be exhaustive but to note the views of the courts on certain key issues that affect professional practice:

**Schenkers** _Schenkers v Overland Shoes_ (1997, Court of Appeal 1998) held that a no set-off clause in a standard form of contract is enforceable provided that it can be demonstrated that it is reasonable and that it is in common use in the profession.

**Moores** _Moores v Yaleley_ (1998, Court of Appeal 2001) held that a professional can limit his/her liability by agreement with the client provided that the limit is fair and reasonable in relation to the size of the project at the time it is agreed.

**Picardi** _Picardi v Cunniberti_ (2002) held that when the client is a consumer, under the Unfair Terms in Consumer Contracts Regulations 1999 (utccr) certain clauses must be individually negotiated and agreed with the client before the appointment contract is entered into. With reference to ID/10 the following clauses must be individually negotiated:
- payment for services
- no set-off
- payment notices
- recovery of costs
- limit of liabilities
- net contribution
- third-party agreements
- adjudication and arbitration

In particular, under the standard adjudication arrangements a consumer client could be required to follow a procedure which would cause him/her irrecoverable expenditure, even if successful in the adjudication. Clause 9.2.2 of ID/10, which allows for costs to be awarded to the successful party, overcomes that objection provided the referring party confirms this provision to the other party and the adjudicator in writing after notice to adjudicate is given.

**Eisenberg** _Eisenberg v Bachar_ (2002) was settled before judgment could be given but it suggested that all commissions from suppliers to a designer, whether disclosed or not, are in law the property of the client, and the designer therefore has an obligation to account for these to the client. In order for the designer to have the right to keep any such commission, the client has to give his/her consent, knowing all the relevant facts.

**Munkenbeck** _Munkenbeck v Harold_ (2005) held that a clause similar to ID/10 clause 5.22, recovery of costs, which was not individually negotiated in this case, was unenforceable with a consumer client but that clause 5.21, late payment, was enforceable.

The judge said that he was not satisfied that the time charges claimed had been contemporaneously noted in time sheets. This emphasises the necessity of keeping a daily record of time spent on a job and a schedule of what tasks were performed each day (see 'PN Notes on time sheets', page 14).

**Merrett** _Merrett v Babb_ (2006) held that where an employer's professional indemnity insurance cover no longer applied to a business, an employee could be personally liable for any damages caused by his/her personal negligence. Clause 7.2.2 of ID/10 addresses this issue by prohibiting the client from holding liable any employee or principal of the designer.

**Domsalla** _Domsalla v Dyson_ (2007) held that adjudication is an acceptable means of dispute resolution, even when the client is a residential occupier, and that an adjudication provision in a building contract is not an unfair term under utccr.

It also established that the prohibition on withholding (set-off) under the Housing Grants, Construction & Regeneration Act 1996 (HGRA), unless the payer has issued a payment notice within five days (as provided for in clause 5.19 of ID/10), is unenforceable with consumer clients unless individually negotiated before entering into the appointment agreement.

Fitzroy Robinson v Mentmore Towers (2009) demonstrates how important it is for anyone submitting an affidavit and/or giving evidence in a hearing to be meticulously honest. In this case, the ceo of the architectural practice (which was seeking to collect over £1m in fees) was forced several times under hostile questioning to admit that his evidence was wrong.

The judge stated in his decision, '... the principal difficulty with [the ceo's] evidence concerned his witness statement. This was a document which had been so artfully spun – so deliberately designed to belittle [the client's witness] on all points, regardless of what had actually happened – that I was driven to conclude that it was a wholly unreliable document. I accept that there can be a regrettable tendency on the part of those preparing witness statements to accentuate the positive, but I have never before come across a witness statement which attempted to do so to such a gross extent that the document itself became evidentially worthless.'

Despite this, the architects succeeded in collecting over £500,000 of unpaid fees although the judge ruled that the architect's estimated £600,000 costs bill payable by the client had to be reduced by 25% in order to reflect his finding of fraudulent misrepresentation.

Fortunately for the reputation of the architectural profession, the ceo had in fact trained as an accountant, not an architect.
