## Design

As the design progresses, it is important:

- that the client is made aware of all the consents that will be required and that these consents are applied for and obtained at the appropriate stages (see various stage descriptions below)
- to ensure that the client carries out his/her duties under health and safety legislation (see 'f Notes on letter to client re CDM Regulations', page 164)
- not to rely on preliminary information provided by utilities; it can often be wrong
- that the client is consulted regularly and approves the developing design at each stage
- that the designer has the construction cost and timetable estimated at the end of each stage and obtains the client's approval before proceeding to the next stage
- that if the client instructs that the scheme is altered to reduce the budget and/or timetables, the designer explains fully any adverse effects that these cuts might have on the project
- that any innovative design or materials specified are brought to the attention of the client and any attendant risks are fully explained
- not to give advice on matters which the designer is not qualified to give (e.g. property values)
- to carefully check and co-ordinate consultants' information with the designer's drawings and specification
- to check the contract documents thoroughly before issuing them for construction (and to ensure that the fee has an adequate allowance for such a check). If possible, this should be carried out by someone who has not previously been involved in the job
- that the designer strongly advises the client against involving any friends as suppliers or contractors.

## Construction

Clients often believe that the designer warrants the quality of the construction, even when acting solely in a professional capacity. It is important for the designer to:

- explain to the client that it is not physically possible for the designer to inspect all the construction work. If the client requires detailed inspection, he/she should consider employing a site inspector (see 'H Notes on letter to client re site inspector', page 178)
- explain to the client his/her responsibilities regarding insurances, ensure both the client (where required) and the contractor have taken out the appropriate policies, and inspect the policies to confirm that they are adequate and will provide cover throughout the anticipated period of the building works
- explain to the client the procedures that should be observed during construction (see 'l Model letter to client before construction', page 200)
- require from the contractor at the beginning of construction a schedule of any further design information required and the latest dates for the delivery of this information before the building programme is delayed

## P3 Risk avoidance (continued)

- keep detailed written and photographic records of construction as it proceeds. The designer should carefully minute site meetings and not accept any written records by others relating to construction until they are checked and approved
- notify the client immediately in writing if a problem is discovered on site, how it is to be rectified and its likely implications on the cost and timetable. Confirm on the next site visit if it has been rectified
- not accept substitution of specified materials or defective construction. Interim valuations should not only omit the cost of defective work but also be further reduced to allow for the cost of removing it
- avoid giving instructions on site unless necessary. Confirm in writing any such instructions within 24 hours
- if a project is to be designed and inspected by separate practices, e.g. because the site is abroad, ensure that there is a clear division of responsibilities. The client should be advised that it is not possible to cover all points of detail in the design and pre-construction stages and that some minor design decisions will have to be taken on site by the inspecting designer
- avoid any suggestion that he/she has been influenced by the client in the issuance of payment and other certificates. The designer must use (and be seen to have used) his/her fully independent judgement when certifying.

Generally The designer should also:

- immediately confirm in writing if new instructions are given altering the initial brief (see ‘d Specimen client’s instruction’, page 140)
- deal immediately with any complaints, either overt or implied, by explaining in detail why he/she took the course of action complained about
- notify his/her professional indemnity insurers if any allegation of negligence is suggested by anyone (see ‘fC Notes on letter to professional indemnity insurers’, page 262)
- if the client instructs a change, particularly during the construction phase, carefully investigate the full implications of the change (including its effects on consultants’ work), analyse the cost and time implications including any additional fees, and advise the client before proceeding to implement the change
- if a job has been suspended for some time and then resumed, review the work done on the project carefully before proceeding. This is particularly important if a partially completed job has been taken over from another practice
- if a design error has been made, rectify it as soon as it is discovered and try to limit its consequences. The designer should advise his/her professional indemnity insurers
- avoid cash transactions. It can be difficult to later evidence such payments and they are often used to evade tax, a practice that the designer would be well advised to have no connection with.

## P3 Background to ID/10

The Form of Appointment for Interior Design Services (ID/05), was published in 2005 by the RIBA and the BIID (at that time the BIDA) and had a distinguished lineage. It was descended from the Standard Form of Agreement for the Appointment of an Architect, first published in 1992 (SFA/92) with a revised edition published in 1999 (SFA/99) which was slightly amended in 2000 and 2004. Slightly simplified versions, CE/95 and CE/99, were also published. In 2010, ID/05 was revised to take account of changes in legislation, and feedback from users and is called ID/10.

Since the publication of these forms, a small body of case law has built up (see ‘Applicable case law’, page 282), reinforcing their effectiveness and establishing their position within the construction industry. They are generally seen as providing a fair balance between the interests of the client and the architect or designer and, when properly used, these forms are fully enforceable.

This is not to say that these forms are universally accepted by all classes of client. Large organisations who regularly commission architects and interior designers, e.g. developers, government, etc. often have their own bespoke forms which they seek to impose on their consultants. It is generally true that such forms will significantly disadvantage the consultant by removing various safeguards (such as the ability to enforce payment) and adding onerous responsibilities and should therefore be considered only after taking expert legal advice.

The following is a brief summary of ID/05 terms that have been significantly changed in ID/10:

- References to the CDM Regulations have been omitted (ID/05 clauses 1.7 and 8.22). This has been done on the ground that compliance with the regulations is required by law and therefore it is unnecessary to include mention of them in the agreement. Designers would be well advised to draw the attention of inexperienced clients to their responsibilities (if any) under these regulations.
- A clause mentioning a Designer’s Representative has been added (ID/10 clause 2.7).
- References to site inspectors (clerks of works) have been omitted (ID/05 clause 3.10). These can be employed under ID/10 clause 4.3.
- Recovery of costs in ID/10 is on a taxed (normal) basis rather than an indemnity basis and now applies equally to both designer and client (ID/10 clause 5.22).
- The consumer’s right to cancel which came into effect on 1 October 2008 (ID/10 section 10) has been added.
- A number of clauses regarding FF&E have been added in ID/10 Annex B.

The following clause-by-clause explanation of ID/10 (see ‘P3 Explanation of ID/10 terms’, page 46) is designed to ensure that designers understand what they and their clients are agreeing to when they enter into this form of appointment, how to complete it properly and how it should be explained to those clients who are consumers.
