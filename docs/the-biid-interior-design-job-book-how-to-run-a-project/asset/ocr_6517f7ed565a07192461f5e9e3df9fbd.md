# P3 Designer’s responsibilities to clients

> In 1917, Beatrice and Sidney Webb defined ‘profession’ as ‘...a vocation as ‘a vocation’ founded upon specialised educational training, the purpose of which is to supply disinterested counsel and service to others, for a direct and definite compensation, wholly apart from expectation of other business gain.’

# Responsibilities

The practice of interior design is extraordinary amongst the construction professions as its practitioners can be acting in both a professional and a commercial capacity in the normal performance of their tasks.

# Professional

In law, professionals are expected to act in their clients’ best interests, owing them a duty of care for which they receive an agreed fee. Historically, they were personally responsible to clients for their advice, a principle now diluted by allowing professionals to practise with the protection of limited liability. Dealings with clients should be transparent. Professionals should insure themselves against being found liable for professional negligence, and it is important to note that this insurance usually does not cover commercial risks. Generally, professionals should avoid warranting outcomes that are not in their full control, e.g. that a project will be completed within time or budget.

# Commercial

A commercial entity does not owe such a strict duty to its customers; for example it is not required to disclose its profit margins. It is not uncommon for them to warrant outcomes, e.g. that they will construct a project or supply furniture, fixtures and equipment (FF&E) for an agreed price and within an agreed time.

# As a designer

First, the designer acts as a professional in advising clients and in the preparation of drawings, specifications and contract documents and in the monitoring of the construction process.

In this role, the designer should declare to the client any additional remuneration that the practice is receiving in relation to the project from whatever source, e.g. suppliers (see ‘Applicable case law’, page 276, Eisenberg v Bachar).

Also in this role, the designer can, acting as an agent, procure FF&E on behalf of the client for an agreed fee or handling charge. When doing this, the contract for supply is between the supplier and the client, and the supplier is responsible for the fitness of the goods supplied.

# As a contractor

Second, if the designer is responsible for the carrying out of any building work on site (and this can be as trivial as redecorating a room using the designer’s own tradesmen) then the designer is acting as a contractor. In doing so, the designer is in a commercial relationship with the client and is required to carry out the building works in accordance with the building contract.

# As a retailer

Third, the designer can supply FF&E to the client acting as a principal, i.e. in the role of retailer. Indeed, some interior designers carry out this task from their own shops. In this role, the designer is also in a commercial relationship with the client and is required to supply the FF&E in compliance with applicable law. When doing this, the contract for supply is between the designer and the client, and the designer is responsible for the fitness of the goods supplied. This can cause a problem for the designer if supplied goods are later discovered to be faulty and the supplier has become unavailable, perhaps due to insolvency.

Clearly, these three roles can overlap at times and present law is not absolutely clear on the precise nature of the responsibility of the designer to the client when the designer is acting in both a professional and a commercial capacity. The safest way, if in doubt, is for the designer to explain to the client how the practice intends to deal with a particular matter, disclose any potential conflicts of interest and obtain the client’s prior approval.
