
## The RIBA Plan of Work

The structure of this book is organised around the construction industry's sequence of defined work stages for managing the building design and construction process, the RIBA Plan of Work: Multi-disciplinary Services 2007, revised in 2008. This admirable document, first drafted in 1964, provides a formal procedure for running a building project and was quickly adopted by the whole industry.

It has been amended over the intervening years to include disciplines other than architecture but, oddly, does not include interior design. One of the aims of this book is to show how interior design should be integrated into the overall construction process.

Also missing from the Plan of Work are preliminary stages dealing with making proposals to clients and other pre-appointment matters, in particular negotiating the form of appointment with the client.

The full Plan of Work is sometimes too complex for modest projects that interior designers often carry out. However, it is cited because on larger projects an architect is likely to be the design team leader and the consultant with whom the interior designer works most closely. Following the Plan of Work will ensure that the work of designers in large design teams will mesh with the other participants in a project.

For smaller projects, it can be useful to group stages together, e.g. C Concept, D Design Development and E Technical Design, for the purposes of submissions to the client, invoicing, etc.

## P1 Fundamentals stage

This preliminary stage covers general procedures that should be used on all projects.

Designers should remember that they are often spending large sums of their clients' money and must always be able to show that they have not been careless. Clients can sometimes misguidedly or even malevolently claim that a designer has been negligent. The best protection, in addition to being competent, is to carefully document every action taken on a job and the reasons for doing so.

Good practice therefore demands that certain procedures should be followed throughout the course of a job. These include making careful paper records of all emails, telephone conversations and meetings, keeping careful time and expense records and registering all documents issued to others.

Job filing should be kept current, at the very least every week, as unsuspecting practices can be 'ambushed' by an adjudication which will be determined in only 28 days. Mounting an effective defence can be achieved only if the practice is always prepared.

Files for small jobs can simply be kept chronologically but it is often useful for the files for large projects to be broken down into sections, for example:

- 0 General
- 1 Client
- 2 Design team
- 3 Consents
- 4 Services/utilities
- 5 Contractor
- 6 Subcontractors/suppliers
- 7 Construction administration
- ... Other as necessary

## P1 Notes on practice stationery

### Stationery layout

The Signer Associates’ practice stationery has been laid out with the firm's name, address and other details collected to the left of the page, thus leaving the remainder for the letter itself. This prevents the body of the letter being lost in the inside page margin when bound into a file.

### Legal requirements

All correspondence from the company including letters, notices, cheques, orders, invoices, receipts, internet sites and emails must include the full corporate name of the company, in this example, 'Signer Associates Ltd'. In particular, all letters, order forms and websites of a limited company must show:

- the practice's legal status, e.g. sole trader, partnership, limited liability partnership, limited company
- the company's registered office address
- the company's place of registration
- its registration number
- the names of all of the directors, or none, but not a selection.

In addition to this information, The Provision of Services Regulations 2009 require that a business offering services shows on its website or makes available at its premises:

- its VAT number
- membership of any professional body and any code of conduct that the firm is subject to, e.g. BIID Code of Conduct
- terms of business generally used, e.g. ID/10
- contact details of PI insurer and territorial coverage of insurance
- main features of the services offered, e.g. interior design.

### Letters

Projects should be given a file number at their commencement. The system shown here is to assign a number for a client and then a further number after a decimal point to indicate the job, i.e. '123' for Mr Ployer and '01' for his office project and '02' for work on his house. This has the advantage that related projects can be located near each other in file drawers. Other designers will have their own systems, perhaps the most prevalent being numbering each job consecutively as it comes in.

The subject of a letter should always contain the name of the project and (except when sent to the client) the name of the client. This shows that the designer is acting as agent for the client, not on their own behalf.

As far as is practicable, letters should be self explanatory so as to be understandable if read in the future by a third party unfamiliar with the job's history.

It is generally recommended that letters of substance are copied to the client so that he/she can have knowledge of how the designer is processing the project and have a timely opportunity to object if he/she thinks the designer is going off in the wrong direction. (The converse of this is that the client cannot later claim that he/she was unaware of the designer's actions if he/she were kept informed at all times.)
