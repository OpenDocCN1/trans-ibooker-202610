
## Building contract

If the designer is acting as contractor, he/she may wish to carry out this portion of the work under Annex A of ID/10.

However, if the designer is not carrying out this task, then there are a number of standard forms of contract between client and contractor that can be used (see 'e Notes on contract documents', page 144 and 'f Notes on letter to client re procurement route', page 168). JCT MW 05 is used as the example in this book.

The designer should meet with the client and explain the recommended form and the responsibilities of the client under it and confirm this in writing.

### Liquidated damages

Specifying a sum for the rate of liquidated damages (sometimes called liquidated and ascertained damages or LADs) is a way of precisely quantifying, for the purposes of the building contract, the estimated amount of monetary damages per day, week or month that the client will suffer if the works are completed late so that, in the event that this occurs, there will be no dispute over the rate used in computing the amount deducted from payment to the contractor. It also allows the tenderer to quantify at tender stage the risk of completing late. The amount of the damages should roughly approximate the client’s likely damages; if they are significantly higher then a court might construe them as a penalty and strike them out.

### Final certificates

Certain standard building contracts (but not the forms mentioned in 'f Notes on letter to client re procurement route', page 168) effectively provide that the final payment certificate issued by the designer to the contractor is conclusive evidence that the works have been carried out in accordance with the building contract. As the designer generally visits the site only at intervals, defective work can be built in and covered up between visits.

Thus the issue of a final certificate under a contract with such a term may deprive the client of any remedy that he/she might have against the contractor for defective work which is later discovered. The client's only remedy will be against the designer if he/she believes that the designer should have noticed the latent defect. If the designer is operating such a contract, such a term should be deleted before the contract is entered into.

## H Tender Action stage

### Stage description
Stage H, Tender Action, covers the process of obtaining tenders for the work, including tenders of any specialist subcontract or supply items, appraising the tenders and submitting them to the client together with recommendations as how to proceed.

### RIBA Plan of Work
The _RIBA Outline Plan of Work 2007_ describes the key tasks for this stage as, ‘Identification and evaluation of potential contractors and/or specialists for the project. Obtaining and appraising tenders; submission of recommendations to the client.’

### ID/10
Stage H is an ‘Installation Management’ stage on page A of the Schedule of Interior Design Services in ID/10 unless the designer is acting as contractor, in which case it is a ‘Building Works’ stage. Where the designer is supplying FF&E, this task is shown in the ‘FF&E supplied by designer’ stage.

### Tasks: Interior designer
When acting as the _interior designer_ in a design team, the designer will carry out only the following task (deliverables in **bold**):
- Invite and obtain **interior specialist subcontract/supply tenders** and report to the client.

### Lead consultant
When acting as _lead consultant_, the designer will carry out the task above as well as the following three tasks:
- Invite and obtain **tenders for the main contract works**.
- Assist the quantity surveyor to appraise the tenders received, prepare a **tender cost and time report** including FF&E and negotiate with selected tenderer(s).
- Present the **tender report** (incorporating the above deliverables) to, and review with, the client.

### Contractor
When acting as _contractor_ under Annex A of ID/10, the designer will carry out only the very first task above (under ‘Interior designer’) together with the following four tasks:
- Incorporate the drawings, specification and schedules carried out in previous stages into a **contract documents package**.
- Prepare a time and cost tender.
- Prepare a **form of building contract**.
- Present the three deliverables listed above to, and review with, the client; agree and execute the **building contract**.

### FF&E procuring
When _procuring_ FF&E under Annex B, sections B1 and B2, of ID/10, the designer will also carry out the following task:
- Invite and obtain **quotations for items of FF&E**; present to, and review with, the client.

### FF&E supplying
When _supplying_ FF&E under Annex B, sections B1 and B3, of ID/10, the designer will also carry out the following task:
- Present the **priced schedule of FF&E** to, review with, and agree with, the client; arrange for payment.

On smaller projects, stages F, Production Information, G, Tender Documentation, and H are often carried out in the same exercise.

## H Notes on letter to client re site inspector

(None of the following applies when the designer, acting as contractor, is carrying out the building works under Annex A of ID/10.)

### Quality control

One misapprehension amongst architects, interior designers and sometimes clients is that the designer is responsible for quality control during construction. This is not the case unless specifically provided for in the designer’s appointment. The designer should not contract with the client to ‘supervise’ construction, rather to ‘monitor’, ‘inspect’ or (as in ID/10) to ‘administer the terms of the building contract’.

The main contractor is responsible for complying with the terms of the building contract, including the drawings and specification. Therefore it is important to be as specific as possible in these documents about the level of quality required.

However, some processes, e.g. special decorative finishes, may require the designer to instruct the contractor on site precisely what his/her intentions are and to monitor the results. In these cases the designer should show a note in the specifications and/or the drawings that the contractor should not commence this work without giving the designer adequate notice to be in attendance and to approve initial samples of the work.
