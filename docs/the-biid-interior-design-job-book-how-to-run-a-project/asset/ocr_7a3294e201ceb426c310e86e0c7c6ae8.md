**Building contract**

If the designer is acting as contractor, he/she may wish to carry out this portion of the work under Annex A of ID/10.

However, if the designer is not carrying out this task, then there are a number of standard forms of contract between client and contractor that can be used (see 'e Notes on contract documents', page 144 and 'f Notes on letter to client re procurement route', page 168). JCT MW 05 is used as the example in this book.

The designer should meet with the client and explain the recommended form and the responsibilities of the client under it and confirm this in writing.

**Liquidated damages**

Specifying a sum for the rate of liquidated damages (sometimes called liquidated and ascertained damages or LADs) is a way of precisely quantifying, for the purposes of the building contract, the estimated amount of monetary damages per day, week or month that the client will suffer if the works are completed late so that, in the event that this occurs, there will be no dispute over the rate used in computing the amount deducted from payment to the contractor. It also allows the tenderer to quantify at tender stage the risk of completing late. The amount of the damages should roughly approximate the client’s likely damages; if they are significantly higher then a court might construe them as a penalty and strike them out.

**Final certificates**

Certain standard building contracts (but not the forms mentioned in 'f Notes on letter to client re procurement route', page 168) effectively provide that the final payment certificate issued by the designer to the contractor is conclusive evidence that the works have been carried out in accordance with the building contract. As the designer generally visits the site only at intervals, defective work can be built in and covered up between visits.

Thus the issue of a final certificate under a contract with such a term may deprive the client of any remedy that he/she might have against the contractor for defective work which is later discovered. The client's only remedy will be against the designer if he/she believes that the designer should have noticed the latent defect. If the designer is operating such a contract, such a term should be deleted before the contract is entered into.
