
## £E Notes on letter to client re payment made

### Acceptance
When payment is received following an offer, it is prudent to confirm the terms on which the offer was made and on which the payment is accepted. The designer should avoid in this letter any suggestion of smugness or self-satisfaction in having collected sums due.

### Full and final 1
One of the most important of these terms is ‘full and final settlement of all parties’ (including principals’) claims against each other howsoever arising’. This should be as broad as possible to preclude any later claims by the client for, say, negligence, or in respect of another project carried out for the same client. This wording should not be amended except on the advice of a solicitor.

### PI insurance
It can be helpful when negotiating future professional indemnity insurance premiums to be able to cite such a settlement term demonstrating that the risk of a successful claim from this disputatious client is greatly reduced.

### Full and final 2
However, a client can also use this stratagem against the designer by under-paying with a cheque ‘in full and final settlement’. If the designer faces this tactic, there are several points to consider:

- Does the payment offer represent a reasonably fair compromise, bearing in mind the value of any uncompleted designer’s work? If it does, then it makes sense to take a commercial decision to accept it and move on from the project.

- If the payment does not represent a fair compromise then the designer could pay the cheque into his/her account and at the same time send a letter to the client stating that the cheque is not accepted in full and final settlement but only on account of the claim. It should also include a request for payment of the balance within seven or 14 days. This can be done by enclosing a current statement allowing credit for the part payment.

If payment is not forthcoming within the requested period then the designer can provide the client with a seven days’ notice of the intention to place the matter in the hands of his/her solicitor.

- However, if the client sends the cheque with wording along the lines, ‘I enclose my cheque in full and final settlement of your claim for payment; if you are not prepared to accept my cheque on this basis, then you must return the cheque to me’, then there is a greater risk that the court will allow the client’s ploy and the designer will be unable to recover the balance if the cheque is paid in.

## £E Dispute resolution

> _‘Discourage litigation. Persuade your neighbours to compromise whenever you can. Point out to them how the nominal winner is often a real loser; in fees, expenses and waste of time.’ Abraham Lincoln._

If a client persists in not paying, there are several alternative methods of dispute resolution provided for in ID/10:

### Mediation
Clause 9.1: The courts promote mediation as a pre-court stage for resolving disputes and sometimes will refuse to award costs if the successful party has unreasonably refused to mediate. It can be cheaper and quicker than other forms of dispute resolution and take up less management time. It can also offer the opportunity for the parties to set out their cases and resolve differences before costs escalate. However, mediation is unlikely to resolve a dispute if one or both parties are acting unreasonably.

As with arbitration, mediation is confidential. It is also without prejudice so that information disclosed cannot be used in court if a settlement is not achieved. Even if mediation is unsuccessful in resolving a dispute, it can clarify arguments and thus reduce the time needing to be spent in litigation or arbitration.

### Adjudication
Clause 9.2: Adjudication is, by law, used in construction disputes, provided that, if the client is a consumer, for this method of dispute resolution to be available, it has to have been expressly agreed before the contract was entered into. The adjudicator has to reach a decision within 28 days (or such longer period as is permitted by the parties), so this method is not only relatively inexpensive but also quick.

As the timescale is so short, an advantage can be gained by the referring party by carefully constructing a case and then ‘ambushing’ the other party, leaving them with little time to organise a defence. Therefore designers should always keep complete and current files to avoid being disadvantaged.

The costs of adjudication are not usually awarded to the successful party but under ID/10, costs can be awarded by the adjudicator, which allows adjudication to be used with consumer clients, provided it had been previously agreed with them.

Almost all adjudications are enforced: only about 5% of decisions go to court for enforcement and less than 1% are not enforced.

### Small claims
(clause 9.3.2(a)) The ‘small claims court’ (i.e. the small claims track litigation procedure in the County Court) can be used for claims not exceeding £5,000 where the litigant can pursue the claim without incurring the cost of a solicitor. However, there is a risk that a client may issue a counterclaim for more than that sum, taking the dispute above the small claims limit. Further, there is also a risk that the judge may decide that the claim is too complex to be dealt with under this simplified procedure.

### Arbitration
(clause 9.3) Arbitration has the advantage of being private so that the designer does not have to risk having his/her name attached to a legal case.

A further advantage is that the arbitrator is prevented from making an order for security for costs under clause 9.3.2(c).

However, the cost of the arbitrator and other expenses are charged to the parties.

### Litigation
(clause 9.1) This is not provided for specifically in ID/10 save as a way of enforcing adjudication decisions. However, the parties can agree before the appointment is entered into to have disputes resolved by a court.

If the decision is taken to resort to a formal means of dispute resolution, say, to recover sums due or to resist a negligence claim, the designer should carefully manage those aspects of the case over which he/she has some control, in particular how to assist his/her lawyers. The designer should always remember that, having lived through the project, he/she has far more knowledge than anyone else (even the client) about its detailed history.

### Documents
The first step is to ensure that all papers relating to the project are in the file and are in good order. If the dispute is being litigated or arbitrated, all the designer’s files must be made available to the other side in a process known as discovery (or disclosure). The designer and his/her lawyers should identify the issues in dispute, copy any documents that relate to these issues and place them in a lever arch file in date order. If there is more than one issue, then a file should be made for each. Doing this will reduce lawyers’ fees and, under clause 5.22.1 of ID/10, the time spent will be recoverable from the client if the designer succeeds in the case. Providing digital copies on disc as well as on paper will also save costs. However, the inclusion of irrelevant or duplicated documents may attract a reduction in any costs awarded.

### Legal cost estimate
Having put together the file(s) that show the general scope of the dispute, the designer will be in a position to seek a cost estimate from his/her lawyer. Lawyers have traditionally charged on a time basis with the disadvantage that the disputant has no real idea of the likely total legal costs. However, some lawyers are now prepared to quote fixed fees, target costs or conditional fees (see ‘£C Payment collection’, page 258). Now, under their code of conduct, solicitors are required to provide clients with estimates of their likely fees from the outset of being instructed.

### Chronology
The designer should prepare a detailed chronology of events relating to the issue(s) in dispute, referenced to all relevant file documents, and take the lawyer through it so that he/she fully understands the sequence. This is particularly important as, in a complex process such as an interior design project, some matters (such as the ultimate cost and timetable) will have become evident only as the project develops.

### Expert witness
If the dispute involves technical issues or questions of opinion (such as whether a designer has properly performed services), the court will usually require that expert witness(es) give evidence. The parties to the action may each appoint their own or in some cases the court appoints a single expert.

If expert witnesses are required, it is advisable that the designer’s witness be appointed early in the preparations for the case. Experts have an overriding duty to the court to give unbiased evidence, even if this is not supportive of the case advanced by the person employing them. Therefore it is sensible to obtain early advice on the strengths and/or weaknesses of the technical issues before expending too much time and money. Experts are required to prepare witness statements for submission to the other side and to the court. The designer and his/her lawyer should agree the strategy of their expert’s statement and the designer should read it at all stages in its preparation to ensure that it conforms to the facts of the case.

Witnesses will be under pressure to give an opinion on the merits of the case as quickly and as cheaply as possible and so again it is important that the relevant evidence is separated out and the expert’s attention is drawn to any key documents.
