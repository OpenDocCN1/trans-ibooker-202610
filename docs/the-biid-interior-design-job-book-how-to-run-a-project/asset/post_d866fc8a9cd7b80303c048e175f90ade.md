
If the dispute involves a claim of negligence, professional indemnity (pi) insurers have often been surprisingly willing to settle on the client's terms because the legal costs of defending a claim can be even greater than the amount of the claim itself. This can be aggravated by the general principle that if a claimant succeeds at trial in just one of his/her claims (even though the action might involve many claims which need to be expensively defended against) the full burden of costs goes against the defendant (see 'p3 Explanation of ID/10 terms', page 46). Clause 5.22 of ID/10 prevents this by providing that an unsuccessful party pay to the other the costs of a claim or part of a claim which is successfully defended or abandoned.

This principle is, however, subject to a number of factors including how the claimant has conducted him/herself both before and during legal proceedings in co-operating with requests to consider alternatives to legal proceedings such as mediation, providing proper particulars of the case in good time and complying with directions of the court and requests from the other party to provide information and details of the case. Failure to demonstrate good conduct could mean that the court penalises an otherwise successful party in the amount of costs which can be recovered from the other.

## Objective

The designer should discuss at an early stage with his/her lawyer what the designer's objectives are and plan a suitable strategy. For example, when sums due are being pursued, an early decision should be taken on the minimum amount that the designer should accept if an offer is made. Part of the strategy must include what level of costs and time the designer wishes to invest in the dispute. As well as legal costs, the designer must consider his/her own time that will be required to support the action and which he/she will not otherwise be able to devote to his/her business.

## Insurers

When negligence is alleged, either as a defence to a fee claim or independently, professional indemnity insurers often insist that specialist construction negligence lawyers be appointed. Under the terms of many (but not all) pi policies and, as legal costs are borne by the insurers, they have this right.

In theory, in these circumstances, these lawyers are acting on behalf of both the designers and the insurers but, as they are being paid by the insurers and as they may well wish to seek future employment by the insurers, their loyalty will always be with them. Insurers often instruct these solicitors to communicate their advice and opinion on the merits of the case only to them and not to the designer. If this is the case, the designer should object strongly, if only to ensure that the solicitors have not ignored or misconstrued crucial evidence.

## Legal advice

Designers should listen carefully to expert and legal advice, particularly if it is adverse. No one likes to be told that their professional performance might not have been competent, or that they have not followed all the procedures necessary to enforce payment. Lawyers certainly do not relish having to give such advice to their clients. However, if a designer receives such advice and if he/she has confirmed that the advice is based on a correct reading of all the relevant facts of the case, then it is far better to instruct his/her lawyers to come to the most advantageous settlement achievable and move on to more profitable activities.

## 8E Giving evidence in a hearing

> > 'Every time you take any action, imagine how you would explain it to a hostile barrister.' Raymond Cecil RIBA.

### Legal action

Sometimes even the most careful designer will become involved in a legal action, perhaps in support of a claim for fees due or in defence of a negligence claim. It is at this point that a carefully kept project file containing correspondence, records of all meetings, telephone conversations, decisions taken, time sheets and all other aspects of a job becomes of vital importance.

In preliminary negotiations between the parties' solicitors, a thoroughly evidenced case can help convince the other side's solicitors that the designer will prevail and may well influence them to recommend that their client settle on terms favourable to the designer.

If the case does go to a hearing, contemporaneous written records (see 'r1 Specimen discussion record, page 9') are often accepted as compelling evidence by a court. However, records that have clearly been created well after the event that they purport to record can prejudice a case. (See Applicable case law: Munkenbeck v Harold, page 282), in which time sheets, which were evidently prepared well after the time in question, were rejected by the judge as a basis on which an architect could recover the cost of time spent.)

### Preparation

If a designer has to give evidence in a hearing before an adjudicator, arbitrator or judge, it is crucial to prepare carefully. Copies should be made of all evidence that bears on matters relevant to the case and kept in a separate case file.

The designer should make him/herself familiar with this file and with the principles upon which his/her legal team is prosecuting or defending the case. It is worthwhile spending sufficient time to do this as, under ID/10 clause 5.22, the designer will be reimbursed for this time provided his/her legal action is successful or the action (or part of it) is abandoned by the client.

### Witness statement

Well before the hearing, the designer will be asked to sign a witness statement, which will include a declaration that the designer believes the statement to be true. It is very important that such a statement be drafted by a solicitor in the designer's own words but without using jargon. The designer should read the final draft carefully and ensure that it contains no errors whatsoever before signing it.

There is nothing more harmful to a witness's credibility or detrimental to the chances of a case succeeding than a hostile barrister demonstrating that statements made by a witness are untrue, perhaps because of the evidence of others or even inconsistencies in the witness's own evidence in the hearing. Anyone doubting this should read the judgment in Fitzroy Robinson v Mentmore Towers (page 282).

### Hearing

When giving evidence in a hearing, the designer will first be asked to give his/her full name, professional address, professional qualifications, how long in practice and any special expertise the designer might claim. It is important not to exaggerate the latter as an expert might be required to meet a higher standard of care than an ordinary practitioner.

### Likely questions

The designer should attempt to discover from his/her legal team what questions are likely to be asked as giving evidence on the witness stand can make the witness feel very exposed.

## 8E Giving evidence in a hearing (continued)

### Being questioned

> *An architect, when asked in court if he knew the current whereabouts of the client's former solicitor, truthfully replied simply, 'Brio', the Czech town from which he had last received a postcard from him. On reflection, it might have been better if he had clarified his answer (although it would have made no difference to the outcome).*

However, there are a number of ways of preparing for a hearing so that the designer can be sure that he/she gives effective evidence. A witness should:

- ask his/her solicitor before the hearing what the correct form of address is: 'sir', 'your honour', 'my lord'
- listen very carefully to the question and answer it only when he/she is sure of its meaning. It can be useful for the witness to re-state the question in clear terms before answering it to ensure that he/she fully understands it and to gain time to consider it carefully. There is no stigma in asking for clarification of a question
- resist any pressure to rush to answer. A careful, considered answer will carry more conviction
- make the answer as clear as possible in lay terms
- consider questions as opportunities to stress relevant matters that support the designer's case
- not give an answer which he/she is not sure about or is outside his/her expertise
- generally decline to speculate or answer hypothetical questions
- always be courteous to all parties, particularly when being questioned by the other side's barrister. Firmly (but never angrily) reject any suggestion put to him/her which the designer believes not to be true or fair
- address his/her comments to the adjudicator/arbitrator/judge
- not be tempted to fill silences
- if the questioner attempts to limit the answer, give the answer to the question asked, then turn to the adjudicator/arbitrator/judge and make clear there is more to be said
- frankly admit any points which are not in the designer's favour but add any rider or explanation that he/she wishes to give. Trying to evade a difficult question only makes a witness appear unreliable
- never attempt to be humorous. (This is the adjudicator/arbitrator/judge's prerogative!).

However, if the designer has followed the procedures in this book, the chances of having to become involved in a legal action or give evidence in a hearing are much reduced.
