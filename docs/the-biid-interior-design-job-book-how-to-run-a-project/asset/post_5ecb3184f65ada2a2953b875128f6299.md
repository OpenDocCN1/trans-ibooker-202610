
## P3 Why use ID/10?

When it becomes clear that a client is minded to appoint a practice to carry out interior design services on a project, subject to terms being agreed, the designer should prepare a written contract.

There is often an understandable, but misguided, reluctance by designers to formalise their contract with the client. Designers are sometimes so keen to secure a project that they rush into an unsatisfactory agreement. Doing so can later cause their practice great harm.

This reluctance is often caused by:

- the worry that the client will be put off by having to sign a detailed contract. The designer should point out that a formal contract gives the client several important safeguards (see ‘Client safeguards’ below)
- the belief that all clients are ‘gentlemen’ and would be irritated at having to commit in writing to what they have previously agreed verbally. The authors have over the years learned of a number of such clients who have not hesitated to attempt to renege on agreements
- the feeling that it would be insulting to demand that a client with whom one had previously worked successfully should sign a contract. Clients can die and heirs can conveniently ignore verbal agreements; similarly, company executives can leave their posts, and their successors can decide that they have no desire to carry on as before.

> The Times obituary on 6 February 2009 of architect Richard Tyler, 94, who specialised in restoring large country houses, noted: ‘Tyler became skilled in dealing with sometimes difficult cash-strapped clients including some who were alarmed when he presented them with a bill, having hoped that he would provide advice and drawings in return for claret and a cold bedroom.’

There are as many reasons for proceeding deliberately at this stage as there are for carrying out the work itself competently. There is no need to think of the design contract as something to which the client will automatically object. Rather, it is a sign of the designer’s competence that he/she prepares a detailed agreement.

It is useful to explain to the client that ID/10 has become the industry standard form for the procurement of interior design services and that provides a fair balance between the interests of clients and designers.

## Client safeguards

There are a number of safeguards for the client in the contract (ID/10 clause numbers shown in brackets):

- Interior designers set out in detail the services they will perform for their fee and show how this fee will be calculated.
- The designer will keep the client informed of issues involving time, cost and adherence to the brief as soon as they become apparent (clause 2.2).
- The designer will use reasonable skill, care and diligence in the carrying out of the work (clause 2.1).
- The designer will make material alterations to the project only with the client’s approval (clause 2.6.1).
- The designer carries professional indemnity insurance (PII) and will maintain this to an agreed limit (clause 7.4) and for an agreed period after the project ends (clause 7.1).
- A relatively quick and inexpensive method of resolving disputes is provided (clause 9.2).

> 'Architects as a class are not known for their keen business sense, particularly where their own contracts with their clients are concerned. This is something of which many clients are not slow to take advantage.' David Chappell in Construction Contracts Questions and Answers referring to SEA/99 (the architects' version of ID/10).

## Client obligations

- The right to cancel the contract within seven days is included (clause 10).
- For the smooth running of the job, the designer should explain to the client that he/she will need to:
  - appoint any necessary consultants under separate agreements (clause 3.8)
  - comply with the Construction Design and Management (CDM) Regulations
  - take decisions promptly (clause 3.3)
  - advise the designer of the relative priorities of the brief, budget and timetable (clause 3.4)
  - pay fees, expenses, disbursements, etc. when due (clause 5.15).

## Designer safeguards

- The designer is protected by:
  - a prohibition of set-off (clause 5.20)
  - an incentive for the client to pay promptly. Clause 5.21 provides for interest on late payment
  - payment for time spent in collecting sums due and for defending a claim which is unsuccessful or abandoned (clause 5.22)
  - the designer's work being covered by copyright (clause 6.1)
  - the right to publish photographs of the project (clause 2.9)
  - a time limit and cap on liability (clauses 7.1 and 7.2)
  - a provision for net contribution (clause 7.3)
  - a relatively quick and inexpensive method of resolving disputes (clause 9.2)
  - a prohibition of a security for costs application (clause 9.3.2).

Most established designers have learned through bitter experience that some potential clients are a serious liability, and their practice would be better off by not becoming involved with them. Often it is not possible to identify such clients until the project is well under way but an early indication can be an unreasonable objection to the proposed design contract or an attempt to impose terms that would be unfair to the designer.

It cannot be stressed enough how important it is to have a proper written contract between designer and client in place before substantive work starts on a project.

However, risks that no contract can fully protect against are the client acting fraudulently or operating from a jurisdiction outside the UK. If the designer is not able to obtain a good credit report on the client or the client has no assets in the UK, it might be wise for the designer to ask for payments in advance, always maintaining a positive cash flow on the project.

> American architects have an apt saying: 'With some prospective clients, it would be cheaper to give them $1,000 and send them away.'

## P3 How to use ID/10

### Appointment

The appointment contract between client and designer should contain several parts:

- appointment letter
- Conditions of Appointment
- Services Schedule
- Schedule of Fees and Expenses
- Project Data Schedule

together with the following (if supplied by the client) to be attached to the executed copies of the appointment:

- brief
- attachments.

### Appointment letter

(See ‘P3 Notes on appointment letter’, page 36, and model letters, pages 37–41.)

### Conditions

ID/10 is conveniently bound together in a single booklet containing the Conditions of Appointment, a Services Schedule, notes and model letters. The designer should always tear out the notes and model letters (the centre white pages) and, if the services are scheduled in the appointment letter, remove the Services Schedule as well.

Two copies of ID/10 should be used, one for the client and one for the designer’s file. The box at the bottom of page 1 should be completed in both copies.

Depending on what functions the designer will be performing, the following sections of ID/10 will apply:

- when the designer is acting purely as a designer, only the main conditions (clauses 1.1 through 10.4)
- when acting as a contractor, also Annex A
- when procuring FF&E as an agent, also Annex B, parts B1 and B2
- when supplying FF&E as a principal, i.e. as a retailer, also Annex B, parts B1 and B3.

Those parts of the agreement that do not apply should be struck out with a diagonal line.

### Negotiated clauses

Consumers are protected by legislation, in particular the Unfair Terms in Consumer Contracts Regulations 1999 (UTCCR 99). If the client is a consumer, then the designer should explain the terms of the proposed design contract, taking the client through the contract, clause by clause, even at the risk of inducing boredom. It should be explained that, as a professional, the designer is looking after the client’s interests in the expenditure of large sums of their money.
