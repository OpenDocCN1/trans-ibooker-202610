## 9 DISPUTE RESOLUTION

If a dispute between the parties requires external resolution, then the designer should notify his/her PI insurers and consult his/her solicitors. Either party can use:

- negotiation or mediation (clause 9.1)
- litigation (clause 9.1): This is the default method of resolving non-construction disputes and construction disputes where the client is a consumer
- the 'small claims court' (clause 9.3.2(a)): This is the small claims track litigation procedure in the County Court for claims not exceeding £5,000
- arbitration (clause 9.3): This is often used by prior agreement of the parties in construction disputes
- adjudication (clause 9.2): This, by law, is the default method of resolving construction disputes where the client is not a consumer. However, if the client is a consumer, adjudication can not be used provided this is explained to and agreed with the client at the beginning.

9.1 The Client and the Designer may attempt to settle any dispute or difference arising under the Agreement by negotiation or to mediation, if suitable, or either party may refer the matter to adjudication, arbitration or legal proceedings as specified in the Project Data

If a dispute can be settled by negotiation, perhaps with the assistance of solicitors, this is by far the cheapest and quickest way of resolving it.

Mediation is also a useful method of dispute resolution. Even if the parties are suspicious of each other's motives, it can be useful in clarifying arguments and if successful will avoid the costs and time of legal proceedings.

### Adjudication

9.2 The provisions for adjudication are:

- 9.2.1 Where a dispute or difference is to be referred to adjudication, the parties may agree who shall act as adjudicator or, the adjudicator shall be a person nominated at the request of either party by the nominator specified in the Project Data.
- 9.2.2 For the avoidance of doubt, the Adjudicator may allocate between the parties the costs relating to the adjudication, including the fees and expenses of the adjudicator, in accordance with the provisions of clause 5.22.

This provision is ineffective unless it is confirmed in writing by the referring party to the other party and to the adjudicator after notice is given of the intention to refer the dispute to adjudication.

Adjudication is a very useful method of dispute resolution for both designer and client for sums over £5,000 as it is a relatively fast (28 days) and comparatively cheap way of settling disputes. However, one party can be ‘ambushed’ by the other who has prepared his/her case in advance and so it is essential that the designer always keeps his/her files up to date.

The substitution of clause 29 of the Model Adjudication Procedures with the alternative text provides for ‘costs to follow the event’, i.e. to be awarded to the successful party. This is important if the client is a consumer (see ‘Applicable case law: Picardi v Cunniberti, page 282). However, to be effective, the party initiating the adjudication must confirm this provision to the other party and the adjudicator in writing after notice to adjudicate is given.

This clause must be individually negotiated with a consumer client before he/she enters into the contract.

### Arbitration

Arbitration rather than litigation has historically been thought to be the most satisfactory method of dispute resolution (unless both parties agree to mediation or one party decides to resort to adjudication or litigation in the small claims court). The advantage of arbitration is that this method is private and a security for costs application can be prevented. However, the disadvantages of arbitration are that the parties have to pay for the arbitrator, there are limited rights of appeal and the procedures can be as lengthy as litigation. The designer might wish to discuss these choices with his/her solicitor before completing ID/10.

9.3 The provisions for arbitration are:

- 9.3.1 Without prejudice to any right of adjudication, where in the Project Data an arbitration agreement is made and either party requires a dispute or difference (except in connection with the enforcement of any decision of an adjudicator) to be referred to arbitration then that party shall serve on the other party a notice of arbitration to that effect and the dispute or difference shall be referred to a person to be agreed between the parties or, failing agreement within 14 days of the date on which the notice is served, a person appointed by the appointor specified in the Project Data on the application of either party.

The Client or the Designer may litigate any claim for a pecuniary remedy which does not exceed £5,000 or such other sum as is provided by order made under section 91 of the Arbitration Act 1996.

This subclause allows either party to take any dispute not exceeding £5,000 to the County Court using the small claims track procedure. This allows the designer to pursue the action for little cost, perhaps without employing a solicitor.

In such arbitration the Construction Industry Model Arbitration Rules (CIMAR) current at the date of the reference shall apply.

The arbitrator shall not have the power referred to in Section 38(3) of the Arbitration Act 1996.

This subclause prevents security for costs applications. This procedure can be used by an unscrupulous client who, having reduced the designer’s cash flow by non-payment, then uses the funds saved to apply to the court to argue that, in the event that the designer fails in an action to collect fees, the now impecunious designer will not have the funds to pay the client’s legal costs. Just defending such an application can be costly and in itself can achieve a client’s aim of depleting the designer’s funds to a point where he/she is unable to pursue the debt collection action.

## 10 CONSUMER'S RIGHT TO CANCEL

New regulations, presumably to protect consumers against hard-selling double-glazing salesmen, have been enacted which have had the (presumably unintended) consequence of applying to the provision of professional services in respect of the repair, refurbishment or improvement of property, the construction of extensions, patios, conservatories or driveways and/or the supply of goods and their incorporation in property.

The Cancellation of Contracts made in a Consumer's Home or Place of Work etc. Regulations 2008 gives all consumer clients a right to cancel a contract within seven days of signing it. Specifically, the Regulations require any supplier of goods and/or services who negotiates a contract with a consumer client in a home, whether it is the client’s or any other home (including presumably the designer’s home if he/she is practising from home), or on ‘an excursion’ organised by the designer away from his/her business premises (perhaps on a shopping expedition), to notify the client that he/she is entitled to cancel any agreement within seven days of signing it. Any charges incurred before the expiry of seven days cannot be enforced unless the client agrees to this in writing. Section 10 of ID/10 provides this notification together with the specified form of notice of cancellation.

One consequence of this legislation is that it becomes even more important for a designer to obtain a signed agreement from a consumer client before starting any work or supplying any goods because otherwise a client wishing to repudiate an appointment agreement could claim that he/she was not given proper notice under the regulations.

The consumer Client has the right to cancel this Agreement for any reason by delivering or sending (including by electronic mail) a cancellation notice to the Designer at any time within the period of 7 days starting from the date when the Client signed this Agreement.
