# ## Procurement route

It is important when commencing Production Information that the designer has in mind on what basis and under which form of building contract the works will be carried out.

Whilst there are a wide range of procurement strategies available in the construction industry, the most appropriate for a project of the relatively small size covered by this book are:

+   - obtaining competitive tenders: the advantage of this method is that the client can be reasonably sure that he/she has received the most competitive price for the job. It is particularly useful at times when the building industry is relatively quiet and builders are keen to tender. In busy times, they may not be prepared to risk the cost of tendering, particularly when a bill of quantities has not been prepared. There is also the risk that the lowest tenderer will have submitted the lowest price because he/she has made an error in pricing and will then do the work with an eye to cutting corners to avoid making a loss on the project.

+   - negotiating an acceptable tender price and timetable with a selected contractor: the advantage of this method is that a contractor can be chosen who is best qualified for the job. This is important as:
+     - small projects, e.g. house extensions and alterations, often tend to be relatively complex for their size
+     - the client for domestic projects often lives in the house during construction, and the contractor has to be used to working around them
+     - the quality of the work is often important, and contractors need to be able to work to a particularly high standard.

Using a quantity surveyor who knows the current rates for the work in the district to negotiate an acceptable price is usually sufficient to ensure that the client is obtaining value for money.

# ## Contract form

It is also important to determine which form of contract between the client and the main contractor should be used. Whilst in theory the client could use a bespoke contract prepared by a solicitor to meet his/her precise requirements, the cost of drafting a bespoke contract, together with the difficulty of finding a contractor prepared to tender on terms with which he/she is not familiar, makes this difficult to achieve in practice. For the size of project covered by this book, the use of one of the industry standard forms is recommended. The most suitable for small works are:

+   - the Joint Contracts Tribunal (JCT) Building Contract for a Home Owner/Occupier (JCT HO/c) for use when a consultant has been appointed. This is a very simple form of contract and is suitable for only the smallest and most straightforward projects to be carried out for a lump sum price.

+   - Annex A of ID/10. This is suitable for small, uncomplicated projects.

+   - the JCT Minor Building Works Building Contract (MW05). This is generally the most appropriate. However, it has its flaws, one of which is that it is not suitable for use with a bill of quantities. When quantities are required, the JCT Intermediate Contract (IC05) is useful.

If the designer is hesitant about which form to use, he/she should consult a specialist solicitor and/or the quantity surveyor.
