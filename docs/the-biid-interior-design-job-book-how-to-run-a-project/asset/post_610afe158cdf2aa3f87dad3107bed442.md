
## E Notes on contract documents

### Contract documents

The principal documents that make up the contract between the client and the contractor are:

- Building contract form: examples of standard forms are:
  - Annex A of ID/10 if the designer is acting as contractor
  - JCT Minor Works Building Contract (MW 05) if the designer is not acting as contractor and there is no bill of quantities
  - JCT Intermediate Building Contract (IC 05) if there is a bill of quantities.

- Drawings: these are divided into:
  - location (general arrangement)
  - component
  - assembly (detail).

Thus a window’s location would be shown in plan and elevation general arrangement drawings (say, at 1:50 scale), its overall appearance as a fabricated element would be shown in a component drawing (say, 1:20) and the details of its incorporation into the building fabric would be shown in detail drawing(s) (say, 1:5).

- Specification and schedule(s): as shown in ‘E Notes on specifications and schedules’, page 150, specification clauses are divided into:
  - preliminaries (contract conditions)
  - components
  - execution.

- Bill of quantities (optional on small projects).

### Conflicts

Due to the complexity of most projects, it is not unusual for conflicts to occur between (or even within) contract documents, e.g. an element being noted in a drawing as being fabricated by one manufacturer and shown in the specification or a schedule as by another. To reduce the likelihood of such conflicts, the designer should:

- avoid repeating information. For example, product names or suppliers of an element should be shown only in the specification or schedules, and the element’s location, shape, size, etc. shown only in the drawings. Items on the drawings should be cross-referenced by clause number to the specification

- include a clause in the specification preliminaries to require the contractor, upon discovering such a conflict, to obtain an instruction from the designer resolving the matter before proceeding.

### Legal interpretation

The general rule is that where a term has been specifically drafted for a contract, e.g. in the drawings, specification or schedules, then that term will take precedence over a standard term such as in the building contract.

However, this rule can be overridden by an express term of the building contract. For example, clause 1.2 of MW 05 states that ‘nothing contained in the Contract Drawings, the Contract Specification or the Work Schedules ... shall override or modify the Agreement or these Conditions.’ If the designer wishes to amend this provision, clause 1.2 (or its equivalent) should be amended in the building contract form itself.

## E Drawing standards

### Drawings

Drawings are essentially a method of communicating complex, annotated pictorial data. Early drafts are used to convey the concept to the client and are then developed stage by stage as necessary to inform planners, building regulation officials and finally the contractor.

At all stages, it is important that the drawings are clear and consistent. It is helpful therefore if the format is standardised as far as possible to make them easily accessible for all the parties who will read them.

> 'Back roll' drawings so that the printing is on the outside – that way they will stay unrolled when they are laid out.

### Size

Most construction drawings are prepared on A1 sized sheets, 840mm wide by 594mm high. This has an area of half a square metre in the proportion of 1 to 1.414, the 'root two' ratio. This means that dividing the sheet in half vertically gives an A2 sized sheet in the same proportion and so on through A3 (420 x 297mm) and A4 (210 x 297mm).

Small projects can be drawn on A2 or A3 sheets.

### Layout

It is useful to divide the A1 sheet into eight A4 size vertical panels (or an A3 sheet into two A4 panels), using various combinations of the A4 panels as necessary for various sized drawings.

Rather than conventionally showing a title block in the lower right-hand corner, locating the title information along the bottom margin, and revisions boxes along the top, leaves the majority of the sheet available for drawing.

> 'God is in the details.' Ludwig Mies van der Rohe.

### Symbols

There are a number of symbols that are universally understood by all people who read building drawings and these should be used.

### Example

The following sample drawings show a new kitchen inserted into an existing period house. The first sheet shows the floor plan, reflected ceiling plan, an internal elevation of the cabinets and a longitudinal section through the cabinets. The second shows six transverse sections through the cabinets and typical details. The third sheet shows typical details at the ceiling/wall and at the floor/wall intersections.

## E Notes on specifications and schedules

### Specification

The construction specification is developed from ‘d Specimen outline specification’ page 137 and covers three areas:
- Preliminaries: the general conditions that are inserted into the building contract form such as the names of the parties, the site etc. together with the general requirements for mobilisation, running the project and general technical matters.
- Components (sometimes called materials): the elements that are to be used, specified by brand or referenced to an applicable BS.
- Execution (sometimes called workmanship): the assembly of components into the building, again, often referenced to a BS.

### NBS

In order to reduce the work necessary to produce a reliable specification, NBS (now part of RIBA Enterprises) developed many years ago what has become the industry standard, the National Building Specification. It is essentially a library of clauses from which the designer can choose those that are applicable.
- The NBS ‘Domestic Specification’ is a loose-leaf paper specification for use on small domestic projects. Unlike their other products, this is not sold on subscription, but can be purchased when required.
- The NBS ‘Building Minor Works’ provides both paper and digital specification clauses, guidance and product information which are updated three times a year. The guidance for each clause appears beside it on screen for speedy referral. It too is updated three times a year.
- The NBS ‘Scheduler’ enables the designer to write work schedules for refurbishment, alteration and small-scale new-build projects without bills of quantities. As the work schedule is compiled, a reference specification is automatically generated. It is also updated three times a year.

### Schedules

Schedules provide the most succinct and comprehensive way of communicating large amounts of related information which cannot easily be contained on the drawings or specification. These can cover finishes, sanitary and electrical fittings, FF&E, doors/ironmongery etc. Sample schedules are shown on pages 153-157.
When it is necessary to show exactly what work is carried out in each location (such as in a refurbishment project), a work schedule is also necessary, referenced to relevant specification clauses and drawings.
