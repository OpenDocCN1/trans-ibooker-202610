If the decision is taken to resort to a formal means of dispute resolution, say, to recover sums due or to resist a negligence claim, the designer should carefully manage those aspects of the case over which he/she has some control, in particular how to assist his/her lawyers. The designer should always remember that, having lived through the project, he/she has far more knowledge than anyone else (even the client) about its detailed history.

**Documents** The first step is to ensure that all papers relating to the project are in the file and are in good order. If the dispute is being litigated or arbitrated, all the designer’s files must be made available to the other side in a process known as discovery (or disclosure). The designer and his/her lawyers should identify the issues in dispute, copy any documents that relate to these issues and place them in a lever arch file in date order. If there is more than one issue, then a file should be made for each. Doing this will reduce lawyers’ fees and, under clause 5.22.1 of ID/10, the time spent will be recoverable from the client if the designer succeeds in the case. Providing digital copies on disc as well as on paper will also save costs. However, the inclusion of irrelevant or duplicated documents may attract a reduction in any costs awarded.

**Legal cost estimate** Having put together the file(s) that show the general scope of the dispute, the designer will be in a position to seek a cost estimate from his/her lawyer. Lawyers have traditionally charged on a time basis with the disadvantage that the disputant has no real idea of the likely total legal costs. However, some lawyers are now prepared to quote fixed fees, target costs or conditional fees (see ‘£C Payment collection’, page 258). Now, under their code of conduct, solicitors are required to provide clients with estimates of their likely fees from the outset of being instructed.

**Chronology** The designer should prepare a detailed chronology of events relating to the issue(s) in dispute, referenced to all relevant file documents, and take the lawyer through it so that he/she fully understands the sequence. This is particularly important as, in a complex process such as an interior design project, some matters (such as the ultimate cost and timetable) will have become evident only as the project develops.

**Expert witness** If the dispute involves technical issues or questions of opinion (such as whether a designer has properly performed services), the court will usually require that expert witness(es) give evidence. The parties to the action may each appoint their own or in some cases the court appoints a single expert.

If expert witnesses are required, it is advisable that the designer’s witness be appointed early in the preparations for the case. Experts have an overriding duty to the court to give unbiased evidence, even if this is not supportive of the case advanced by the person employing them. Therefore it is sensible to obtain early advice on the strengths and/or weaknesses of the technical issues before expending too much time and money. Experts are required to prepare witness statements for submission to the other side and to the court. The designer and his/her lawyer should agree the strategy of their expert’s statement and the designer should read it at all stages in its preparation to ensure that it conforms to the facts of the case.

Witnesses will be under pressure to give an opinion on the merits of the case as quickly and as cheaply as possible and so again it is important that the relevant evidence is separated out and the expert’s attention is drawn to any key documents.
