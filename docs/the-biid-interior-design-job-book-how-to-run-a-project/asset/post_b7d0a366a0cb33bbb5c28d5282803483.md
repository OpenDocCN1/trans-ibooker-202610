
## B Notes on brief schedules and diagrams

### Room schedule

Having received (or prepared) the brief, the designer’s first task in any space planning exercise is to schedule the rooms or, if this not known precisely, the functions required in the new facility.

### Room requirements

Second, working with the client’s key staff who will use each room, the designer should schedule the detailed requirements for each room. The specimen schedules shown may not fit all types of projects and so these forms should be customised for each project.

In addition to spaces identified, the designer should provide for normal support spaces, e.g. lavatories, cleaners’ cupboards, circulation, etc.

### Adjacency

Next, the designer should look at the proximity/separation requirements for each space by means of an adjacency relationship matrix. This is a trial and error procedure and may require several iterations in the process of bringing together into groups those spaces that need to have close proximity.

### Space relationships

This will lead to the preparation of a space relationship (or ‘bubble’) diagram showing the relationships of each space to the others. Again, this may be an iterative process.

### Planning

From the space relationship diagram, the designer can start to introduce internal circulation, external access points, division into floors, etc. to produce a planning diagram.

### Floor plans

The planning diagram can then be used in the next stage as the basis for generating floor plans.

## B Notes on letter to client confirming stage approval

### Stage approval

It is important to record in writing to the client when each stage has been approved and any changes that might have been instructed, in order to be able to invoice for fees to the end of that stage. The designer should also confirm that the client wishes to proceed to the next stage. Whilst the example refers to stage B, similar letters should be sent upon completion of all stages.

### Client alterations

If the client requires alterations to the submission these should be recorded, and if these represent an increase in the work for the designer when a percentage or lump sum fee for the stage has been agreed, then the designer should issue a client instruction (see ‘d Specimen client’s instruction’, page 140) to the client.

Designers should remember that, as long as their design satisfactorily meets the requirements of the brief (and other essential criteria such as planning consents, building regulations and the laws of physics), they are due payment for work done. They should not be expected to produce alternative schemes without additional fees unless this was agreed in the designer’s appointment (or unless fees have been agreed on a time basis).
