# # H Notes on obtaining tenders

**Tendering** When the employer has decided to use competitive tendering to select a contractor, the designer should follow a careful procedure to ensure that the tendering process is effective for the employer and is fair to the tenderers.

**Tenderers** It is generally thought reasonable to ask between three and five contractors to submit tenders. Too few and there is a risk that not enough valid tenders will be received; too many and the designer may find that he/she does not find contractors willing to tender for future work.

**Cover pricing** Until 2009, it had been common for contractors who were not interested in tendering for a particular project, but did not wish to offend the consultant by declining an invitation, would submit a ‘cover price’. This was determined by obtaining from another tenderer a price that was in excess of the other contractor’s bid, thus ensuring that this tender would not be competitive. This illegal practice was investigated by the Office of Fair Trading which, in that year, penalised 103 large contractors with fines totalling almost £130m.

However, the practice still seems to persist and it is therefore helpful for the designer to reassure tenderers that declining an invitation will not prejudice them with regard to the designer’s future projects.

**Specialist work** When certain items of specialist work are of particular importance, the designer may wish to separately arrange for subcontract tenders to be obtained, nominating the successful subcontract tenderers to the main contractor. If this procedure is to be followed, a list of the subcontract tenderers should be shown in the main contract tender documents so that the main contractor has an opportunity to object.

**Notification** When tenders are received, it is only fair to the tenderers to advise them of the results. It is prudent to have the lowest tender checked and the second lowest notified of the position so that they remain available if necessary.
