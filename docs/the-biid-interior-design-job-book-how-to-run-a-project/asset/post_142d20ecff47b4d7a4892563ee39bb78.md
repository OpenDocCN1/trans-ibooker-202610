## P3 Pre-appointment stage

### Stage description

The preliminary stage covers the preparation and negotiating of the designer’s terms of appointment. When a potential client issues a Request for Proposal (RFP) for design services in respect of a project, the designer should be prepared to submit the documents shown in ‘P3 How to use ID/10’, page 33.

### Contract form

Professional bodies such as the BIID and the RIBA rightly recommend that the agreement between the client and the designer is recorded in writing and signed by both parties. In law, a verbal agreement can still be treated as a form of contract but it is highly unadvisable to agree to carry out professional services on such a basis because:

- the wide range of terms necessary for the efficient running of a project and the protection of both the parties will not be covered in detail
- there is no objective evidence of the agreed terms (or even if they were ever discussed).

Despite this, many designers fall into the trap of not agreeing a written design contract and often end up in difficulty as to the scope of their obligations or in enforcing fee collection as a result (see ‘5C Payment collection; Safeguards’, page 254).

As discussed below (‘P3 Why use ID/10?’, page 31) the BIID Agreement for Interior Design Services (ID/10) is now the construction industry standard form of designer/client agreement and provides a fair balance between the interests of clients and designers.

Designers should not just refer to ID/10 in correspondence but should send a copy to the prospective client unless the designer is quite certain that the client is familiar with this form of contract, perhaps from a previous project carried out by the designer. If the client is a consumer then the designer should individually negotiate certain clauses with him/her (see ‘P3 How to use ID/10; Negotiated clauses’, page 33).

### Services and brief

It should never be assumed that a ‘normal’ interior design service is to be performed. Inexperienced clients may not understand what this involves and some clients may want a partial service. Most jobs are prototypes. Therefore the designer should discover from the client:

- his/her brief, as far as it is known at this stage
- the client's view of the interior design services necessary to properly respond to this brief.

This is particularly important when competitive fee tenders are being sought. In such cases, the designer’s fee quotation should respond precisely to the client’s stated requirements in order to be competitive.

## P3 Designer’s responsibilities to clients

> In 1917, Beatrice and Sidney Webb defined ‘profession’ as ‘...a vocation as ‘a vocation’ founded upon specialised educational training, the purpose of which is to supply disinterested counsel and service to others, for a direct and definite compensation, wholly apart from expectation of other business gain.’

### Responsibilities

The practice of interior design is extraordinary amongst the construction professions as its practitioners can be acting in both a professional and a commercial capacity in the normal performance of their tasks.

### Professional

In law, professionals are expected to act in their clients’ best interests, owing them a duty of care for which they receive an agreed fee. Historically, they were personally responsible to clients for their advice, a principle now diluted by allowing professionals to practise with the protection of limited liability. Dealings with clients should be transparent. Professionals should insure themselves against being found liable for professional negligence, and it is important to note that this insurance usually does not cover commercial risks. Generally, professionals should avoid warranting outcomes that are not in their full control, e.g. that a project will be completed within time or budget.

### Commercial

A commercial entity does not owe such a strict duty to its customers; for example it is not required to disclose its profit margins. It is not uncommon for them to warrant outcomes, e.g. that they will construct a project or supply furniture, fixtures and equipment (FF&E) for an agreed price and within an agreed time.

### As a designer

First, the designer acts as a professional in advising clients and in the preparation of drawings, specifications and contract documents and in the monitoring of the construction process.

In this role, the designer should declare to the client any additional remuneration that the practice is receiving in relation to the project from whatever source, e.g. suppliers (see ‘Applicable case law’, page 276, Eisenberg v Bachar).

Also in this role, the designer can, acting as an agent, procure FF&E on behalf of the client for an agreed fee or handling charge. When doing this, the contract for supply is between the supplier and the client, and the supplier is responsible for the fitness of the goods supplied.

### As a contractor

Second, if the designer is responsible for the carrying out of any building work on site (and this can be as trivial as redecorating a room using the designer’s own tradesmen) then the designer is acting as a contractor. In doing so, the designer is in a commercial relationship with the client and is required to carry out the building works in accordance with the building contract.

### As a retailer

Third, the designer can supply FF&E to the client acting as a principal, i.e. in the role of retailer. Indeed, some interior designers carry out this task from their own shops. In this role, the designer is also in a commercial relationship with the client and is required to supply the FF&E in compliance with applicable law. When doing this, the contract for supply is between the designer and the client, and the designer is responsible for the fitness of the goods supplied. This can cause a problem for the designer if supplied goods are later discovered to be faulty and the supplier has become unavailable, perhaps due to insolvency.

Clearly, these three roles can overlap at times and present law is not absolutely clear on the precise nature of the responsibility of the designer to the client when the designer is acting in both a professional and a commercial capacity. The safest way, if in doubt, is for the designer to explain to the client how the practice intends to deal with a particular matter, disclose any potential conflicts of interest and obtain the client’s prior approval.

## P3 Risk avoidance

### Appointment

Many problems can be avoided if the designer takes care at appointment stage to do the following:

- Check prospective clients before accepting a commission (see ‘5C Payment collection; Safeguards’, page 254).
- Attempt to obtain a detailed brief from the client. If the client is not clear about his/her detailed requirements, consider commencing the project at work stage A or B.
- When proposing on a percentage, or particularly a lump sum, fee basis, ensure that there is a clear brief. If not, do not hesitate to clarify any ill-defined matters with the prospective client.
- Attempt to ensure that there is a reasonable budget, although some clients are (unwisely) reluctant to set a budget at the beginning of a project.
- Ensure that there is provision in the form of appointment for additional payment for additional work carried out by the designer (e.g. ID/10 clause 5.8).
- Ensure that adequate information about the premises involved is supplied to the designer or that the client understands that he/she will have to arrange for it to be prepared (except on the simplest of jobs where the designer might be prepared to carry out a small survey). See ‘c Notes on letter to client re surveys’, page 104.
- Explain the proposed role of the designer to the client, particularly if the designer is to act as contractor and/or retailer.
- Avoid bespoke forms of appointment as they are almost always prejudicial to the designer. Legal advice should be sought to understand the implications of any unfamiliar terms proposed. In particular look carefully at the ‘duty of care’ clause, and if it is different from clause 2.1 of ID/10 (where the key words are: ‘shall exercise reasonable skill, care and diligence in accordance with the normal standards of the designer’s profession’), ensure that professional indemnity insurers are happy with the revised wording.
- Avoid committing to a budget or a timetable when acting as a designer. When acting as contractor, only commit to these after the design has been finalised and agreed with the client.
- Never contract to provide services for which he/she is not qualified.
- Do not employ consultants directly and ensure that the responsibilities of all consultants are clearly defined.
- Do not start work until the form of appointment is agreed and signed by the client. Those expected the part(ies) who actually sign the agreement are those expected; beware a sudden change of name. If two people are involved, e.g. husband and wife, business partners etc. ensure that both sign the form. This will prevent the all too common ploy of one party later claiming that the other had no authority to incur fees.
