# # P1 Notes on practice stationery

# ### Stationery layout

The Signer Associates’ practice stationery has been laid out with the firm's name, address and other details collected to the left of the page, thus leaving the remainder for the letter itself. This prevents the body of the letter being lost in the inside page margin when bound into a file.

# ### Legal requirements

All correspondence from the company including letters, notices, cheques, orders, invoices, receipts, internet sites and emails must include the full corporate name of the company, in this example, 'Signer Associates Ltd'. In particular, all letters, order forms and websites of a limited company must show:

+   - the practice's legal status, e.g. sole trader, partnership, limited liability partnership, limited company
+   - the company's registered office address
+   - the company's place of registration
+   - its registration number
+   - the names of all of the directors, or none, but not a selection.

In addition to this information, The Provision of Services Regulations 2009 require that a business offering services shows on its website or makes available at its premises:

+   - its VAT number
+   - membership of any professional body and any code of conduct that the firm is subject to, e.g. BIID Code of Conduct
+   - terms of business generally used, e.g. ID/10
+   - contact details of PI insurer and territorial coverage of insurance
+   - main features of the services offered, e.g. interior design.

# ### Letters

Projects should be given a file number at their commencement. The system shown here is to assign a number for a client and then a further number after a decimal point to indicate the job, i.e. '123' for Mr Ployer and '01' for his office project and '02' for work on his house. This has the advantage that related projects can be located near each other in file drawers. Other designers will have their own systems, perhaps the most prevalent being numbering each job consecutively as it comes in.

The subject of a letter should always contain the name of the project and (except when sent to the client) the name of the client. This shows that the designer is acting as agent for the client, not on their own behalf.

As far as is practicable, letters should be self explanatory so as to be understandable if read in the future by a third party unfamiliar with the job's history.

It is generally recommended that letters of substance are copied to the client so that he/she can have knowledge of how the designer is processing the project and have a timely opportunity to object if he/she thinks the designer is going off in the wrong direction. (The converse of this is that the client cannot later claim that he/she was unaware of the designer's actions if he/she were kept informed at all times.)
