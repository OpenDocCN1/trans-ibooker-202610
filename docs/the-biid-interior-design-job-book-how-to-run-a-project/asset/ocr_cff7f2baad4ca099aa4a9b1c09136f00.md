# P3 Why use ID/10?

When it becomes clear that a client is minded to appoint a practice to carry out interior design services on a project, subject to terms being agreed, the designer should prepare a written contract.

There is often an understandable, but misguided, reluctance by designers to formalise their contract with the client. Designers are sometimes so keen to secure a project that they rush into an unsatisfactory agreement. Doing so can later cause their practice great harm.

This reluctance is often caused by:

+   - the worry that the client will be put off by having to sign a detailed contract. The designer should point out that a formal contract gives the client several important safeguards (see ‘Client safeguards’ below)
+   - the belief that all clients are ‘gentlemen’ and would be irritated at having to commit in writing to what they have previously agreed verbally. The authors have over the years learned of a number of such clients who have not hesitated to attempt to renege on agreements
+   - the feeling that it would be insulting to demand that a client with whom one had previously worked successfully should sign a contract. Clients can die and heirs can conveniently ignore verbal agreements; similarly, company executives can leave their posts, and their successors can decide that they have no desire to carry on as before.

> The Times obituary on 6 February 2009 of architect Richard Tyler, 94, who specialised in restoring large country houses, noted: ‘Tyler became skilled in dealing with sometimes difficult cash-strapped clients including some who were alarmed when be presented them with a bill, having hoped that he would provide advice and drawings in return for claret and a cold bedroom.’

There are as many reasons for proceeding deliberately at this stage as there are for carrying out the work itself competently. There is no need to think of the design contract as something to which the client will automatically object. Rather, it is a sign of the designer’s competence that he/she prepares a detailed agreement.

It is useful to explain to the client that ID/10 has become the industry standard form for the procurement of interior design services and that provides a fair balance between the interests of clients and designers.

# Client safeguards

There are a number of safeguards for the client in the contract (ID/10 clause numbers shown in brackets):

+   - Interior designers set out in detail the services they will perform for their fee and show how this fee will be calculated.
+   - The designer will keep the client informed of issues involving time, cost and adherence to the brief as soon as they become apparent (clause 2.2).
+   - The designer will use reasonable skill, care and diligence in the carrying out of the work (clause 2.1).
+   - The designer will make material alterations to the project only with the client’s approval (clause 2.6.1).
+   - The designer carries professional indemnity insurance (PII) and will maintain this to an agreed limit (clause 7.4) and for an agreed period after the project ends (clause 7.1).
+   - A relatively quick and inexpensive method of resolving disputes is provided (clause 9.2).
