# P2 Fee proposals

# Fee quotations

Clients often ask designers by way of a Request for Proposal (RFP) to submit quotations for fees in competition with other practices, in the mistaken belief that this will achieve the best value for money. The designer might attempt to explain to the client that professional services should be competitive on quality, not price (would they choose a surgeon only because he/she charged the lowest fee?) but sometimes it is unavoidable. EU rules require that public work over a certain value be competitively tendered and some commercial clients follow this rule as well.

# Fee calculation

In order to submit a realistic quotation for a lump sum or percentage fee, a practice must have a good idea of what projects cost to design, based on careful time records of previous projects, adjusted for inflation, relative size and complexity. This then should be applied to the project in question, planning in detail how the project will proceed and the specific staff who would be assigned to it. Tasks difficult to estimate, e.g. negotiating a planning consent, should be omitted from the quotation if at all possible and made the subject of time charges.

In difficult economic periods, there can be great pressure on a practice to win a job at all costs (with some practices having been known to submit nil fee bids in order to secure possible future work from large clients) but in doing so there is a great risk of:

+   - under-resourcing a job to a level that it cannot be carried out to an adequate standard, leading to a dissatisfied client and even a claim for negligence
+   - reducing the cashflow of the practice to a point where the financial viability of the practice is in jeopardy.

# Negligence

Designers must carry out work on all projects to a reasonable level of competence whatever the level of fee. It is no defence to a negligence claim to argue that the agreed fee was, in retrospect, inadequate.

When fees are squeezed due to competition, it is particularly important to make contractual provision for, and charge for, work additional to that originally requested. It is important to immediately notify clients when circumstances arise (whether due to unforeseen circumstances or client changes) which cause the designer unforeseen expense (see ‘b Notes on client’s instruction’, page 138) which he/she will be charging to the client.

Designers should remember that it is generally better to lose a job if an adequate fee for it cannot be secured than to ‘buy’ a project and live with the financial consequences for the many months or years that the project takes to carry out.
