Once Taylor and Malone became a Foley team, they worked full time from 1971 until Taylor retired in 1992. They were a unique team as Taylor was a respected editor and Foley artist and Malone was an established dancer and admired Foley artist in her own right, as well as an actress. Malone continued to work until 2003. What distinguished this pair from Dufva and Fein as the longest lived Foley team is that they worked separately as well as together, and Malone also worked as an actress while Taylor also edited. While their partnership lasted as long, they were not a single entity—as Dufva and Fein had been—for the entire time.

Another recognized team in Foley was that of Margie O’Malley (Deneke) and Fred J. Brown [*Conan the Barbarian* (1982) and *The Last* *Starfighter* (1984)]. While O’Malley was strictly a Foley artist, Brown was a sound editor who performed Foley. As O’Malley began working in the San Francisco Bay Area with Jennifer Myers [*The English Patient* (1996) and *Good Will Hunting* (1997)] and Marnie Moore [*Chocolat* (2000) and *The Singing Detective* (2003)], Brown continued working in Los Angeles until 1997.

### Realism

Robert Rutledge is included here as one from the transitioning Foley generation of John Post, although he was younger. His mother, Evelyn Rutledge, had been one of the first female sound editors, and as a young man Rutledge worked with his mother. While he inherited the profession, he established himself as a respected and gifted sound and dialogue editor and performed a lot of his own Foley [*Wolfen* (1981) and *Fandango* (1985)]. What was special about Rutledge, and the reason he must be included in the historical perspective, is that he understood the Foley stage as a place to recreate Foley to sound like production. Because Rutledge knew how to save production sound, he stressed the importance of having Foley do only what was required and have it sound like it occurred in production. Rutledge, along with his famous sound colleague Walter Murch, was known for his dislike of Foley that draws attention to itself.

Rutledge knew how to spot a show, pull the effects, supervise a team, walk and edit the Foley, shoot and edit the automated dialogue replacement (ADR), edit the effects, and supervise the final mix. He was a humble and unassuming man, who worked as an equal with other Foley artists rather than as their supervisor. He was a perfectionist and understood how Foley could achieve excellence in the performance of sound, while editing had its own tasks. Rutledge was one of the first to say, “You have to get the feel right. You can cut sync. You can’t cut ‘feel’.” Rutledge believed that the best Foley was intuitive, and he had a special gift for “becoming the character” in his Foley performance.

Rutledge trained his two stepsons, Scott and Gary Hecker, in these crafts. Scott is now an Oscar-nominated, supervising sound editor, and Gary is a veteran Foley artist who has expanded an inventive Foley practice.

### Mr. Elegance

It would be impossible to leave this section of Hollywood Foley history without mentioning Charles L. Campbell, who has left an extraordinary body of work behind. Campbell began his career as an actor and worked in television and motion picture films as a sound editor and supervisor. However, his attention to Foley detail and his exercise in “shoe auditions” for characters in films was unique. Campbell supervised such films as *Misery* (1990), *A Few Good Men* (1992), and *The River Wild* (1994).

Campbell directed Foley artists with a meticulous methodology that translated to many sound creations. Campbell and his Foley artists operated as colleagues. Campbell passed away in 2013, but his contribution to Foley will continue to be felt for decades to come. He encouraged Foley artists to use the Foley stage as a design shop, and, as a result, several of his regular Foley artists expanded their practice to include more special sound effects created on the Foley stage, rather than limiting themselves to the more typical practice of performing the sonic embodiment of the characters in the film.

Campbell will be remembered for his soft-spoken demeanor and his elegant Henry Fonda–like walk. However, it was his determination that the Foley stage be used to design effects and his belief in the importance of highlighting the narrative of the film with sound that will be most remembered.

### New York, New York

Elisha Birnbaum was a production mixer and sound editor in Israel. In 1955, he began to add what he later understood was Foley to his soundtracks. Birnbaum was the first Foley artist in Israel. In 1970, he immigrated to New York and started a sound company called Image Sound Studio. He had one Nagra recorder and a Scully two-track, quarter-inch, reel-to-reel tape recorder. His company was holed up in two rooms in the legendary Brill Building, and he had brought an abundance of quarter-inch sound effects tapes with him. He hired a young man by the name of Tom Fleischman—as a favor to the young man’s mother, picture editor Dede Allen—to categorize the effects and organize them as a sound effects library. Birnbaum used one of the two rooms to do minimal recording, including Foley.

Shortly afterward, Birnbaum was approached by the three owners of a small company, Sound One, to become their fourth partner. At that time, Birnbaum was the only Foley artist in New York and was essentially defining the New York style of Foley—more naturalistic and more like what he had heard as a production mixer. Birnbaum supervised and performed Foley for such films as *The Untouchables* (1987), *My Blue Heaven* (1990), and *Shadows and Fog* (1991). Sound One became the primary postproduction sound facility in New York and remained so for over 30 years. Birnbaum worked until 1996. As part of his contribution to the art of Foley, he trained several Foley artists, including Marco Costanzo, who is now located at c5 in New Jersey. Costanzo has maintained the high standards Birnbaum set. Birnbaum has said of Costanzo, “I am very proud of Marco. He was the most talented of all of my Foley students.” Birnbaum was a gifted Foley artist. He created Foley for many films, most of them uncredited. Additionally, Birnbaum provided employment for every sound professional to work in New York from the 1970s until he retired. Sound One had virtually no competition until c5 opened its doors in 1989 under the direction of supervising sound editor Skip Lievsay and Foley artist Ron Bochar. Tom Fleischman, who had built the first sound library for Birnbaum back in 1970, stayed at Sound One—having earned both Academy Awards and Emmys as a rerecording mixer.
