
Burgis considers himself an “alchemist” when it comes to creating Foley. He subscribes to the suspension of disbelief, and when it comes to discerning what sound components to put together for a Foley cue, “if it sounds right and is telling the story I want to make, I use it.” He does not concern himself with using a recognizable prop for a sound if “another object has a better dynamic to it.” He has several favorite props that he uses frequently: a Zippo lighter he bought when he was 19 while in the Royal Air Force; his grandmother’s purse—“it’s got the most lovely snap;” his son’s wallet from his teen years; some Hush Puppies shoes that he has used for Daniel Radcliffe in the Harry Potter films—“he has dyslexia and has a funny walk;” and some moccasins made by Clarks.

Meltem Baytok and Richard James Hinton are Foley artists who primarily work in Bristol. Baytok is originally from Turkey and learned Foley in the United Kingdom before she could speak English fluently. She relied on reading actors’ faces, emotions, and reactions to understand how to do Foley. “It’s like being deaf and reading their bodies like sign language,” says Baytok.

Hinton edits his Foley as well as performs it. His Foley mixers originally worked on a PC-based digital system called Pyramix—from Switzerland—but now they work on Pro Tools to conform with industry standards. Hinton often works without cue sheets and pays close attention to the microphone technique when he records. In a smaller room, he positions the microphone more closely, but when he works on a larger Foley stage, he likes more distance on the microphone.

## ONCE UPON A TIME IN THE BOOT: ITALY

When one thinks about Italian sound, the name that probably comes to mind is Sergio Leone. His films featured sound effects, Foley, and replaced dialogue to emphasize the narrative. Leone and Ennio Morricone, his composer, utilized a technique for scoring *Once Upon a Time in the West* (1968) before shooting a frame of film. The actors performed the scenes with the prerecorded score playing on the set to enhance the style, pace, and drama of the film. Italy has four Foley artists who are considered the veterans of the craft: Aldo Ciorba, Luciano Anzellotta, Italo Cameraccanna, and Sergio Basili. Foley and special sound effects for Leone’s films were performed by Ciorba, Anzellotta, and Cameraccanna. Basili began his career a bit after the first three but is included in the list of the earliest Italian Foley artists. Ciorba’s son, Marco Ciorba, continues his father’s tradition and techniques

Prior to the elder Ciorba, one of the sound creators was Tonino Cocciuttuolo. In Italy, the tradition of what corresponds to Foley began in the early twentieth century. Cocciuttuolo was famous for his performed sound effects. So, too, was Aldo Ciorba—who studied under Cocciuttuolo—and now Marco Ciorba continues as a highly respected Italian Foley artist.

In Italy, the practice of the craft varied from both the French and the American in one very specific way: the artist would sometimes go to the set and watch the production as it was being shot. This allowed the artist to be familiar with the sets, characters, and style of the film. Additionally, the Foley artist had a closer relationship with the director than is enjoyed in the United States. There was, and still is, a sense of collaboration with the director on the larger films.

Another Foley artist in Rome is Mauro Eusepi, who began his career at Cine Citta where he discovered sound as an important component of filmmaking. After studying new media and the science of sound at University of Rome Tor Vergata, Eusepi began working as an intern in 2005 at Dreamsound, a postproduction sound facility, and in 2009 he committed to a career as a freelance Foley artist. Presently he has his own Foley stage on the east side of Rome in the Pigneto neighborhood. Eusepi agrees with Aldo that recording Foley outside is a preferred method for more naturalistic sound and for bigger props, as the stage itself is rather small and better suited for footsteps and small-prop work. Eusepi prefers sound that resembles production as much as possible: “\[y\]ou can tell the difference between production and artificial sound.” To that end, he will save as much of the production sound as possible, with the intention of having sound effects and Foley that “reproduce the color of the production sound.” He adds that the modern Italian director has altered the sound aesthetic from earlier Italian films by leaning toward using more production sound, as Italy’s signa ture of totally reconstructed sound tracks from beginning to end of the film has evolved over the last few decades.

At times, and perhaps due to the influence of Italian neorealism, the Foley would be performed in a natural setting—outside in a forest, as an example—to capture the right sound and ambience for the footsteps.

## DAS LAND DER DICHTER UND DENKER: GERMANY

Germany has a well-developed film industry, so it is no surprise that the country can boast several established Foley artists, such as Martin Langenbach and Peter Deininger. One of the more recent additions to Germany’s Foley artist stable is Niklas Matthaei, who has his own studio after working only a few years as a Foley artist. Matthaei had studied literature, philosophy, and music, but he changed his educational trajectory to enroll at the Akademie Deutsche POP in Berlin to study sound. Afterward, Matthaei tried to find work at various sound design and mixing facilities. He discovered he was better at performing and mixing Foley, and by 2019, he was working full time as a Foley artist.

In Germany, it is not unusual for an artist to self-record. While some German sound professionals use Nuendo, Matthaei uses Pro Tools, which has become the recognized industry standard. The cueing for Foley is part of the mixer’s responsibility, and there are usually two spotting sessions with the director. Matthaei does not have a favorite genre of film to work on, but he prefers “something with meaning that is challenging.” Additionally, he loves to spend time getting a sound right because he prefers to not have to redo or alter it. “I am not really good at compromising.” While some believe a Foley artist should use the exact prop that appears on screen, and others might “do everything with that one key,” Matthaei considers himself “somewhere in between” and prefers to receive much clarity and information from the director “to get the drama right.” He enjoys doing the small and sensitive props that add nuance to a scene. When it comes to performing the footsteps, he believes he should “give as much as the actors on set” to get “emotion in the steps.”
