
Robert Deschaine, a veteran Foley, ADR, and rerecording mixer, was mixing Foley on a movie, and the editor wanted fresh grass for the Foley every day. Fresh sod was brought in, and Deschaine carefully watered it every night and put it under globe lights. At the end of three nights, the grass was dead and did not sound right. Someone said, “Why don’t we use quarter-inch tape and put a cloth on it?” They did so, and it was remarkably realistic. Sometimes we *all* overthink this process.

When working on *Antichrist* (2009), Julien Naudin relates that the director, Lars von Trier, wanted the sound of the inside of a man’s body. Naudin said, “I could not do this, of course.” So, he put a microphone inside a raw chicken and another microphone on the floor close to the chicken. He then manipulated the chicken to give von Trier the sound he wanted. Naudin considers von Trier to be easy to work with because “he knows what he wants. We have many brainstorms before the movie.” Most directors are not as involved with Foley as von Trier, but he has a strong sense of what he wants from film sound. Naudin is immensely creative and knows exactly how to give von Trier anything he wants.

## YOU DO NOT KNOW WHAT IS GOING TO HAPPEN

Many years ago, I worked with a young, but smart editor who was new to supervising at the time. He spent all his time on the stage and micro-managed every cue. It was a frustrating experience for everyone, because no one can be in another person’s head and know exactly what it is they are imagining. Additionally, try as he did to express just how he wanted every prop to sound, it seemed that he used every word in the sound lexicon to no avail. After hours trying to decipher his concepts, we started getting silly with our humor to get through the day. Finally, after thinking our expertise had been ignored all day, we began to believe we were building a rapport with the editor. But we were wrong. The clarifying moment occurred when he commented on how he wanted the street to sound. He said quite seriously, “Just junk it up.”

Another experience working with a Foley editor on the Foley stage during a session had to do with replacing some footsteps. We were nearing the end of our work for the day when an editor came to the stage and asked us to replace a few of the steps in the middle of a scene. She did not want the entire walk, just a few of the steps in the middle of the walk itself. There was a picture edit that changed the scene significantly, and the footsteps no longer matched the picture. The problem for us was that since we had finished walking all the characters, the stage had flooded and that particular surface had been destroyed. I suggested we rewalk the entire scene on a different surface. My concern was that the new footsteps would not match the old ones. She rejected that idea. So, I obediently replaced just the few steps she wanted. I still do not know how that turned out. The essential lesson from these incidents is this: while a Foley artist may have one perspective of how to solve an issue, the editor may have another idea.

What is important to take away from all of these examples of unpredictable instances that have occurred is this: you do not know what is going to happen, and you cannot control how other people are going to respond. You do not know what crazy ideas might come at you that you are supposed to implement with no explanation, nor are you always going to be respected for your opinion or expertise. You will have to find your own way to make peace with that part of being an artist. Unless you are going to be a solo filmmaker who does everything yourself, someone, at some time, is going to pull the rug out from under you or cause you to doubt yourself. Being a Foley artist, or any kind of artist, requires you to interpret the ideas of others who might not acknowledge what you can do. It is a life lesson we must all learn to grapple with.

Robert Mott expresses it best when describing his ultimate responsibility as a soundman. He says, “I had to fill in what they imagined.” When asked, “What was your favorite part about doing sound effects?” His reply, which speaks for all who do Foley today, was, “I think the creative challenge.”
