
Sometimes, Foley artists have to walk more than one character at once. This may seem nonsensical at first, but there is a reason for the practice and a method to the madness. When confronted with a scene with lots of background actors (BGs), an artist does not want to walk each character separately. They are not important to the scene and there is no point in wasting tracks. So, artists make sounds that cover more than one person. It is often a good idea to approach this in one of two ways. One way is that one member of the Foley team picks a generic pair of shoes that works for most of the people for one track, and the other (assuming they work in twos) does the track in another generic-sounding pair. The second approach is to have one kind of shoe on one foot and another kind on the other foot. This is tricky but can be effective. It just takes practice. Some Foley artists can make a series of sounds with their feet that are generic but work for crowds. It looks ridiculous, but in the playback it sounds reasonable. Whatever works.

Some Foley artists can utilize the various parts of the foot. This may sound a little strange, but when considering a person who has a gift for walking characters or has trained as a dancer, it makes sense. The foot has more than just a sole. There is the ball of the foot, the heel of the foot, and the two sides. The foot can be manipulated in many ways. A foot can be moved in lots of peculiar ways to obtain different sounds in a shoe or to make footsteps seem less predictable and contrived. One can walk on the insides of the foot and the outsides of the foot, and one can emphasize the ball, the heel, the flat footfall, or the heel-toe footfall. Just play with the sound. Any person who truly wants to investigate all the ways to walk a character will, at some time or other, experiment with how the foot can actually move. Be curious and inventive. What complicates all footsteps cues is one fundamental Foley technique: Foley artists cannot walk forward and travel. Typically, they must walk in place so they are positioned near the microphone. Thus, they have to learn how to make the footsteps sound natural without traveling.

Recently, I discovered that Julien Naudin, at times, has attached a small microphone to the top of his shoe to allow a sense of amplification to the footsteps that sounds hyperreal and larger than life. He has also recorded footsteps utilizing this technique while using a second boom microphone to simultaneously record in the more conventional fashion. Sometimes, he records Foley outside in the wild. He attaches a small microphone to his shoes and walks in a forest while the mixer records his footsteps. Foley artists like Naudin do not let conventions limit their possibilities. Naudin works in Europe and is trusted by the directors to experiment and be innovative.

Another Foley artist who has utilized microphones on his shoes is Richard James Hinton. For the film *Marathon Boy* (2010), he performed a stylized close-up of the boy running. Hinton clipped two lapel microphones to his laces and taped the cords up his legs.

Joan Rowe, a Foley artist since 1978, began her career in Foley at Gomillion Sound accidentally while auditioning as a voice actor. Ted Gomillion, the owner of the studio and a rerecording mixer, liked her sync and asked if she wanted to learn Foley. He accompanied her to a strange room, and they watched some woman, behind a glass window, throwing a car door around and stomping in dirt. Joan thought to herself, “Ooh, I am an artist,” and thought this strange act was beneath her. Then she remembered she had two small children to support and was cleaning houses for a living—film folks often do different jobs to support themselves between gigs—and perhaps she should rethink what she now refers to as her “arrogance.”

What Rowe had to learn about performing footsteps should help in understanding that it takes time to get good at Foley. She had a great deal of trouble learning how to get her footsteps to match those of the people on the screen. She was just beginning her career in Foley and had discovered in her first session how hard it was to perform footsteps properly. Her first day on the Foley stage, she faked her way through the day and vowed to learn how to walk before her next session, which was weeks later. Rowe had found performing prop cues to be easier. This, however, is the typical scenario for a beginning Foley artist. It is easier to relate to making sense of props and how to manipulate them for a reasonable sound than to walk in sync with someone else. Rowe practiced walking like other people on the streets when she was out. She turned on her television and practiced walking to all the characters on the show. She did this for weeks on end. When she was called back in to work, she had some sense of how to walk her characters. However, as Rowe relates it,

> I could not get my feet to walk “heel-toe.” I was just flapping around. It was hard to control my feet. John Post could manipulate his feet many different ways. He could contort his feet. I could not. So, I decided to practice and practice until I got better. Now, my footsteps are really good. But it was not easy for me.

Most Foley artists have expressed a similar frustration. It is not as alien to learn how to perform props, because at one time or another most of the ordinary prop cues one does on the stage mimic what we have done in our own lives. Creating footsteps, however, is different. To perform footsteps in sync, with an awareness of each impact of each step, and make those steps fit the action on screen are less familiar to most of us—that is, except for those who have been dancers. Susan Fitz-Simon reveals that she “practiced a couple of hours a day” to perfect her footsteps Foley. Be patient. Any art takes time to master. Learn from Fitz-Simon and Rowe. It takes tenacity, patience, and practice.

A final thought about footsteps from Robin Harlan and Sarah Monat, who work as a team at Sony Pictures: different supervising sound editors and directors have different tastes about how footsteps should be performed. An example they relate is Quentin Tarantino, who likes his Foley sharp and aggressive and likes the footsteps that way as well. Supervisor Randy Thom likes to hear the surface under the shoe (this is very much a Walter Murch aesthetic), so they are careful not to play the shoe as having too much personality with Thom. Wiley Stateman likes to have the Foley artists carry props and walk just like the characters, so if the character is carrying a golf bag while she is walking, the Foley art ist does too. However, Harlan leaves us with this: “Some people really like it vanilla.”

## [MUST YOU DANCE?](#content.xhtml#bck_sec8_3)

Jerry Trent had been a dancer in many musicals. Raised in Illinois, he moved to New York and became a Broadway and touring dancer. He then went to Los Angeles to appear in the film *The One and Only, Genuine, Original Family Band* (1968). The dancers were asked to Foley their dance footsteps in postproduction. This was Jerry’s introduction to postproduction sound. He continued to make a career as an on-screen dancer and off-screen Foley dancer until he made the transition to being a Foley artist and added the more traditional sounds to his skill set. Jerry has been an organized and disciplined artist, with Foley bags that have categories and compartments for all his tools. Many Foley artists are less organized than Jerry. Some are quite the opposite. But Jerry has taken that famous dancer’s discipline to the ultimate in his approach to his work.
