
If building a floating floor is impractical, rugs positioned inside the Foley pits to deaden any sounds can be effective. Baffles and rugs should be placed under the microphone stands as well. The Foley mixer will be the best guide for determining what works.

Jody Thomas was a story editor who is now a Foley artist. She cut her Foley teeth at Warner Brothers on many television shows. She now specializes in animated television shows, which she Foleys on her own Foley stage next to her house. She has confronted all of the issues of building a stage from inception to practical use. She began with a large, prebuilt shed as the shell for her building and used all the wisdom of the best experts to build herself a stage so she could work at home and be available to her daughter. Because Thomas does both animated television shows and feature films, she needed a stage that was large enough to operate in but small enough to be economical. Her approach is useful to any film school or independent filmmaker who wants to have a workable arrangement that sounds good.

First, Thomas went through the process of acquiring the proper building permits. She then selected her team to construct and wire the stage. She carefully crafted where her Foley pits would be, where to build the water pit, where she would store her props, and what level of technology she would use.

Thomas has constructed her Foley pits so they are built into the floor. Some of the pits have some air under them, but her dirt pit is actually dirt on a dirt foundation. Thomas’s home is out in a more rural area of Los Angeles, so she does not have a lot of street traffic. Also, her home is built up in the hills, so her floors are away from the street.

Another innovation is that she has several exchangeable surfaces that can slide into the top of a pit. This allows her to have more surface choices without the need for a big room with several custom pits. One is a shallow splash pit that can have a metal grate over it for a hollow metal surface.

Thomas has utilized two clever ideas to maximize the storage space for her props. Her room has a Rubbermaid garage system rail that looks like crown molding. She can clip all sorts of things onto the rail. Also, she has pegboard on her wall. Not only is pegboard acoustically absorptive but she can also hang props on it.

For her mixing booth glass, Thomas has followed conventional Foley stage wisdom. She has two panes of glass, one angled, so the sound waves do not reflect either in the stage or between the panes.

Thomas uses a TV monitor for her picture. This monitor has an articulating arm, so she can move the monitor to any angle in the room. This allows her to use all her space for performing cues. Monitors give off less noise than a typical television, so while it may be tempting to get a fancy television, think sonically. Get a monitor that can be moved around. That is a simple and effective solution.

The door to her stage is a double door. This is an essential element to diminish the effect of noise from the mixing booth and from outside. One door will not be enough to keep noise from leaking. Two doors together, constructed with a bit of air in between them, offer the best chance of keeping sounds isolated. It helps if all doors that lead to the outside or to other rooms are double doors.

## IT’S THE PITS

Foley pits are ideally set into the ground. The size can vary, but keep in mind that at some time more than one person is going to be standing in a pit or on a surface together. Thus, the size needs to easily accommodate that scenario. The location of pits in relation to each other is a matter of personal taste. It is helpful to think about what surfaces one travels to and from most frequently and put them next to each other. Keep in mind that anything with metal on it will cause “ring off” on a cue. That will be a constant problem. It is best to have any pit with metal away from most of the others. Rugs or foam baffles can be used to cover the metal.

Some artists like to have exterior surface pits near each other and interior surfaces near each other. They also like to have a concrete surface near one or two interior surfaces, for example, wood and linoleum or tile. It really is just logic. Most live-action shows will, at some point, have a character go inside from outside and vice versa.

Some artists like to have two dirt pits, if possible. One would contain very gritty, coarse dirt that has a noticeably dry sound. Another, however, might contain a softer kind of dirt. Try decomposed granite or brown dirt. Potting soil is not recommended. It is moist, which leads to all sorts of things that grow and cause an odor, and it has a soft sound that has virtually no character at all. It is a good idea to have at least one gravel pit. Foley artists can use fine pea gravel and add some of the gravel to a dirt pit to create more interest to the dirt. Rather than have large rock gravel sitting in a pit, store a bag of it for occasional use. Buy the dirt and gravel at any large home improvement supplies store. A small section of real, poured asphalt is a great addition. Put it near the concrete.

In addition to a concrete surface, there should be at least one hard wood surface, but two hard woods with contrasting sounds would be better. If possible, it is terrific to have a boardwalk surface. This surface needs to be built over a pit so there is echo and life to the sound. Another practical surface is an industrial pallet. It is also useful to have a section of tile and/or linoleum. Metal surfaces are terrific, if they can be built in. However, it can also be useful to have many different types of metal surface plates that are easily stored, because metal surfaces vary greatly in the movie world. Think about the escalator, the subway grate, the bus, metal bridges, etc. It might not be possible to build all of these sounds with a small room and budget. *Be practical*. Build what is used the most and store the rest. Usually, when creating something futuristic, epic, or animated, a Foley artist needs several possible metal sounds. Keep in mind that metal reflects sound and adds a quality to any prop recorded around it. It is a good idea to have absorptive material around metal or drape cloth over it when not in use.

It is helpful to have a mat to use for grass. Some Foley artists have found that the grass mats used for display around Easter or summertime are perfect. Wholesalers who specialize in display materials for retailers usually have several kinds of grass-like materials. Astroturf is not the same surface and sounds like what it is. Previously, many artists used quarter-inch tape. It was fast, accessible, and inexpensive. With the improvements in technology, sound recording is so sensitive now that sometimes quarter-inch tape sounds rough and scratchy; it is fine for augmenting certain types of foliage, but for grass it may not be acceptable. If one is relying on quarter-inch tape, it might help the sound to put it under a piece of cloth in the dirt pit.

Any Foley stage needs various carpet and rug surfaces. Industrial carpet pieces and various throw rugs are essential. Some stages have carpeting built over pit covers, which is useful for minimizing the reflection of the covers themselves but is not always useful to walk on. Variety and versatility are the goal.
