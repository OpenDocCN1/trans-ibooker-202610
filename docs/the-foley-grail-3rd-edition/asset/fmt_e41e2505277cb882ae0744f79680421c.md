
Cornelia Baumgartner, who lives in Bregenz, Austria, near Switzerland and Germany, studied visual media literacy and is a composer and singer. These combined areas of expertise have led Baumgartner to a career in various areas of sound design. Beginning as a film composer, Baumgartner relates that she expanded her work into sound design, using sound libraries and creating “dark atmospheric” soundtracks. Her experience as a pianist led her to working with midis and digital audio workstations (DAW).

One of Baumgartner’s vocations has been to design sound effects and Foley for audio guides for cities. “I wanted to create cinema in the ear,” she remarks. In these audio guides, actors portray historical characters, and Baumgartner incorporates the voice acting in her projects. Bregenz is a small city with a population of about 30,000, and there are no Foley stages nearby, so Baumgartner performs her Foley in the quiet areas of her home.

Baumgartner finds projects in animation and commercials for advertising agencies, and she has recently designed sound for an episodic series. In addition to taking a sound design workshop in Bristol, England, she visited a Foley artist in Vienna on a small Foley stage. Through her studies and her professional experiences, she finds she is on “the search for the perfect sound” and enjoys “adding more levels of sound” to add a sense of hyperreality. One illustration of her approach to layering sound is the sound of a fishing reel used for the squeakiness of a mountain bike. Baumgartner summarizes, “in every object lives a sound.”

## THE FORMER YUGOSLAVIA: SERBIA
Misa Jakovljevic, Serbia’s primary Foley artist, was introduced to what he calls “Foley magic” in 1995 when working as an assistant engineer in a postproduction facility. At the time, Serbia was in the midst of political and social upheaval and Jakovljevic was a young man. He made the “mistake” of walking onto the Foley stage and saw what the three Foley artists—who worked in the dark so as to keep their craft mysterious—were doing. From that day on, Jakovljevic waited patiently for his opportunity to engage with the three famous artists who had worked in the former Yugoslavia and had been the first Foley artists in his country.

Marko Rodic, Sinisa Jovanovic, and Vladimir Tomanovic were the three Foley artists on the stage that day. Jakovljevic was working in the capacity of what would have been a recordist in the United States at the time—keeping track of the magnetic film stock, coordinating the cues on the cue sheets, and doing the general maintenance of the administrative duties in the control room. His lucky break into Foley “magic” came one day when the artists were having difficulty creating an appropriate prop for a “walking tree.” Jakovljevic said, “I have \[an\] idea. I need light!” Rodic let him into the sacred space of the stage, and after Jakovljevic created the prop for the artists, they told him to get out! But he began training with the three of them.

Rodic was the owner of the studio—Markoni—and Jakovljevic later bought the studio and continues the Foley tradition in Serbia. The economy is difficult in Serbia, and, for this reason, he has been the only Foley artist in Serbia. No one has the time to train for free instead of getting a job that would pay immediately. Jakovljevic loves what he does and is a jokester. “All day I stay alone in \[a\] darkened room and think how to make what \[is\] in \[the\] director’s mind and I must be quiet. When I walk out of that room, I like talking!”

## MUSING ABOUT MOSFILM: RUSSIA
During the Stalinist era in Soviet Russia, there was a renaissance in culture. According to Pavel Doreuli—who learned his sound design skills while working in the sound library at Mosfilm—the history of Russian sound design is uneven, but it has some interesting peaks. This Stalinist renaissance was “not from \[the\] previous Russia, but from \[the\] new Russia,” says Doreuli, “new artists, new composers, totally new music!” Mosfilm was infused with state money to develop a national film industry. While there were no specific terms that delineated sound designer or supervisor in Russia, there was the development of the sound engineer. This profession included anyone who could edit or design the sound effects for film. The 1970s also proved to be a period when Mosfilm first began to categorize the sound effects library in any sort of codified fashion. Two women were hired, and they stayed at Mosfilm until the 2000s putting voice slates on each sound effect and devising a library system for editors.

The once dynamic Mosfilm became stagnant after the fall of Communism. In the 1990s, Mosfilm tried to cultivate the “Russian blockbuster,” beginning with *Nightwatch* (1994). However, the American blockbuster style did not coincide with the Russian cultural sensibility. The “Russian blockbuster” was a failure, but the American blockbuster remained popular.

In 2001, director Karen Shakhnazarov began to revitalize Mos-film. He conglomerated several independent film companies together and began the task of digitizing the sound effects library. Doreuli was charged with the digitizing task. As part of his tenure at Mosfilm, Doreuli worked with various sound engineers and encountered the various Foley artists that worked there. These Foley artists have stayed at the forefront of Russian filmmaking from the 1970s until contemporary times. Recently, two of these women, Ala Meichick and Faina Yampolskaya, have begun training new, young artists. Doreuli recalls that the most famous Foley artists in Russia were Sergei Figner and his father, Edgar.

## TRANSCONTINENTAL: TURKEY
When Meltem Baytok began her Foley career in Bristol, UK, there were no Foley artists in her home country of Turkey. She began her career working on nature documentaries, and as her career has expanded, she has contributed to British television shows and films. Although she attempted to bring Foley to Turkey in the earlier years of her work in film, she found the climate inhospitable to the endeavor.

Over the past decade, there has been an expansion of professional sound and Foley in Turkey. Aleyna Seymen, a 23-year-old Foley artist in Turkey, explains the lack of Foley in Turkey: “\[n\]obody in Turkey wants to be a Foley artist.” Seymen knows Baytok and agrees that the United Kingdom was the best place for her to begin her career. However, Seymen began her career as an assistant to other artists to learn Foley, while she researched Foley artists in other countries. Her ambition to excel in the nascent beginnings of the craft in Turkey propelled her to write to veteran British Foley artist Peter Burgis to ask for guidance. Burgis encouraged his colleague Paula Boram to travel to Istanbul to mentor Seymen. According to Seymen, there are two other Foley artists in Turkey, both of them male. As the only female artist, she experiences a sense of sexism in the field of sound and explains, “\[t\]hey always try to give me up” when working with male editors or mixers. After several frustrating attempts to be taken seriously and allowed to make creative decisions, Seymen made two key decisions to enhance her professional agency: (1) work with female Foley mixers and (2) work closely with her directors, who trust her taste and artistry. She prefers to work closely with her director to discern “what he wants me to feel” with the Foley. To that end, she enjoys having the director on the stage and perceives Foley as a performance that excels under a director’s expressed vision. Seymen has worked most often for the two sound companies Melodika and 1000 Volt Production. The budgets for Foley tend to be small and there are gaps between projects, but this is not unusual in a younger film sound community.
