Foley artists enhance the clothes that the characters are wearing. Typically, there is one pass, at the end of the recorded tracks, that is a dedicated cloth pass. An artist has a variety of fabrics with which to work. The primary cloth pass coordinates with the dialogue of the characters and is mixed through the center speaker at the final dub. However, if there is a specialty cloth, such as a leather jacket or wedding dress, that cloth will be treated as a separate prop and will be assigned to a different track in the session. This being said, some artists prefer to separate out various tracks for cloth for important characters, as Pakistani artist Kashif Ejaz does. The danger with this practice is that it can create too much “whooshing” noise on the Foley tracks that will need to be eliminated in the final mix. It is always a good practice to be judicious and mindful about cloth noise, both performed and from one’s own clothing.

Robert Rutledge is credited by some in the Hollywood community with the innovation of the single cloth pass. Up until the early 1980s, it was customary to have cloth that a Foley artist would carry in your hands and move while walking a character. This is what Jack Foley did. Bob had affected other artists with this “new” way of utilizing cloth. My own introduction to Rutledge included a correction in my use of cloth. As I worked with him on a film, he watched me holding a piece of cloth while performing a footstep cue, and then he showed me how to use one piece of cloth on a separate track to follow the general movement of the characters as they walked or made bold gestures. He explained to me that it would fit in with the dialogue. Rutledge, an experienced sound editor as well as gifted Foley artist, had a better perspective than I had on how cloth mixed in with dialogue.

## PROP CHARACTER

Actors handle props differently. Depending on the training of the actor and the character portrayed, along with the conventions of the time period in the film and the style of the genre, there can be quite a few variations in how to do the props the actors handle. In a comedy, the props sometimes are supposed to get a laugh. In *Young Frankenstein* (1974), the candles in the famous “put the candle back” scene sound unrealistic and heightened for broad comedy. In *Glory* (1989), when the young soldier is asked to load and shoot his gun over and over, it is clean, loud, and dramatic. In *Malice* (1993), when the Anne Bancroft character is pouring and drinking her single malt scotch, it is not just a drink. It is her best friend, and she handles it accordingly. The attentive Foley artist will take into account all of the character and dramatic purpose of the prop and not get into a rote performance.

Most Foley artists have an affinity for performing props before they are comfortable with footsteps. Both David Fein and Ken Dufva found props to be easier in the beginning. “I think Ken has an instinctual creativity about what to use. It probably started to feel natural pretty quickly,” says Fein, “Our approaches are pretty similar.” Jody Thomas prefers to do props for animation. “It keeps me on my toes,” she says. “I love figuring it out!” Props are personal for artists, and their particular methods for performing them may vary profoundly yet be equally effective. As Ronni Brown, Foley artist at Skywalker Sound remarks, “how would I take it apart? How would I put it together?”

The quality that causes the most anxiety for the beginning Foley artist is almost invariably how to walk a footstep cue or do a prop cue and be in sync with the action on screen. The ability to match the footfall of the character or the action of the prop on screen is enough to cause any beginning Foley artist some trauma. Even the most experienced and talented Foley artists whose names appear on screen for all of the major films have had this anxiety.

The first time I entered a Foley stage was an unforgettable and strange experience. The year was 1980. The world had just discovered who had shot JR Ewing on the television show *Dallas*. I walked into this dark and cluttered room where Duane Hensel, who was a veteran Foley artist and sound editor and who had worked on all the Universal television shows of the 1970s, was on the stage waiting for his new trainee. In those days, all Foley artists were called “walkers” because the majority of the craft focused on replacing the footsteps of characters, with props cues taking a lesser priority. He proceeded to guide me through some of my first Foley cues. One of my cues that day was to drop a remote for an explosive on the ground for the latest episode of the Ewing clan. I do not know if my work was good, awful, or what, but I can say that I felt I was in over my head. I was nervous, timid, and enthralled. I was actually working on a television show for money doing this strange new craft. By the end of the day, I was exhausted from the excitement and stress. I was pretty sure I would never hear from these people again. But I did hear from them again, and, thus, my temporary gig turned into a permanent career.

## PATIENCE AND PRACTICE ARE YOUR FRIENDS

Why do I relate this story? Because the pressure any beginning Foley artist will feel trying to do Foley might be as terrifying as it was for me. I got better at it and so will anyone who persists. Patience and practice are our friends. Good Foley sync takes both, as do most skills in life.

When I first worked on television shows, Foley artists were working with simple and primitive technology compared to what is used now. Normally, there were three tracks on which to record. This meant that all the footsteps, the props, and the cloth were often combined. Foley artists worked with partners, and the two would usually walk two characters or more at one time on one track. The projectors used did not have high speed, so all of the Foley artists in this era, from the 1970s into the mid-1980s, had to work in real time and then rewind to the beginning of the reel, which was a 10-minute reel, also in real time. Thus, work procedures and even coffee breaks were dictated by the rewind times.

In my early days as a Foley artist, my Foley partner (sometimes Duane Hensel, sometimes John Post, and, most often as time went on, Jim Bailey) and I would pick characters and walk them simultaneously. We would usually do two tracks of footsteps and one track of whatever props we could manage that were not going to be edited into the sound effects tracks. These cues took up all three tracks. Out of necessity, the cloth movement was added in with the footsteps tracks. We would hold a piece of cloth, a pair of softened jeans or some similar fabric, and move in sync to our footsteps. It was a simple technique and, by today’s standards, rather crude. As time went on, we recorded to the three tracks (called full coat) and one additional track (single stripe) for a grand total of four tracks of sound. We could separate out a character now and then or record more props. It was during this addition of the fourth magnetic stripe of audio that the practice of a single cloth pass began.
