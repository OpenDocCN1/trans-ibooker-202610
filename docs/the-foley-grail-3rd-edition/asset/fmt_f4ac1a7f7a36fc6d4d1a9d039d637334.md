
If someone decides to pursue a career as a Foley artist, there comes a moment when they realize that the responsibility to create the recipe for the sound falls on the artist and not on someone else. This is when they must ask the question, “Am I a chef or a cook?” This is not a question to be taken lightly for, in reality, all Foley artists are some combination of both. However, if an artist is at the stage where you are asking how to make a sound or how to approach a particular technique in Foley, you are leaning toward being a cook, which is a great place to start. This chapter is meant to help guide a new artist along with more specificity.

[Chapter 9](#Ch09.xhtml), which details the specific use and manipulations of props, gives some overall guidance regarding how Foley artists work with props that are used repeatedly in films. I recommend a review that chapter for assistance with technique, as well as this one, while developing skill with props. [Chapter 8](#Ch08.xhtml) is meant to give insight and guidance with advice from Foley artists regarding footsteps.

If, however, you feel you need some real basic ingredients and recipes that can be broken down, either because you are beginning the process or because you are finding gaps in your development in Foley, this chapter may be of help to you. A word of caution to all readers: no one can tell you how to be a chef. You have to develop your own style, your own flavor, and, eventually, your own recipes for how to create sounds for various films, television shows, games, animation projects, and commercials or advertising spots. At some point, you must let go of any idea that there is actually a rubric or hard and fast rule to follow. There is not. Foley is an art. Art has no rules, only guidelines. So, let us begin with some ingredients, then a few basic recipes. I encourage you to experiment and become the great chef you can become in Foleydom. The true stars of Foley did not copy anyone else. They were bold and tried things others did not dare. What is the worst that can happen, really?

## RECIPES

1. **Cop gear: baseball glove (or leather purse), keys, handcuffs, dead bolt**
Hold in your hands in a way that feels comfortable. Be sure to arrange the configuration to feature the leather of the baseball glove to get leather squeak. Only feature handcuffs if it seems appropriate to the scene. Otherwise, keep in mind that metal records hot. Handcuffs and keys will record hotter than the leather.

2. **Gun rattle: dead bolt, door handle, starter pistol or handgun that is plugged**
Hold the item lightly so it has a little bit of “gun life” to it. Too much rattle will seem silly and unreal. The point is to add a sense of drama to the scene. If needed to, hold two items together but do not rub them together. This will take a little practice.

3. **Eyeglasses: either dark or reading glasses**
The trick to having glasses sound natural is to control them with one’s hands so they sound like they are against skin. Do not have them dangling unless they are dangling in the scene. If they are on a desk, be sure to have the sound of the desk as they are picked up. Always consider what they are on.

4. **Paper sounds: newspaper, sheets of paper, writing paper**
The trick to paper is to use it lightly. It is surprising how little it takes for paper to record. Be as specific as possible. Do not overdo and be precise. Even when touching the paper, be very specific and clear about the touch. I suggest playing back the paper cue every time until you are certain you know what you are doing with paper. Newspaper in particular is likely to record more crinkles than expected.
