
When I was a young dancer in training, I performed in musical productions in San Diego. One of my dance teachers, who also directed some of the shows, was a former *Our Gang* actor, Bonnie Jean Gore. “Coach” Gore told me stories about how she replaced Ann Miller’s dancing sounds in postproduction, uncredited. At the time, I was unaware of this practice, but once I began replacing Foley dance steps for feature films, I gained a greater appreciation of what this skill entailed. MGM choreographer Hermes Pan reputedly performed dance Foley for Fred Astaire and Eleanor Powell. What is uncontested, and totally in character, is that Gene Kelly did his own dance Foley. Mr. Kelly was a perfectionist who insisted on doing his own stunt work and performed the recorded sounds for his dancing as well. Dancing is not always live action, and not always human. In the animated film *Cats Don’t Dance* (1997), the animals danced and the sounds were performed on the Foley stage. It was the last film project for Mr. Kelly. He was the consultant for the animated dance steps.

In the 1960s and 1970s, sound editors were in the union, but the newly codified job of Foley walker was not in any union. This remained true until the summer of 2006, when “Foley artist” was finally included as an official category of the Motion Picture Editors Guild. Until that time, any Foley artists that were in the Editors Guild were accepted in the category of sound editor. Some earlier Foley artists who had been dancers were in the Screen Actors Guild.

During Jack Foley’s time at Universal, there were changes in the venue where the sound would be recorded. Most of the time, Jack would be on a sound stage. An edifice was built for him to isolate his sounds. This would be known as “Foley’s room” or “Foley’s stage.” Now, the studio where Foley is performed is officially a Foley stage—an obvious homage. Desilu Studios, which had originally been RKO, built the first official record ing studio for Foley, which, according to Hollywood oral tradition, is when the term became official.

Ken Dufva and David Lee Fein for many years were the “longest-lived Foley team” in the film industry, having worked together as Foley artists for 21 years until the late 1990s. They worked first at MGM and later at Paramount. They both entered the system at a time when studios had a standardized progression into virtually all jobs in filmmaking, and the unions and guilds had a strong influence on the training and job protection of employees. Thus, Dufva and Fein have, individually and together, quite interesting perspectives on the growth of and changes in the world of Foley from the 1960s to the present day.

Dufva was assigned the job of projectionist at MGM in 1965. He was part of the team at MGM for 11 years and, during this tenure, moved to the Foley stage as the regular projectionist. As was common practice in those days, there was a great deal of job security and protection through the unions, and Dufva was very secure at MGM. However, it was tricky to move from a position in one union to a position in another union. When the opportunity came for Dufva to move to sound as an editor and Foley walker, it took some time for the position to materialize. Dufva was making the move from Foley projectionist to replace the retiring Jack Morissette.

David Fein’s entrance into Foley actually started at Gomillion Sound. He had worked on small independent films as an editor and moved into Foley as a natural next step. When he got the opportunity to audition to replace Scott Perry at MGM, Fein was very nervous. Although he had done some Foley at Gomillion, MGM presented a huge opportunity, and he did not want to make a bad showing. Fein remembers “feeling the eyes of some persnickety person” on his back during the audition. The audition resulted in Fein’s hire as a sound editor who walked Foley, and within months, when Dufva joined the team, their partnership began. For the first two years, Dufva and Fein worked with a third veteran, Lovell Norman, until his retirement. Norman was then replaced by another Foley artist, who was subsequently replaced when he retired. Eventually, it was just the two of them—Dufva and Fein. Even as the rest of Hollywood adopted the standard two-person Foley artist convention, Dufva and Fein were the last to lose their third Foley artist. Jack Foley may have been the first person in Hollywood to create the role of the Foley artist, but MGM had expanded the role to be far more substantial.

“Kenny and Dave,” as those in the industry know them, are both gentle in demeanor, respectful of each other, and professional in their work ethic. They had a high regard for each other and never had any conflicts arise between them. They got so used to working together that they did not even have to discuss who would do which cue or how they would approach it. They had a truly functional Foley “marriage,” which was admired by all who knew of them or worked with them. They attribute this to a lack of competitiveness between them, and one can quickly see when the two are together that they genuinely like and admire each other. However, they also were considered one of the best teams in Foley, and their work was highly valued. They handled an abundance of major films, including *Robocop* (1987), *Ghost* (1990), and *The Godfather: [Part III](#part3.xhtml)* (1990), although until the late 1980s, screen credit was not regularly awarded to Foley artists. So, unless one does careful research or is from postproduction sound, they might never be aware of the number of films the team Foleyed. Dufva and Fein have also been eager contributors to any educational project regarding Foley, as they value passing on the traditions and informing others about the craft.

Dufva and Fein started at a time when Foley was recorded on one track. Most of the prop effects were edited in, so their task was more focused on the footsteps of the characters. Having a team of three people as walkers allowed many characters to be covered at one time. Because recording and rerecording technology was still rather simple, dubbing mixes did not include any stereo mixing or panning, as is now the common practice. As long as principal character footstep cues were kept together and background footsteps were kept together, characters could be combined or “married” easily. This, of course, changed as technology allowed more separation of characters and props.

MGM had a nine-person team on the Foley stage when Dufva and Fein began working together. There were three walkers, a projection-ist, a boom person, a mixer, a recordist, and two prop persons. This tradition conformed to who might be on a sound stage for production. But as the years progressed and the system evolved, the tradition became two walkers, a mixer, a recordist, and a projectionist. The stage began to accumulate props, and there was rarely a need for someone to go gather props during a session. When Dufva and Fein were not performing the Foley, they were editing as they were both editors as well as Foley artists.

Ross Taylor and Kitty Malone were a Foley artist team for over 20 years. Taylor had been a sound editor who had edited in his own footsteps. Similar to John Post, Taylor had also been allocated as the editor to do Foley footsteps for other editors. Malone was a dancer on *The* *Andy Williams Show*. When the *Tarzan* television series—which was filmed in Mexico—needed Foley footsteps, someone who knew Malone suggested she be hired to replace the sound for the jungle hero. She continued on as the Foley walker (uncredited) for the series, even as she continued dancing for *The Smothers Brothers Comedy Hour*.
