
Foley is in its nascent stage in Karachi, Pakistan. Kashif Ejaz began his career in Foley in the last decade is also a sound effects editor. As a child, Kashif cannot remember when he was not interested in sound. While working at a TV channel in Karachi and taking courses at a film school, he visited mixing stages in London, Istanbul, Bangkok, and Dubai. He observed the workflows of the various sound professionals to adapt their practices to his own sound editorial business. In addition to editing sound and performing Foley, Ejaz records the ADR for his projects. “Sometimes the directors are there, sometimes not.”

Pakistan’s show *Pepsi Battle of the Bands* (2017) on Pakistan Television Corporation gave Ejaz an opportunity to work as the recording engineer and postsound supervisor on the series. Pakistan’s small film industry allows Ejaz opportunities to wear several hats (editor, mixer, artist, musician), and he worked on *Zero Dark Thirty* (2012) recording special sound effects. While Ejaz enjoys his work in postproduction sound, he says that, in Foley, “footsteps are more challenging. Putting them into the scene is difficult.” He considers the distance the footsteps are from the screen, with the goal of having the footsteps fit in with production sound. Utilizing only one studio mic, he will add reverb on another track if he feels it is warranted.

Ejaz does not utilize one track for cloth, which is the more common standard for most Foley artists. Instead, he separates out characters’ clothing on several tracks. Because many Pakistani films require a large amount of ADR, the cloth tracks add life for each character. He emphasizes cloth in his animation projects.

## TAIWAN

Master Hu Ding Yi began working at Central Pictures Corporation in Taipei in 1975. Central Pictures was founded in 1953 and has produced films by some of Taiwan’s major directors, including Ang Lee and Edward Yang. Master Hu began as a studio assistant and learned production mixing and postproduction sound. His interest in Foley led him to watch and study carefully the artists at the studio, and by the early 1980s, he was working primarily as a Foley artist.

Master Hu, who communicated with me through translator Zi Xian Lin, told me that when he worked with analog sound four or five Foley artists might work at the same time to maximize the sounds in a cue; however, the transition to digital Foley mixing has altered this workflow. Now he normally works alone or with one assistant. He usually watches a cue one or two times before performing it. In the film *Vive l’Amour* (1994), he had to walk the main female character for 6–7 minutes in one take. He finds footsteps to be more natural for him than prop work: “It’s not hard to do sound, but you have to take it to your daily life, like unconsciously observing the sound around you,” says Master Hu.

Hu’s perspective is that the quality of the character performed is very important: “we have to really feel the character … like an angry man putting down a coffee cup.” He recalls a strange prop cue for the film *Who Killed Cock Robin* (2017), when for a gruesome scene he had to do the sound “for digging out a character’s eyeballs, and that’s something that does not exist in the sound library, so I had to create it.” He adds that the sound “must be vivid, and I like to challenge myself to make different sounds using diverse props.”

While Hu has worked on many Kung Fu movies in the past, he finds the emphasis on realistic films is the trend now in Taiwan. He performs Foley for films from Hong Kong, but he remains in Taiwan and does not travel.

When asked how Foley is different for television shows in Taiwan, Master Hu answered, “\[t\]here is no Foley for TV shows.” Although he has not worked on any of the musicals made in Taiwan or Hong Kong, he would like to do so.

Master Hu shares his philosophy about being a Foley artist:

> You have to have a steady and strong mind set. Do not panic. Sometimes you think you did great job but the \[mixer\] asked for one more take, you better answer positively “yes.” Do it again, if the \[mixer\] says it needs to be better. Otherwise, you can’t manipulate the actor’s emotions from the picture because you have \[an\] unstable mind.

Master Hu has some thoughts for those who want to become a Foley artist:

> After digitalized of sound workflow, we separate movement in many short parts and finish them one by one. Which is more friendly to \[a\] new learner but in my opinion, that’s not going to make you a great Foley artist. You have to capture the whole scene and do them within a take. When you learned how to manipulate each sound for a scene, that’s the moment that you know you are qualified for this job.

## LATIN AMERICA: CHILE AND BRAZIL

Latin America’s developing professional sound community has exploded recently and includes a plethora of Foley artists. One of these artists, Mauricio Casteñeda, lives in Chile and works in his home country as well as Brazil. Casteñeda’s father ran a radio station, which fueled his love for music, and after studying sound engineering, his interest in film sound led to him to work as a sound assistant during the late 1990s at Filmosonido in Santiago. When he watched the Foley artists on the Foley stage, “it was love at first sight,” says Casteñeda. Some of the earlier artists performed their Foley outside.

Daniele Fillios, an editor, said, “you are doing it wrong,” and some of them were sent to France to learn new techniques in late 1999 and early 2000. Roberto Espinoza, Casteñeda’s Foley teacher, was one of those who learned the French methods for Foley. Espinoza wanted to take on the mixing duties, and Casteñeda began his Foley career. He credits the mixers he has worked with for encouraging him and helping him improve his technique and art. In 2015, he left Filmosonido because home sound studios were hurting business; he remarks, “I had to start my own Foley studio.”

Casteñeda also works in Brazil, and as he notes, “Brazil did not have the passion for Foley,” so he travels from Chile to Brazil to perform his Foley. His enthusiasm for Foley and his desire to “be like another character in the movie” motivates him to “offer more … I try to add something … you have to have your moments.”

## GLOBAL PERSPECTIVE

International Foley artists provide different perspectives and insights regarding performing, mixing, and editing Foley. Many international Foley artists utilize techniques and methods similar to those of the United States, while others have adapted their own methods for performing Foley.

Expansion of our understanding of the cultural differences that exist in the world of Foley can enhance film literacy and enhance appreciation for artists outside of the United States.
