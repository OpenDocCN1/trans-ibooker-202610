
### [Multiple Depths, Layers, and Distances](#content.xhtml#bck_sec11_2_3)

The person recording the Foley has to be much more involved with the actual Foley process than with film or episodic television. To begin with, Deenen discovered that the use of one microphone—typically the method used to shoot films and television—would mean the Foley artist would have to perform a prop the exact same way and with the exact same ferocity and depth as all prior takes, but at different microphone distances and angles. This is a nearly impossible task. It quickly became clear that a mixer who could adapt their recording methods to utilize three or four microphones at a time, at various distances, and using varying settings would allow the artist to perform the action while the mixer recorded it on several tracks with the different distances and depth perceptions. This saved time and energy for the Foley artist. Few mixers, however, are ever required to develop such a skill.

David Jobe, who has many years in the chair mixing ADR and Foley for both features and television, was up for the challenge. When David Lee Fein was the Foley artist for a game, Jobe was mixing for him and Deenen wanted to try some different techniques with processing and microphone placement. Jobe was accommodating and innovative and caught on to what was needed. Both Jobe and Fein turned in fantastic work for Deenen. The qualities they displayed were innovation, a tireless sense of curiosity, and a desire to try something new. Fein, who is discussed in [Chapter 1](#Ch01.xhtml), is a veteran Foley artist who never loses the desire to jump in and have an adventure on the Foley stage. Soft-spoken and thoughtful, he masterfully handled everything Deenen wanted from Foley.

Foley artist Dan O’Connell can create magnificent props with whatever is around. He often surprises the sound supervisor with inventive ideas not considered previously. When Deenen first encountered O’Connell, it was to create the sound of a gigantic, creaky ship on an ocean. The problem? There was no visual referent to which O’Connell could relate. When O’Connell pulled out one small wooden chair, Deenen was concerned. He reminded O’Connell that this was a very big and creaky ship. O’Connell agreed. He said, “Trust me.” O’Connell knew his prop and got every possible creak at every possible volume imaginable. The lesson here is twofold: a gifted and experienced artist knows how to work the prop, and never assume that what something looks like is any indication of what it sounds like. What Deenen says about O’Connell now is that he is one of the best at bringing the kind of energy and vitality to Foley that is required for game sound.

Another Foley artist that understands the complexity and “punchy, big impacts” that Deenen says are necessary for the over-the-top Foley in games is Gary Hecker. Hecker has become the “go-to guy” for games, says Deenen. Hecker is particularly good at very boisterous and attention-getting Foley that works beautifully in the game world. He works very quickly and has a knack for making the Foley as loud and as big as necessary—which is essential in today’s cacophonous games. Many film Foley artists specialize in more naturalistic or understated Foley. Games and action films have cross-pollinated their sound aesthetics, so understatement is not appropriate for game Foley. As Foley has contributed more to game sound, Foley artists more accustomed to working on feature films and television find they are often asked to take on a game project. The approach is very different, because the artist must work in short cues that do not lead to a story necessarily but do contribute to the multiplicity of variations provided for the gamer. Each cue is miniscule in length compared to traditional Foley work and requires intense concentration, as each choice a player makes must include a variation in the Foley sounds performed.

### [Simply Put](#content.xhtml#bck_sec11_2_4)

Foley plays a part in all three aspects of sound design in game sound: interactive, cinematics, and interactive cinematics. The cinematics aspect of game sound is the most similar to films. The interactive, or “in-game,” aspect is the least, and the interactive cinematics is a hybrid—half like a film and half like an interactive. The tasks for Foley change depending on which of these three are being performed. The in-game aspect is the heaviest and most complex. This is the aspect that has the multiple choices that determine the direction in which the game will go next. Thus, the Foley required for this part of the game will be altered and processed. The cinematic aspect will be performed more as a film, with two artists working together, and the hybrid aspect will have both.

The preferred microphone to use when recording Foley for games is a shotgun microphone. It will pick up the sound without getting other ancillary noises. The type of microphone is not the primary challenge when recording and mixing Foley for games. The challenge is in utilizing multiple microphones at several distances and recording them all at once while processing them to get multiple sounds out of one performance.

Deenen used to have many racks of equipment. Now he uses Pro Tools with a lot of plug-ins. He finds that is all he really needs to do the work, and had found it unnecessary to invest in too much equipment. He believes it is better to get the right equipment and learn to use it properly.

When asked why it would not be easier to put sounds in a library and use them for the Foley, Deenen leaves us with this thought: “It takes more time to get it from a library. It is quicker to get an artist who can do it. And it sounds better.”

Ele Matelan and Joe Griffin work together as a Foley artist and Foley mixer team in Chicago. While there is a growing abundance of film work in Chicago, the two have found a niche designing and performing audio drama Foley for Chicago theatres. Theatrically produced radio plays traditionally are a rich arena for a sound artist, and they have grown in popularity as contemporary audiences become reintroduced to the audio drama. Matelan began as an actor, studying in Texas at Southern Methodist University in Dallas. Her entry into Chicago theatre and her introduction to Foley presented her with a unique opportunity to work at theatres such as WildClaw Theatre and The House Theatre. As a voice actor and Foley artist, Matelan has made herself a valuable asset in the Chicago theatre and film scene.

Griffin met a theatrical sound designer at the Goodman Theatre in Chicago, and he found that his work as a production sound mixer on low-budget features could be expanded into the theatre scene. He had been a recording engineer for advertising, which quickly led to commercials. He and Matelan have worked together for about five years and work on film as well as radio plays.
