
An intelligently designed and appropriately constructed Foley stage begins with the electrical requirements. Although most rooms have conventional standards for electrical wiring, a Foley stage has specific needs. When all is said and done, no matter how beautiful the room looks or how impressive the recording equipment is, if the room is not wired with the specific goal of recording sound effects with a wide variety of dynamic ranges and little, if any, interference from outside noise and electrical hum, then it is possible to spend countless hours tracking down the noises and trying to get rid of them. The first rule of the Foley stage is that it must work electronically.

Jim Fulmis, owner of Fultek Professional Audio and a veteran electrical engineer, has designed and wired many recording and rerecording stages and knows firsthand the nightmare of trying to fix a stage that is broken. When asked when he prefers to be involved with the construction of a Foley stage, he says, “It is always best to be involved in the early stages of design rather than one \[a Foley stage\] that has already been built.” This is the sentiment of most engineers. The earlier the electrical experts can give assurance that the concept for a room is workable, the better. Once the construction of the stage has begun, it is already too late for optimal electrical applications.

To begin with, the engineer must consider the shape of the room. The best shape for the Foley stage is a rectangle rather than a square. The walls, ceiling, and floor should not be perfect parallels. There are formulas in construction that allow for walls that are slightly off in measurements so the sound waves can be broken up. The goal is to reduce the chance of sound wave reflection (bounce), so that the room is less reverberant. Most acousticians agree with this perspective. Some, however, prefer rooms with more bounce and life. On a Foley stage, it is more typical to want less reverb.

In a room built to capture and record sound, the electrical needs are peculiar as well. When building an office building or a home, one does not have to be concerned with any buzz or hum that is running through a microphone cable. However, on a Foley stage, a hum or buzz can shut down operations for hours or even days. Thus, it is essential for the wiring used for microphones to be placed away from, and not parallel to, the wiring used for electricity. If the microphone wiring and the electrical wiring are too close together or, as is sometimes the case, bundled together, then when the mixer tries to record there will be a hum. Microphone wiring will draw current from the electrical wiring, and it can be impossible to get rid of the resultant hum.

I have spent many days on Foley stages when we could not find the source of the hum. Sometimes we had to stop work until an engineer could find and eliminate the hum. Sometimes this fix would last for a while; other times the hum came back. There are stages that Foley artists and mixers dread working on because there is hum that never goes away. Had an expert who understood the use of the room laid the wiring, this problem could have been avoided.

Another possible pitfall to be avoided via sound engineering is perpetual buzz in the microphone line. When wires are properly grounded, there will never be a buzz. It is best to have two types of grounds. One is the audio ground and one is the electrical. Some engineers will have one ground that does both. The bigger the ground wire size, the better. It is also essential to have an adequate pipe size for the wires to go through.

It is ideal to have proper grounding and wiring for a room that relies totally on the efficient and reliable use of electricity and microphones. However, every Foley artist and mixer have horror stories about hum and buzz. Most of the issues that come up on a stage could be avoided if the time and money were used at the front end to set up the stage properly. If the room is being adapted after being used for other purposes, an expert should examine the wiring before building anything. Simple mistakes can cause huge problems, as Fulmis illustrates:

> I can cite an instance where they brought in a welding cable and ran a copper ground in and added another ground. I was called in after the stage was down for two days and I said, “We’re going to disconnect all the grounds.” Then we found out there was an air conditioning conduit touching a cabinet that the equipment was in and that was creating a ground loop. So, we put blocks of wood on it to keep that from ever happening again and put one ground in. The stage was quieter than it had ever been.

The goal is to have the room work properly from the time the first switch is flicked to record. It is never true that the bugs necessarily have to be worked out of any stage. Fulmis reiterates,

> I’ve seen so many places where they just keep adding grounds to this or grounds to that, and that will work today. But then tomorrow you plug something else in and now you’ve got a new ground loop and a new buzz or hum.

If the room is wired well, it will not be necessary to spend a fortune on equipment. As Fulmis says, “You can use pretty cheap gear, provided it’s adequate to the job, and get away with it. You can get really good sound out of inexpensive gear if it’s all put together correctly. That’s the most important thing.”

## THE INTERIOR DESIGN

An ideal Foley stage combines function and form. Not only does it need to have the essential elements of a workspace that makes sense to the artists, but it should also have an atmosphere that allows artists to be comfortable and efficient. It is easier to be creative and productive if the stage is designed in a way that maximizes the artist’s time and energy.

Most experts agree that Foley stages need to have a minimum of reflection. While it is possible to use baffles to soften sounds that are too sharp or reflective, it is easier to work in a room that is not as live as a music studio. If the stage is utilized for other purposes, then some compromises with the acoustics will be necessary.

If one is using a room that has already been built, chances are it has four walls specifically designed to be parallel. It is possible to build walls inside walls to shape the room in a preferred way. A Foley stage does not have to be locked into the original design.

In addition to nonparallel walls, ceiling, and floor, the surfaces should have traps built in or added on that enhance the flow of sound waves. Instead of hard walls (too much reflection) or soft walls (too absorptive), a stage should include a mixture of both surface types. Traps with different textures and shapes to help absorb sound can be added without making the room so dead that it has no personality at all. A good rule to follow for the placement of traps is to mix it up. Pair soft with hard: soft surface with hard surface, and so on. Two traps of similar material put together do not usually result in the best acoustics. Play with the shapes as well. Each room has its own identity. It is a good idea to learn the acoustic style of the room and be willing to adjust and modify traps as necessary. Two rooms designed, constructed, and engineered the same way will sound different.

It is a great idea to build a floating floor to have the best results with the Foley pits. A floating floor has air between it and the foundation. Foam or some other acoustic material can be inserted into the space to isolate sound that can travel through the floor from another source.
