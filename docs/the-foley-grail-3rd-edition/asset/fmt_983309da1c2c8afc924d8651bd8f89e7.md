
Once all the editors have edited their tracks, the tracks are sent to the dubbing stage to be predubbed and prepared for the final mix with the rerecording mixers. One mixer predubs the dialogue. That task can take a week or more. Another mixer predubs the effects and Foley. Then the ADR is predubbed. For the final mix, all rerecording mixers work together. One focuses on dialogue and ADR, and the other does the Foley and sound effects. One of them also mixes the music. On smaller or lower budget films, or in some film markets, one mixer does all of the mixing tasks.

Every editor has to be aware of how their material will dub. The editor is responsible to the mixer. Ideally, the SSE will sit on the dub stage during predubs to oversee the process and make sure the mixers have the material they need. The SSE might fix some edits on the mixing stage. The particular editor that worked on the session being mixed might make the changes. If the edits are minor, the mixers themselves may make the editorial adjustments.

The Foley artists and the Foley mixer should also be keenly aware of how the Foley will be dubbed at the final mix. Not only do the Foley artists and Foley mixer have to pay attention to the specific cues, but it is also essential that they know enough about the tasks of the editors and the mixers to be helpful. All too often, the Foley session arrives at the dub stage with too many tracks of Foley. The Foley is one aspect of sound. Do the Foley that is needed. No more, no less.

The Foley team needs to remember that the mixers at the final dub have the director, producer, picture editor, and others watching and contributing ideas during the dub. It is a stressful situation, and the best favor any Foley artist, Foley mixer, or Foley editor can do is to anticipate the final mix when presenting the Foley. A consideration of how much material should be included and, more importantly, *not* included aids the rerecording mixer a great deal.

In television or episodic web series, the situation is similar, except that everything is happening at once and on a compressed schedule. It is not uncommon for a television show to “turn over” one week before it is supposed to air. When a web series is produced with an intended single release date, the workflow becomes even more crucial and includes more variables regarding who will be working on what aspect of the sound. It is not uncommon for a series to have more than one sound team and one rerecording stage working simultaneously. Under these circumstances, consistency is difficult, at best.

Thus, it is even more essential that the postproduction sound team be organized and aware of each other’s tasks. It is also helpful to have little, if any, duplication of an effect. It should be covered in the production track, the effects tracks, or the Foley tracks.

There are many stories of a dubbing mixer having three or four versions of a sound. Perhaps the sound effects editor covered it several ways, as did the Foley team. Options might be offered when the sound team is unclear what works best. The decision has to be made—on the dub stage, under the watchful eye of the producer paying for the stage time, and with intense time pressure—what effects to use. As Dave Stone relates, Mike Finnell, producer for Joe Dante, once said, “I have to pay someone to cue it, then pay someone to cut it, then pay someone to get rid of it.”

Then, of course, there is the famous, yet perhaps apocryphal, story of Richard Portman, a revered rerecording mixer, who simply threw out tracks 13–24 of the Foley and only dubbed tracks 1–12. It was neither the first nor the last time that a mixer has been burdened with the task of dealing with too many cues that might not have needed to be performed at all.

## ALTERNATIVE WORKFLOWS

Now that you have read about how the traditional workflow is established, there are some variations in how postproduction sound is approached that may be useful to you as you prepare your projects. While the Hollywood system is the most established and consistent, it would be a mistake to think it is the only way you can manage a soundtrack.

For an independent film, the context is different, so the protocols are different. The person in charge of sound is often the same person who composed the music or wrote the script. Perhaps the mixer is also the editor. Also, it is typical on a small film that some of the team members are less experienced. Thus, although the film is smaller and the budget is miniscule, it is helpful for independent filmmakers to understand what the professionals in “the big show” do so they can adapt the practices to their own projects.

The most important step is to have one person in charge of organizing the soundtrack. That person acts as the supervisor. The supervisor can also edit, or perform the Foley, or mix, but there needs to be one person who is the team leader and point person.

## WHAT EFFECTS NEED TO BE COVERED AND BY WHOM

Once the leader is established, the organization of the soundtrack can vary. The Foley artist can cue and edit the Foley as well. This is actually an excellent idea. By editing your own Foley, and even mixing it, you can learn more about what works appropriately for a project and what does not. The Foley mixer can mix the final dub. The editors can predub in Pro Tools. The dialogue editor can also compose the music. The configuration of skills can vary, but the tasks and accountability cannot. The system used in features and television is tried and tested. It works very well and is a good template for any project. The main difference is that there will be fewer people and they will multitask. Some of the biggest names in Hollywood learned filmmaking this way by working with Roger Corman. It is a great way to learn.

One of the first issues that will come up when spotting a project for sound is what effects need to covered and by whom. A very experienced dialogue editor knows how to “save” a lot of the production sound, and the effects and Foley editors will not need to cover as much material. More typically, however, there will be plenty of issues in the production sound that will require enhancements from the Foley artists.

Dialogue editing is a fine art, and if improperly executed it can yield disastrous results. Dialogue that is “overcleaned”—that is, does not have enough background noise—will sound sterile. A sterile background means sound effects, backgrounds, and Foley will have to take more responsibility. If the dialogue is muddy and sloppily edited, then sound effects, backgrounds, and Foley have to top the production and add even more layers of sound.

If the editors cutting sound effects do not communicate properly with the Foley team, then sounds will be made on the Foley stage that duplicate the sound effects that are already on the tracks. It is a common problem for the rerecording mixers to have to slow down the predub process to hunt for the effects. Are they covered in Foley? Production? Sound effects? Not at all?

The Foley stage is a wonderful laboratory to create unusual sounds, in addition to performing ordinary and uneventful sounds that create the sonic life of a character. It should also be essential to retain a sense of practicality. A Foley artist needs to be intuitive as well as analytical, but it is not always possible to be both at the same time. The key to being the best Foley artist is to balance the two. The important key is to remember that there will be people who handle the Foley after it is recorded. Editors and mixers will need the Foley well prepared and artfully executed.
