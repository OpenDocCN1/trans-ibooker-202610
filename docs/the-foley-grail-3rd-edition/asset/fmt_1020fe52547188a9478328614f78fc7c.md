
9. Do not drop anything loud or heavy on a stage before saying, “Watch your ears.” The mixer needs to know to turn the microphone down.

10. Always know where the light switch is.

## [HINTS](#content.xhtml#bck_sec12_5)

1. Commit to the cue.
2. Remember it can be done again.
3. Be experimental.
4. Try layering sounds.
5. Remember that props sound different depending on what they are touching. Example: metal changes the sound of a prop. So does wood.
6. Think in terms of components. The exact thing is not needed. What is it made of?
7. Think outside the box. What story world is the project in? The sounds one creates need to support the narrative in that story world.

## [Magic Wind](#content.xhtml#bck_Ch13)

### [Unusual Foley Requests](#content.xhtml#bck_Ch13)

DOI: [10.4324/9781003008439-17](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003008439-17)

![](Images/fig0023.jpg)

I was filling in for a Foley artist colleague on the video *Pocahontas 2: Journey to a New World* (1998), which was a sequel to the animated film *Pocahontas* (1995). The cueing had been executed by a well-intentioned, but seemingly inexperienced editor. One of the cues, a prop cue, was called “magic wind.” While this is a proper sound design concept, and the wind in the original film had a magical quality, this would have been a special design element executed by sound editors.

When the editor called the stage to see how we were doing, we politely asked what he meant by “magic wind.” His response was some version of, “You know, do the wind that you see.” He probably was unaware that this request had two distinct problems: first, it was a sound design that was going to be handled by edited effects, and second, it had to match the effect that was in the original feature.

Foley artists will get requests that do not necessarily make sense to them. There is a plethora of stories that float around the editing rooms and Foley stages about requests that are either impossible to do or more appropriately done by editors or special sound design. Sometimes sound editors are not certain about what they want, and other times they cannot communicate what they hear in their own imaginations. The purpose of relating this story is to illustrate that some Foley cues will not make sense to the artist, even if they do make sense to editors. Communication is always essential, and to ask for more information about what the artistic intention is can help the Foley artist a great deal.

## [SOMETHING NEVER HEARD BEFORE](#content.xhtml#bck_sec13_1)

Robert Mott relates a story about a television newscast for which he was preparing sound effects:

> They wanted the sound of an atom bomb. [It is important to note that no one had ever heard one before. The testing of the bomb on American soil was a new phenomenon.] They went out to field record, and the bomb melted the microphone. They parked so far away, yet it lifted the van off the ground.

So, they asked Mott to make a sound effect for the atom bomb. He had only worked with established taped or disc recordings. He watched the footage and saw the mushroom cloud. He used a recording of the Mogambi waterfalls and slowed it down for the roar. Then he added a layer of a wall being demolished and raised the level. By the time he was finished, he had used four records, some of them doubled up for the resultant effect.

Sound professionals have often been asked to create unusual sounds with the expectation that anything can be done on a Foley stage. Foley artists will often try to solve these puzzles with creativity and imagination, resulting in effects that surpass the editor’s request. Other times, the results are not as satisfying. These special requests take time and money to execute.

## [THE SLOWED-DOWN EFFECT WAS OMINOUS AND BIZARRE](#content.xhtml#bck_sec13_2)

John Post, while working on The Thing (1982), needed to have a sound for the monster underneath the board planks. Supervising sound editor David Yewdall asked him to create the monster moving under the planks while the planks came apart and flew into the air. This is a tricky effect to create on a Foley stage, but Post was always up for the challenge. So, he and Duane Hensel took short pieces of lumber and laid them on the stage, rather like dominoes. The two men stood at each side of the planks, and in sync to picture they grabbed a plank and rattled it against the floor and the next plank. Then, Post put on his editor’s hat, and after the cue was recorded he slowed it down to a snail’s pace, which added more depth. The slowed-down effect was ominous and bizarre.

As a side note, Post also did a dog voice for the dog as it ran through the snow for the same film. Today, an actor in SAG-AFTRA would be hired to do that.

David Jobe was the Foley mixer on some of the *Terminator: The Sarah Connor Chronicles* episodes (2008). On one episode, the Foley artists were asked to create something for the terminator’s metallic, articulated arms. David Fein “grabbed an I-beam and rubbed it. I picked the chorusing voice on the Eventide [harmonizing hardware] and we did it,” Jobe recalls.

## [FOLEY HUMOR EVOLVES OUT OF EVERY PROJECT](#content.xhtml#bck_sec13_3)

Joan Rowe relates a story about a request on *ET: The Extra-Terrestrial* (1982):

> Spielberg wanted ET to be “liquidy and friendly,” and sometimes “liquidy” sounds slimy. It’s hard to put words to sound. I walked through some stores and listened to the movement of packaged liver in a flat container. It had a “cheery little sound.”

Rowe would handle this package of liver as one of the sounds for ET’s body. Every few days, she would go to the store near the studio and get another package. She would walk into the store in her Foley attire (leotards and Foley shoes), and one time she heard the cashier whisper to the person he was chatting with, “That’s the woman who listens to the liver!” Rowe also recorded a track of Jell-O clumped inside a wet towel and a track of a bag of popcorn to augment her liver effect. She moved all three props in sync with ET as he moved.

One of the most frequent questions regarding the Foley on *Predator* (1987) is how the predator’s body was created. There is a complicated answer. The predator’s body required several tracks that would be played together. One track was a wet chamois. Another was hand lotion on my hands. A third was a wet leather purse. A fourth track was some mouth noises. We called it “predabody.” There was also “predaface,” “predahands,” and “predafeet.” Clearly, Foley humor evolves out of every project.

## [COLLEGIALITY GOES A LONG WAY IN ART](#content.xhtml#bck_sec13_4)

Greg Barbanell had an odd task for the film *Defending Your Life* (1991). He spent a day trying to find a way to “walk in heaven,” as the supervisor had requested. Barbanell believed that this was not really a sound you should really hear, but the supervisor was attached to the idea. Barbanell cannot remember how it was resolved, but it cost stage time and money lesson here? Some ideas should be ironed out before they hit the Foley stage. Foley artists will not always have control over this situation.
