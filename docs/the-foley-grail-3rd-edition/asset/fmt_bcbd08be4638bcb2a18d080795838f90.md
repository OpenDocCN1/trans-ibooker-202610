
Jerry relates a story about one of his experiences doing dance Foley. He was working on *The Turning Point* (1977) and was asked to do some of the ballet sounds for Mikhail Baryshnikov. The choreographer was concerned that Jerry could not dance like the gifted Russian. He was asked, “Can you do as many turns as Baryshnikov? Can you stay in the air as long as Baryshnikov?” Jerry, in his typical dry delivery, replied, “I don’t have to do the same steps, they just have to sound like the steps. I can do most of this sitting down.” The choreographer was dubious, but Jerry proved his point—what matters is what it *sounds* like. “Sometimes we put the shoes on our hands,” says Jerry. “You don’t have to know the steps. It has to sound like the dancing, not look like the dancing.”

In 1997, I was presented with a unique opportunity to perform dance sounds for animals in the animated film, *Cats Don’t Dance*. Gene Kelly had been the consultant for the choreography that the animators created utilizing cel animation. It was great fun, as I had been a professional dancer and was accomplished in tap dancing. Some of the dancing was done sitting down, some standing upright, and all of it in short takes of just parts of each dance. Dancing cues require vigilant attention to the subtle moves of each foot, but they also include the arms and hands. A single dance for a single character requires paying close attention to the most minute movement the character performs. Thus, it is not easy, fast, or obvious. The Foley artist charged with doing choreographed dance Foley needs to take the time to make each scuff, tap, slide, turn, jump, or toe shoe movement sound as authentic, yet theatrical as is warranted by the film. Each hand movement, flick of a wrist, touch of the body, or touch of an object must be included. In *White Nights* (2005) and *Tap* (1989), Gregory Hines did his own “improvitap” dance Foley on a constructed wooden stage for the purpose of recreating the best sounding tap sounds. Both Jerry Trent and I have replaced dance footsteps for Baryshnikov, as well as the easier tap steps for Hines. My work was for *White Nights*, while Trent did the Foley for *Tap*.

What is essential to remember is that just because the characters are seen to be dancing does not mean Foley artists have to do the same action. They only have to sound like they are doing it. It is sonic acting.

## WHERE ARE THE FEET?

One of the issues that comes up for a Foley artist performing dance footsteps or ordinary walks is where to look if the feet are not on camera. Robert Deschaine, a mixer on many films and television movies, had great advice whenever he saw a Foley artist struggle with a character walking or running in a long sequence with a lot of edits. He would tell the artist to “watch the shoulders.” That was all he said, all he would need to say. The artist could fall into rhythm, even with the difficult edits. This is the key to tricky walks. The shoulders are the clue to how the character is moving.

Often, a character’s feet are not on-screen, and the edits do not keep the character in the same rhythm. A skilled Foley artist has learned where to look to get the character’s footsteps in sync. It could be the shoulders, the head, the hands, or any other part of the body that indicates the actor’s intention. Picture editing is not focused on the sync of footsteps. Rather, its task is to support the story and the flow of the dramatic or comedic action. So, the sound experts have to concern themselves with smoothing over the cuts that do not match. This can be tricky. There are times when a character is stepping on the right foot at the end of one cut (the A side), only to be in mid-step on the next cut (the B side). One has to try two things. Either option might work. Try double stepping: step the same step twice and see if that works. Or one can let it hang over: do not make the step on the first cut and complete it on the next cut. One never knows which way works better in the rhythm of the walk until one tries it.

Sometimes actors do strange things that cannot be accurately interpreted. John Forsythe had the appearance of taking longer to get out of a car than most actors. This was hard to figure out. Jerry Trent, while working on a television show starring Forsythe, finally saw in one angle of a shot why this was so. The classy Mr. Forsythe was planting his feet on the ground and then taking the time to pull his pants legs down before he got out of the car. Once that unpredictable behavior was discovered, it became easier to anticipate the actor’s exit from the car. The moral of this story? Pay attention to the actor. They are all different with unique habits.

## FOOTSTEP SURFACES

To begin with, when Foley artists walk characters on a gritty street or sidewalk, some use dirt sprinkled on the surface. Many, however, use coffee grounds, right out of the can. A staple in many artists’ bags might be a container with Folgers or some other grocery store brand of ground coffee. They sprinkle the grounds on the surface and then work it into the surface for the appropriate “grit factor.” If the street is really gritty and they want to give the impression of a dirty walkway, they might use more of the grounds or their selected type of dirt. If the walkway is cleaner, or the scene takes place in a nice part of town and they want to support the beauty of the tasteful environment established in production, they use a smaller amount of the coffee or dirt and grind it into a fine powder. Some shoes have soles that the grit clings to, while others do not. The shoe element must, therefore, also be taken into account. High heels can often be problematic for “grit cling” due to the smaller surface area on the heel.

It takes some practice to walk on a gritty surface. The tendency for the beginner will be to walk normally while watching the picture. However, the real design of Foley comes in altering the footsteps with intention so that every step does not have the exact same amount of grit. On any street or walkway, grit is uneven. Thus, the Foley walking needs to have a certain unpredictability to it as well.

When Foley artists have to walk characters in snow, there are many opinions on what makes the best kind of snow. It used to be common practice to use vermiculite, which is poisonous to the lungs. However, it is absurd to hurt one’s health for a movie. Some use rock salt. Some use shaved ice. Some use a combination that includes cornstarch. A popular technique is to use a stew of cornmeal and cornstarch, plus some dirt if the snow is dryer. If it is wet snow, the artist might use a wet cloth for one track of footsteps, and then record another track with cornstarch on top of dirt, which is on top of a cement surface. The question that must be asked is, “What kind of snow is it?” Wet? Fresh? Refrozen?
