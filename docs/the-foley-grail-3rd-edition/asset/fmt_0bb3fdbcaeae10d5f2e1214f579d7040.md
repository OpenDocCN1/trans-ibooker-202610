
Most films have a supervising sound editor (SSE). The production company, at the request of the director or picture editor, usually hires this person. Sometimes, in animation and television, the postproduction supervisor for the production company does the hiring. The important point is this: the first person hired for postproduction sound will be the supervisor. Some films have a sound designer and a supervising sound editor. The two positions have different purposes. The sound designer is responsible for very specific conceptual sound effects, and the supervising sound editor is the team leader for the rest of the sound crew. The SSE then hires the necessary editors for the show. The film may have a budget that allows for a generous supply of editors. More likely than not, however, the budget will be slim and a few, well-chosen editors will be hired. There will be someone to edit dialogue, someone to edit sound effects and backgrounds, someone to supervise and edit the automated dialogue replacement (ADR), and someone to edit the Foley. The music is composed and edited in a separate place by a separate crew. A music editor works with the film composer to select the proper cues for each musical motif. The composer and music editor do not normally collaborate with the sound team in Hollywood, but there are exceptions. In other film communities, such as New York or the San Francisco Bay Area, the composer and music editor might, indeed, collaborate regularly with the sound team.

On a big-budget show, the SSE will have the luxury of hiring more than one editor for each task. That is desirable as the schedules become shorter, more demanding, and more labor intensive. The crew may have to constantly change the edits of the film for “temp dubs” and picture changes.

The Foley artists are usually hired by the SSE. Normally, there are two Foley artists on a film or television show. However, if the budget is tight, it is not uncommon for one artist to have to do the entire show alone. This is not the preferred protocol, but budgets vary greatly, and some shows cannot afford the cost of two artists. In New York, there is typically one Foley artist.

The Foley artists are accountable to the supervising Foley editor. There may be another Foley editor, but the supervisor is in charge. This editor will have spotted the required Foley with the SSE and will have cued the Foley for the stage. Once the Foley has been performed and recorded, it is this editor who ensures that the Foley is edited and that all the cues are completed.

### The Process of Each Team Player

The SSE spots the show with the director and picture editor. At this session, the SSE takes detailed notes on what is requested from the sound team. Notes are made regarding style, important dramatic moments, the specific conceptual ideas of the director, and continuity issues. The SSE may have the ADR editor at the session take notes on what lines the actors will need to replace during the ADR session. If the ADR editor is not at this spotting session, the SSE will have a separate spotting session later. The supervising Foley editor will also have a spotting session with the SSE.

The dialogue editors will get the most recent version of the film, television episode, or web series with the selected sound “takes” from the picture editor. In the days of analog sound, they would then divide up the reels to be finessed and work carefully to smooth and separate out the dialogue in the film. With the transition to digital sound, the process is the same, but the execution is different. The sound can be separated nondestructively on tracks and played back together. Dialogue editing can save the other editors on the sound crew a lot of unnecessary work. By arranging the dialogue properly, the dialogue editor and the rerecording mixer can retain the footsteps and props that were recorded in production. Thus, the sound effects editors and Foley editors can put their efforts into the truly necessary effects that need to be added to the tracks.

The Foley editor will cue the footsteps, hand props, cloth and special props, and custom effects that need to be performed on the Foley stage. Specific tracks will be assigned for each character walked and the specific types of props performed. The cloth will be put in the last track assigned. Traditionally, cue sheets would be sent to the Foley stage that would lay out the blueprint for the Foley mixer and Foley artists. Previously, when Foley was recorded onto a 24-track tape, the cue sheets were handwritten. Nowadays, they are cued directly into the digital session (the standard in most major film markets is Pro Tools) and sent to the Foley stage. Sometimes, cue sheets can be printed out from these sessions for the Foley artists to preview and plan what shoes and props to use.

The Foley mixer (or recordist, if there is one on the stage) will then load the session into the computer from a drive or download it from a server. Some studios (and some film schools) have a server that uploads and downloads, so no external hard drives or flash drives are ever sent to the stage. Sometimes, the Foley artists prefer to have the Foley mixer “call out” the cues to them. If this is their practice, they will not bother with printing out the sheets.

While the Foley artists are performing cues on the stage, the sound effects editors are cutting in their effects. Traditionally, the SSE usually would have auditioned and assigned effects for the film, and the editors would be given their “kit” of effects in the form of a library. However, now editors can download sound effects and, if time is really tight, decide for themselves which effects they should use. It is their job to organize and layer the effects and create the soundtrack for each session. More often than not, if time is tight, the sound editors might also include “mixing-in-the-box” because the technology allows them to. Pro Tools is designed to encourage boundary crossing and the work-flow often relies on this fluidity.

Meanwhile, the ADR editor is on the stage getting the required replacement lines from the actors in the film and then going back to the cutting room and cutting in the selected takes. This, however, can also be done at the stage because the ADR mixer has Pro Tools and can “move” the line right there, if there is limited time and it is an easy fix. Also, if the ADR is long distance—as is often the case for television shows and films shot in cities such as Atlanta—the ADR mixer, whose job is executed similarly to that of the Foley mixer, can record the ADR and send it online to the studio in Los Angeles edited and ready to go by the next morning. Once again, technology is allowing some alterations in the workflow.

The point is that all of the sound editors, whether they are cutting dialogue, effects, ADR, or Foley, are doing their tasks collaboratively but also simultaneously. They have to coordinate their sessions and must know what each other is doing. The best way to understand this unique form of collaboration is that the team works in unison, but not necessarily together.

### Team Collaboration

All of the members of the sound team have to have a working knowledge of what the other members are responsible for so that there is no overlap in work effort, but everything is covered. The sound effects editors need to know what the dialogue editors are “saving” in production. The Foley editors need to know what the sound editors are cutting in from effects. The sound effects and Foley editors need to know where the music will be loud. All of the editors need to know what lines are being replaced. Everybody’s job affects everybody else.
