
In December 2020, The Alliance Theatre, located in Atlanta, Georgia, produced an innovative radio drama of *A Christmas Carol* at Georgia State University’s Center Parc Credit Union Stadium parking lot. The play was presented as a drive-in movie, with attendees being charged per car and featuring the actors in large containers the size of small rooms, complete with lighting and small set pieces. Percussion professor Stuart Gerber, from Georgia State University’s School of Music, performed the Foley effects in his own container that replicated a miniature Foley stage. I was hired as consultant for this project to collaborate with Gerber, sound designer Ben Coleman, and director Leora Morris. We worked together over the course of two months. Gerber’s gifts as a percussionist were considerable, and with Coleman and Morris as co-adapters for the play, it was a unique and exciting experience. Even during the Covid-19 pandemic, theatres have innovated to allow live performances, while expanding their audiences.

The point of these illustrations is to emphasize the need to break out of conventions and utilize sound components to support characters and story in different and experimental ways. Rather than working within a comfort zone of familiar techniques and methods, sometimes a Foley artist will find a situation in which reinventing the purpose and execution of performed sound requires fearless abandonment of all things known, while relying on tried-and-true techniques for combining sound elements to support the narrative. Radio drama presents such an opportunity and can encourage new ideas for both listening *and* hearing. As Austrian sound artist Cornelia Baumgartner says, the critical issue for live radio plays is “how to create sounds when you don’t have the big props.”

## [HITCH YOUR TRAILER](#content.xhtml#bck_sec11_4)

Postproduction sound editor and mixer Rick Sanchez has been working on movie trailers since 1998. Until 2019, Sanchez worked at Post Haste Sound in Los Angeles. Now, he occasionally freelances. Sanchez’s professional journey began as a music engineer after taking classes in Los Angeles. Around 1975, a friend told him that, “a guy got fired today,” at Record Plant, which was a premier recording studio. The job involved, among other duties, sweeping floors. Sanchez happily took the opportunity and began to work days and observe nights. He recalls setting up sessions for artists such as The Eagles, Stevie Wonder, and John Lennon. “Everyone had their own favorite mics and placements,” Sanchez recalls. “I was told to sit and watch and don’t say anything.” He did, he did, and he did not. These were the days when recording studio staff would do “whatever it takes to make the artist comfortable.”

Sanchez worked his way up to substitute recording engineer and then primary engineer. His years at Record Plant, both in Los Angeles and in San Francisco, trained Sanchez to have a finely tuned ear for music and sound, which came in handy in 1991 when he transitioned into mixing Foley for artists including Fred Brown, Michelle Sharp, Rick Partlow, and Monique Reymond. It was in 1996 that Rick Partlow introduced me to Sanchez as a replacement Foley artist partner for me. Sanchez’s understanding of mixing was invaluable for his development as a Foley artist, and we worked on television shows and films for a few years. Both Part-low and Sanchez were my Foley partners on *Cats Don’t Dance* (1997).

When Sanchez moved his skills over to work as a sound professional on movie trailers for films to be released on video after theatrical release, he found his experience in mixing and performing Foley to be an asset. He was skilled at adding any additional Foley for the trailers himself in the small studio where he mixed and edited sound effects to the “stems” from the rerecording mix. Sanchez also mixed the voice-over narrative for the trailers. Many of the voice-over actors are familiar voices to film viewers, as there are a few select voice actors who do most of the trailers. One such voice actor, Mark Elliot, is often heard on the trailers for Disney videos.

Budgets have gotten smaller, and more demands have been made of those who make trailers for the video releases. Sanchez notes, “as technology got more sophisticated, the producers would say, ‘oh, well, you can fix that’ or ‘can you put a sound effect or whoosh in there to cover it up?’” As another illustration of the expectations of producers for trailers, he relates that often there is a misunderstanding that sound for action films is louder, more aggressive, and more emphasized than sound in dialogue or animation films. When mixing sound for the video for a dialogue film, he muses that the producer queried, “why is ours not as loud as theirs?” (referring to an action film trailer) to which Sanchez reflects to me, “It’s a sweet little commercial; it is not world-wide wrestling.”

What is important to understand about the world of movie trailers is that they are little “mini” films in their own universe with their own sets of protocols. One person might be mixing the trailer with the voice-over, the sound effects stems from the final dub, additional Foley, and enhanced sound effects. The narrative is not the concern; rather, the task is to “sell” the product to the potential viewers, so sound, including Foley and music, is included to advance the purpose of the advertising trailer, not the story in the film itself. With this in mind, there is no real crew to do the work. Rather, one sound expert will typically rework the original sound effects, add some Foley if necessary (in an editing studio or small mixing room), and repurpose the music. For Sanchez, his experiences as a Foley artist, mixer, and editor have allowed him to work on trailers and understand the entire postproduction sound process.

## [FOLEY COMPLEMENTS](#content.xhtml#bck_sec11_5)

As you can see, Foley for animation, games, radio dramas, and movie trailers serves varying purposes, depending on its role within the world of animated films or cartoons, games as interactives or cinematics, radio dramas to evoke character, or movie trailers to supplement the original sound. Unlike its original purposes to create and expand the aural representation of the characters in film, support the narrative, and collaborate with edited sound effects as a partner in sound design, Foley’s use has expanded to breathe life into animated characters, bring a plethora of choices to a gaming experience, evoke a sense of connection to voice-driven radio drama, and to amplify the commercial appeal of a movie trailer.

By utilizing the very skills, conventions, protocols, and best practices of high-level film Foley, the artist can become versatile at employing the craft for many different situations. The Foley artist may be asked to perform visually, be invisible, make heightened but realistic sounds, create an environment from props that do not in reality exist in that environment at all, and fill in segments of Foley between other Foley performances already codified in the project. Flexibility, a working knowledge of more performance arts than simply film, and a willingness to learn by making mistakes are valuable skills for a Foley artist open to multiple opportunities.
