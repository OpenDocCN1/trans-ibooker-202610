
If it is possible to have a real water pit, with a pump to empty it, that is great. The sound is better if it is flush to the ground, which means cutting into the floating floor. If this is not possible, then a water pit that is above the ground is fine. Be careful about using a microphone with water cues. Water slaps against the sides and can sound like a bathtub. Move the mic around to see where the best placement is to avoid reflection and splashing. The pit should be deep enough to simulate the ocean. A good guideline is to allow several feet of space for water, which will prevent it from spilling out or splashing everywhere on the stage.

Many films have dishes in the sink or water drip cues. Therefore, it is useful to have a place to install a sink and faucet. If it is not possible to do that (which is more often than not the case), then the workspace will need to include a large vessel in which to drip and splash small amounts of water.

Foley stages get dirty. Walking in dirt pits puts dirt in the air. It is essential to have an air conditioner system that cleans the air. After dirt pits are walked for a few hours the air is unhealthy to breathe, and chances are an artist cannot work with the air conditioner on because the noise will be recorded, so calculate how to manage dirty air. Also, the dirt lands on surfaces, so stages need to be cleaned regularly. This is a real problem. Often Foley artists are asked to use a stage that someone else has used during another shift. Because there is not enough time to clean between shifts, the dirt can accumulate, and an unhealthy work environment may result. Also, dirt pits become full of bacteria, so the dirt needs to be removed and replaced regularly. It takes time and discipline to maintain appropriate Foley hygiene. However, it is the considerate and professional way to proceed.

## TOOL TIME

When it comes to purchasing equipment for the stage, although it is seductive to buy the priciest tools with the most accessories, these are really unnecessary for a Foley stage. Even a downloadable edition of Pro Tools will suffice for most purposes. It is important to remember the task at hand. Mixers need to be able to record props and footsteps with a wide variety of dynamic range. It is best to have several microphone choices. A preamplifier is essential for the recording system, as well as a microphone amplifier. A toolkit or application that utilizes time code and some kind of intercom system that allows the artist on the stage to hear playback and allows the mixer to hear the artist in between cues are preferable. If possible, some kind of audio or visual system that alerts the Foley artist to the upcoming cue should be installed. Most Foley stages incorporate a series of beeps (as is used in ADR to cue the actor prior to a line) or a visual streamer that crosses the picture to predict the cue.

Thomas has several microphones and a preamp. “I knew what I needed and what I didn’t need,” she says. Sennheiser, Schoeps, and Neumann have well-respected microphones that are typically used when recording Foley. Ric Viers, author of *The Sound Effects Bible*, is also fond of Rode microphones. Microphones are like wines: there are many varieties and they appeal to personal choice, but some are standards. The most popular microphone for the most overall usage is the Sennheiser 416.

## ALTERNATIVE SPACES: WALK A MILE IN MY SHOES

Some sound professionals like to try other ways to record Foley. Mark Mangini is an Academy Award–winning SSE for the movie *Mad Max: Fury Road* (2015) and is responsible for the sound design of many notable soundtracks. He tried a different approach when he supervised the sound on *The Green Mile* (1999). Mangini had grown frustrated with sitting on a dub stage during predubs while the rerecording mixers would finesse the Foley to make it sound realistic. As he states it, “It doesn’t sound like what it’s meant to represent. It is meant to represent some acoustical reality that we can all relate to. We record Foley in a way that is surreal.”

So Mangini decided to try something different. He wanted to mimic reality, so he went on his own location-scouting endeavor. He wanted to “cast” locations for their “acoustical signature,” and he found an abandoned military base that had a concrete corridor and an abandoned prison. He recorded “auditions” of the spaces and had a remote Foley rig assembled with a battery-operated Pro Tools console. He worked his budget to allow for the unusual approach to recording Foley, and he left time and money in the budget for mistakes and unexpected events. At this point, he was convinced he would record the Foley in this new way.

Finding a Foley artist who wanted to try this new concept, however, was not so easy. The logistics of how to record, where to keep props, how to control the sound, technology, and workflow, and other unforeseen issues caused most of the Foley artists Mangini approached to be less than excited. Foley mixers also had concerns. A mixer is used to following a kinesthetic routine with their mixing board and monitor that allow optimal functioning. A change in the recording configuration is a disconcerting issue for any mixer.

Robert Winder, who at the time managed the stages and operations at Warner Hollywood Studios (formerly Samuel Goldwyn, now under new ownership and called The Lot Studios), proposed a compromise. The film had been shot on the lot, and the shooting stages were still available. They had been built with specifications that were authentic to the real-life spaces of prisons and offices. Thus, instead of having fake surfaces and walls that cause soundstages to sound similar to each other, these sets had a more authentic look and sound than most. Frank Darabont, the director, had insisted on this detail in the sets.

So Mangini did a test. He directed Foley artist John Roesch to do sample Foley on both the sets and the Foley stage, and he let the director pick his favorite. The director chose the set samples. Thus, the first issue was resolved. The Foley would be shot on the sets of the film.

The next challenge was how to record the Foley. According to Mangini, Mary Jo Lang, a meticulous Foley mixer, was reluctant to abandon her methodology of mixing, lest the product suffer. She had the configuration of her mixing board and monitor in positions that allowed her to mix with optimal efficiency and maximum technical expertise. It happened that Warner Hollywood had recently upgraded its infrastructure with fiber optics throughout the lot, including the soundstages and all recording stages. This meant it was technically possible to have sound travel across the lot from the soundstages to the Foley stage. It was also possible to have a talk-back system, a monitor, and headphones put on a rolling rig, so Roesch and Hilda Hodges, his Foley partner, could perform the Foley on the sets while Lang watched and guided them from her well-oiled mixing booth.

Roesch and Hodges had more adjustments to make. Mangini wanted the footsteps walked in a nontraditional way. Typically, a microphone is set in one place, and the artist walks the character while staying in one location. Mangini wanted to try to have the characters walk away or toward the microphone to replicate the action on screen. This is not an easy task for a Foley artist who has learned how to walk characters while staying in place. One is not quite sure how far away to walk, as a Foley artist is always aware of where the mic is. Mangini wanted Roesch and Hodges to abandon the established practice. Mangini decided to place the microphone on a boom, as is done in production, and to move the mic stands around. As he would say to Roesch, “John, walk down the mile—really—walk *down* the mile.”
