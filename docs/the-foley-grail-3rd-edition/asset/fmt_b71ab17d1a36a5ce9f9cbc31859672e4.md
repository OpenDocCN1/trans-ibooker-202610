
# Predub Practices in Film Sound Mixing

## Traditional Purpose and Initial Process

The first days on the dub stage traditionally were primarily for predubs. This is, of course, in addition to any temp dubs that had occurred. Temp dubs introduce conceptual sound ideas for the purpose of further galvanizing the picture and editorial collaboration. Additionally, temp dubs allow the production company to present a respectable exhibition to a screening audience for the purpose of gaining insight into the most likely audience demographic for the project, as well as the critiques the selected test audience might offer for further editorial picture changes. The predub has, in the past, been a critical and time-consuming aspect of the dub. Mixers meticulously analyze and scrutinize each sound and finesse the timbre and quality of each piece of the track. This process requires patience and focus.

## Predub and Final Dub Relationship

A well-executed predub assists the final dub, which is the last chance to “get it right.” As David Stone says, “the predubs are like rehearsing a symphony orchestra; details are everything. The final dub is the concert!” “

## Historical Track Grouping and Workflow

Traditionally, when adequate time was allotted for the predub, the mixers would take one category of sound and mix the many tracks down into groupings of six tracks. Dialogue would traditionally be mixed down into six mono tracks. ADR would have six tracks of principals and perhaps another six of group ADR. Hard effects might be three to eight sets of six tracks, including any special design effects. Background effects (BGs) would have one set of six tracks. Foley would have six tracks of footsteps and six tracks of props. The cloth track—which is optional for a domestic dub, but invaluable for a foreign “music and effects” dub—would be split off onto its own track and be played at the final dub, but not at the predub.

## Contemporary Scheduling and Team Changes

Most predubs would ideally start with the dialogue. Everything starts with what is in the production track, so the dialogue predubs would often be given a week or more to truly finesse the tracks and make them sparkle. Next might be ADR, then sound effects, then Foley. The music—which is typically being composed right up until the last minute—would not be included until the final dub. Today, as schedules are more squeezed and time is tighter, it has even become acceptable for one stage to be predubbing dialogue while another might be predubbing Foley. In contemporary dubs, the three-person team is not used anymore. Typically, two mixers, and sometimes one solo mixer (the typical practice in New York film culture), will dub the entire show. Many small independent films also utilize a one-mixer model to dub an entire show. Economics is always a consideration, and digital technology has empowered both editors and mixers to be more fluid in executing tasks.

## Foley Predub Preparation and Conventions

When mixers would predub Foley, they would find that time was used more effectively if the preparation by the editor had been in anticipation of mixing needs. The tracks needed to be tagged properly and sheets needed to be printed out. Some dubbing mixers would watch the meters, some would look at the sheets and mark them up with notes on where they assigned the tracks in the predub, and some would prefer to look at Pro Tools. The conventions for laying out the tracks, however, were similar to dubbing prior to digital because the system was meant to utilize time efficiently, with a common literacy understood by all sound professionals. Rerecording mixers would be looking for principal footsteps in the leftmost channels, background footsteps in the next channels, and finally props, with similar types of props on similar tracks. The cloth track(s) would be at the far right of the cue sheets.

## Dialogue Mixing Philosophy

Richard Portman spent most of his years as a dialogue mixer. He would listen painstakingly for hiss and hum on the tracks: “It all starts with the production dialogue. If it is recorded well then everything else is much easier.” Many dubbing mixers would still probably agree with Portman. While his background as an engineer made him more sensitive to the technical recording quality of the production track, most mixers prefer the production dialogue mixing to be the template used for all remaining sound effects.

## Foley and Effects Integration

Some mixers liked to predub Foley against the dialogue tracks. Sometimes the Foley might be mixed while the mixer listened to the sound effects. This was advantageous because both effects and Foley might have coverage on a scene, so they needed to be played against each other to see which covered the material best. Often, the decision to marry the Foley effect with the edited choice was made in predubs. The purpose of predubs was, and still is, to cull down many units to a smaller, more manageable amount in order to finesse the final mix.

## Modern Flexibility and Practitioner Perspectives

In today’s myriad of technological possibilities, the predub is not a fixed convention. Depending on the film, the SSE, the mixer, and the schedule, the predub might be, in reality, an editor’s session from Pro Tools. The dialogue, however, is still the territory of the dubbing mixer. While sound effects and Foley might predub before ever being presented on a dub stage, the dialogue—which is the most sensitive aspect of any film—is still the cherished territory of the rerecording mixer. While work-flows have changed for other sound professionals, the dialogue mixer must still massage the dialogue until it sparkles. They might want to predub the dialogue alone or, as New York mixer Tom Fleischman prefers, have some ambient sound effects to predub against so he can assess how the dialogue will fit into the scene.

Michael Semanick mixes sound effects, and when he predubs Foley, he likes to take a lot of time to predub. Semanick mixed Foley for quite a while before becoming a rerecording mixer. He likes to play the other elements against the Foley in predubs: “When I’m predubbing, I’m trying to use whatever I’ve got to balance against.” Time, of course, is always the issue. Although Semanick does not always get as much as he would like, there are films he remembers where he had the time he requested: “Just on *Sweeney Todd* \[2007\] alone, I took about two weeks to predub the Foley, I think, because it was so important.” Semanick often works with directors such as Peter Jackson and David Fincher, who are careful to put more time in their budgets for postproduction sound.

Ren Klyce, who was the sound designer on numerous films including *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo* (2011) and most recently *Mank* (2020), is artful in his sound design and likes the Foley to sound like it occurred in production, so from the Foley stage to the dub, he prefers to allow time for experimentation and finessing. Klyce is not concerned with technical perfection as much as he is with a sense of artful randomness in the sound design. Both Fincher and Klyce will negotiate to get the money necessary for postproduction sound so they have the time to get the quality they both feel the professionals, like Semanick, need to do the best sound possible for the film. However, when Klyce and Fincher performed their duties on the episodic show *House of Cards*, schedules were shorter, so the control they more often exercised on a feature was not available. Several different teams of Foley artists performed the Foley, different teams of sound editors edited and predubbed the sound effects, and the final mixes were on different stages with different mixers. Yet, Klyce still managed to have the material predubbed by the mixers, as he prefers. Recently, Klyce has added rerecording mixer to his toolkit, which allows him more control over the final mix.
