
Carrie Sheldon (*Liza on Demand*, *You*) is a boom operator in production sound. While attending Lane Community College in Eugene, Oregon, she took courses in broadcasting and visual design and demonstrated a talent for sound from the beginning. In 2006, Sheldon agreed to work in production sound for a student who was doing her film thesis on the indigenous people in Panama. This experience led to her decision to move to Los Angeles to pursue a career in production sound. Similar to Gramlich and Burdick, she took whatever jobs she could find, and by 2005 she was working regularly. Although she was eligible to join the production mixers union (Local 695) in 2008, she waited until 2016 when she was offered a stable situation on a TV show.

Sheldon discovered that the convention in her profession is to bring your own gear and “charge back” the expense to the production. There is continuing pressure to keep the gear up to date as technology changes. As Sheldon advanced in her career, she became increasingly aware of sexism in production sound. She perceived a distinct preference for men to be hired as both mixers and boom operators. For this reason, she and production sound colleague Mary Jo Devenney started the group LA Sound Sisters, which offers support and advice and shares work opportunities with members. Sometimes if a producer wants her to work on a low-budget show, “I will donate my gear and send out a [female] that I know is trying to be in the sound department and that needs the experience,” explains Sheldon.

Production sound mixers and boom operators are responsible for recording the dialogue on the set of a show and are the essential link to the sonic template that postproduction sound professionals must utilize. How a show is mixed on the set influences how sound editors, Foley artists, ADR editors and mixers, and rerecording mixers approach their crafts. A good production track sets a great foundation for sound design and enables both editors and mixers to focus more on the art of sound, rather than the technical crafting, to overcome or compensate for a less than stellar production mix.

What Sheldon discovered was that only 7% of the union production sound professionals are women. Sheldon and Devenney decided to work toward diversity and equity. “I’m really trying my best to change the face of 695 [production sound union],” proclaims Sheldon. “It’s possible for me to recruit enough women and help get them into the union and get 10% over the course of my lifetime … and that is what I’m trying to do.” Sheldon amplifies,

> As we get more women in positions of directing and more people of color … these people have not as much experience as the men … and they need to take a little longer, and they might make mistakes and whatnot, and you find a lot of crew members will say, ugh, she doesn’t know what she is doing. What they don’t realize is you’re actively working against the diversity and inclusion of these people if you don’t understand that we need to be there to support them being taught. They haven’t had as many hours on the job, so of course, they’re going to make some mistakes.

While Sheldon is concerned with the attitudes of some men on the set when they see a woman handling the production sound, “not every guy will give you a chance, and this business is word of mouth,” she is enthusiastic about her work: “my favorite thing is to put microphones on clients.”

## WOMEN IN SOUND

At Ball State University in Muncie, Indiana (where David Letterman attended), the Virginia Ball Center for Creative Inquiry awards several fellowships every year to faculty who have a specific project that is chosen to be funded. The professor and the students spend a semester focused on the single purpose and project of this class. I was awarded one of these fellowships to work with 13 students that I personally selected to work on a short film about women who work in film sound. The students and I traveled to New York and Los Angeles, and as I guided them through the process of the industrial method of filmmaking, we produced the short film *Amplified: A Conversation with Women in American Film Sound*. Sheldon, along with Devenney and other production sound women, were interviewed. Additionally, we interviewed Foley artists, sound editors, mixers, and facility owners. What we discovered was that what Sheldon has uncovered is rampant all over the professional sound industry in film. What was illuminating were the various responses the women had for coping with and even thriving in a part of the film industry that frequently and sometimes blatantly privileges men over women. The general consensus is that situations are getting much better for women in film sound because the younger men coming into the industry have more positive perspectives regarding women working in filmmaking.

Another professional woman in sound who appears in *Amplified* is Midge Costin, the Kay Rose Endowed Chair in the Art of Sound and Dialogue Editing at the University of Southern California. She has also produced and directed her own documentary about film sound that is getting rave reviews, nominations, and awards. *Making Waves: The Art of Cinematic Sound* (2019) is a wonderful documentary that does not specifically focus on the role of women in film sound, but Costin does include interviews with some notable women in the profession. The film is written by Bobette Buster, with music by Allyson Newman, cinematography by Sandra Chandler, and editing by David J. Turner. In this film, many notable film professionals discuss sound design, editing, and Foley.

## THE HOLLYWOOD SOUND MUSEUM

In 2015, film sound professional Steve Lee cogitated around the idea of a museum that would feature and celebrate the many aspects of film sound and the individuals who have contributed to the craft. “I envisioned an entertaining place to learn more about creating sound for film, TV, radio, and other media,” says Lee. His vision is coming to fruition as The Hollywood Sound Museum. One of the features of this museum is “an interactive Foley exhibit where guests could perform sound effects in sync to a film clip of their choice—and then hear it played back.” Lee has raised money for this nonprofit endeavor and has collected many artifacts to display. While the goal is a “brick and mortar” museum for all to visit and enjoy, during the Covid-19 pandemic Lee has adapted his museum to be enjoyed virtually at [HollywoodSoundMuseum.org](http://hollywoodsoundmuseum.org). He is also creating regular webinars with professionals from every aspect of film sound.

## FOLEY FUTURES

The continuing interest in Foley as a craft as well as a performative art has resulted in blogs, social media pages, scholarly articles, podcasts, webinars, and films about the world of Foley and the professionals who continue to perfect and adapt the craft for the ever-changing applications. Film schools are incorporating more courses about film sound, and an increasing number of these institutions are including Foley in their curriculum, with some schools either adapting rooms to accommodate Foley or designing dedicated Foley stages. Those who are enticed into the profession can find more opportunities to start their own Foley businesses or amplify their media work utilizing Foley. What is increasingly clear is the evolution of Foley and the interest in those who are Foley artists continue to provide opportunities to learn it, perfect it, practice it, teach it, discuss it, and write about it.
