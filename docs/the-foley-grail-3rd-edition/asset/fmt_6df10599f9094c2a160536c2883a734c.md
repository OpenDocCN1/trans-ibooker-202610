
# Foley Artist Shoe Selection and Performance

## Surface and Shoe Choice

When a Foley artist selects shoes for a performance, she or he considers the choice of surfaces. This affects the sound of the shoes. The microphone used—as well as the distance from the microphone—affects the performance. The sonic character of the room affects the performance. The choice of a shoe for the character is not simple, but, as one gains experience, the elimination of improper shoes becomes easier.

If the character is walking on a hard surface, it is important to consider a shoe that is not too “bright”—that is, not too sharp and high-pitched in sound. Chances are that if the shoe has too much “high end” it will not blend into the dialogue track easily. If the character is walking on carpet, it might be better to avoid walking the steps at all. Carpet is hard to record properly. It has a “swish” sound to every impact, and to get enough level in the recording, you have to walk with more definition and weight. If the character is walking lightly or is in a soft shoe or socks or is barefoot, it might not be worth doing.

## Changing Surfaces During a Walk

If the character changes surfaces during a walk, it is critical to have a plan. Are you going to try to make the changes in one take? If so, be prepared. This is not easy. You will be watching the screen, not your feet or the surfaces. If you do not know where your surfaces are and cannot walk from one to the other on the correct footstep in the picture with the correct emphasis, it is better to punch in for the steps that change surfaces. Or, perhaps, you can change tracks and walk each part separately. Your best friend, at this point, is your mixer.

## Character Weight and Walking Style

If the character is heavy, that does not mean she or he walks heavily. This is a common misconception: most people would assume that a heavy person is a heavy walker. Yet Charles Durning, a rather rotund actor, had a fast-paced, clipped walking style and was light on his feet. Kathleen Turner, while having an appealing and attractive appearance, has a fast, athletic walk in many of her roles. She walks with more determination and weight than some.

How a character walks in different shoes varies. A woman in heels walks very differently from a woman in flats. Someone in soft shoes has a more casual walk than someone in hard street shoes or dressy hard-soled shoes. Bare feet are hard to portray.

## Bare Feet Example from Die Hard

I want to use a personal illustration of how a seemingly difficult footstep task can actually be performed in a sensible way for the Foley artist and be useful for the dubbing mixer. In *Die Hard* (1988), the Bruce Willis character is barefoot through most of the film. I walked his character barefooted, but with more weight and emphasis than if I had shoes on. My feet would make joint-cracking sounds now and then. Lee Tinkham, my Foley mixer, had to punch in and let me replace the steps that contained the distracting and jarring “crack” sound. When the character was walking on broken glass, I did one track of straight barefooted steps, and then I added a track of glass impacts by putting glass on tile and “walked with my hands” wearing gloves. I was careful to be varied and selective in the impacts. Some Foley artists used to have a saying in Foley that illustrated the arbitrary nature of some cues: “Next they’ll want us walking on broken glass.” Life imitates art.

## Recording Technique and Environment

When you do a cue that requires bare feet, you will find it takes time to figure out the odd issues your feet have. Maybe you have high arches. Perhaps you are more flat-footed and have a heavier footfall. You can take your time to figure out if you need to walk heel-toe or even toe-heel to make it sound natural. Even the placement of the microphone can help. If the microphone is too close, your feet might sound sticky. If the microphone is too far away, you might sound too light. Be mindful of the room as well. What sounds great in one room will sound different in another. The same is true for the difference in mixers. No two elements in any Foley recording are the same.

## Shoe Personality and Room Interaction

My partner on *Die Hard*, Robin Harlan, walked the footsteps for Hans Gruber, the Alan Rickman character. He was suave, international, and poised. She auditioned several pairs of shoes and then selected a gorgeous-sounding pair of shoes that had a rich and authoritative sound. Her character was the boss of the whole operation, and he needed to sound “in charge.” He did. Robin’s choice was artful and appropriate. You will find that shoes have to be tried on several surfaces and in several rooms before you really get a sense of what they can do for you. Also, you might find that in some rooms they become your worst enemy and they betray you. Shoes seem to have personalities all their own. You might also find that the very shoes that sounded great on linoleum sound terrible on concrete. Also, pick your carpet carefully. Shoes can lose their character easily on carpet.

## Appearance Versus Sound

Shoes do not necessarily look like what they sound like. This is an important aspect of shoe selection. Sometimes a director might actually show up on a Foley stage. The director might decide to “cast” the sound of the shoe according to its look. The assumption that one can discern how a shoe will record by how it looks is erroneous and uninspired. Listen to shoes and do not look at them. I remember that I used a pair of glamorous-sounding shoes for stiletto heels in *The Woman in Red* (1984). They were ugly blue shoes that were shredded yet sounded great. My favorite shoe for representing classy, elegant men was actually a woman’s patent leather oxford. I had a pair of brown shoes with flaking paint that was great for tall, heavy men in a hard shoe. If one cannot judge a book by its cover, the same is so very true of shoes. Foley artists find themselves forming a real affinity for some shoes and an aversion to others. There will be certain pairs an artist relies on with regularity and they become like family members. Others are like those cousins one has to visit now and then but really does not want to play with very often.

## Maintenance and Modifications

Some shoes pick up a squeak. A favorite pair of Foley shoes may develop unwanted noises. It might be appropriate to have a squeak for the character. If that is the case, keep that pair as is. A squeak can be eliminated, however, by taping the side of the shoe that squeaks with gaffer’s tape. Many a Foley artist has walked across a studio lot in taped shoes to get a cup of coffee. Another trick is to use Neatsfoot Oil. It softens leather and can eliminate unwanted sounds. Some Foley artists use talcum powder to lessen the creaks.

## Specialized Shoe Collections

Foley artists have shoes that are specialized. They have shoes with cleats, both metal and plastic, for sports movies. They have boots that work for military films, boots for westerns, and boots with various heels.

They have shoes for police officers, fire fighters, doctors, and nurses. They have tap shoes, ballet shoes (both soft and toe), and ballroom dancing shoes. They collect shoes on a regular basis and try them out as often as possible. Good Foley shoes are an artist’s best friends, and they treasure them. When a favorite pair of shoes no longer does the job, either by wearing out or by getting that horrible squeak, Foley artists actually mourn.
