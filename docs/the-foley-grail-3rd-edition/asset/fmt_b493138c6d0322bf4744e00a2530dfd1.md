
Foley artists enhance the sounds of military equipment that characters wear. The backpacks have assorted sundry accessories with emphasized movement of specific elements. Artists have a variety of backpacks, bags, and canvas items on the stage or in their own personal prop collections, but there are times when they must rent the real items from prop companies. It takes time to get the right textures in the sounds. There is an abundance of choices in packs, guns, and other equipment, and all props have particular sound qualities.

The loading of shells and cocking of guns are usually edited in for expediency and specificity. Sound effect libraries have literally thousands of gun sounds. It can be an inefficient use of stage time to perform them, but there have been many films where the artists have been requested to do them either due to a shortage of editing time or a lack of library effects, or because the artist wanted to give the editors and mixers some choices. Again, there are no hard and fast rules, just personal preferences. What really does need to be done is all the handling of guns by the characters.

Guns rattle in films, although in real life, “rattly” guns would be very poorly maintained and dangerous. In film, however, the drama that is added by having a bit of “gun life” can be theatrically tasteful. The trick is to do just a little. Foley artists try to avoid too much rattle because, when played back, it sounds comedic. Some artists use real guns (they plug them for safety), and some have various props for particular gun sounds. Dead bolts, door handles, starting pistols, office staplers, and wrenches have all been gun life at one time or another.

When a film has a gambling scene, there are cards, dice, chips, coins, paper money, felt tables and, in short, lots of activity. These types of scenes can be grueling. There is a lot of detail, and the on-camera action cannot be faked because it is usually the story point of the scene, so the audience is probably going to notice it. Foley artists have to split out the principal on-camera gambling action and be very precise with each card movement, each chip spill, and each dice roll. They then do general background tracks for the atmosphere that they see on camera. Sound editors cut in more background sounds to add another layer.

Roller skates, skateboards, baseballs, bats, golf clubs, tennis racquets, and any sports equipment cues are sweetened in Foley. The artists use the correct props, usually, but if they are not available, the artists consider the sonic elements in the prop and find reasonable replacements. Face slaps, hand claps, caresses, back pats, and even sexual encounters are sweetened in Foley. Brushing hair, brushing teeth, brushing a horse, or any kind of brushing prop is faked with whatever “sells” for a brush and the item being brushed. They do not have horses on the stage, but they may have wigs or furry coats. The more an artist is confronted with a prop, the more the artist has collected a variety of ideas on how to perform it.

At some point in many films and television shows, Foley artists will be asked to do body falls. Normally, a film with a decent budget and time will cut in a hard impact of some sort and rely on the artists to enhance or sweeten the stage. Most Foley artists have some variation of a technique that puts the artist on the floor with cloth, throwing, in a controlled manner, their torso and arms on the ground using the cloth for mass, with the focus being on the character of the particular fall. Some Foley artists actually throw their bodies down on a floor in some fashion. This might not be the safest method, nor does it allow the most control over the sound, feel, and weight of the body landing. Some artists use some kind of contraption they can throw on the ground to mimic a body landing. Again, the actual need from the artist is the particular movement of the character’s fall in the scene. There is an important lesson here: the artist is manipulating a prop or movement to *sound* like the action. Being literal is not necessary, nor desirable.

Jerry Trent recalls that in *Funny Lady* (1975) the close-up of Barbra Streisand for the song “Great Day” showed her clapping out of sync with the song. Trent was supposed to Foley the claps. He followed picture, and he was asked to “fix it” and put it in sync with the music. “How?” he asked, “It’s a close-up. I can’t change the picture.” This is an illustration of a common problem Foley artists have with props. They are asked to fix something that will result in drawing attention to the lack of continuity, when their job is to do the opposite. The solution is always to perform the prop in the best way that covers the visual error. The artist might need to skip one impact, add an impact, or change the quality of the sound entirely. It bears repeating: no rules, just conventions. Artists do what works.

## LISTEN, DON'T LOOK!

At one time or another, a Foley artist might have to deal with an authority figure’s input about how to perform a prop cue. One solution that many artists have used is to do the props behind a baffle on the stage so the person cannot see what is being used. The reason for this is simple. Typically, the person thinks that the appearance of the prop has a relationship to the sound. The Foley artist has thought carefully about how to create the sound and has a plan. So, by occluding the vision of the onlooker, the artist can proceed to perform the prop and ask how it sounded. The problem can then become the onlooker asking, “What did you use?” as though it really matters. Some artists will use the exact same thing that was objected to earlier by pretending to try another idea, but just hiding the prop. Austin Foley artist Susan Fitz-Simon speaks for most artists when she comments, “you can’t tell someone how to use the prop.” Performance is personal, specific, and an art. Regard less of how another might assume the cue should be performed, no two sound professionals see or hear a cue in the same way.

Alyson Dee Moore tells a story where she had been using a can opener for a gun cue—a reasonable choice. The mixer at the time, who is now an excellent Foley artist, insisted she use something different. He did not believe the prop would work. After several attempts to make the sound work using props he insisted she use, Moore went behind a baffle and performed the cue. The mixer said with enthusiasm, “That was great! What did you use?” Moore, quite amused, answered, “What do you think? My can opener.” The baffle shields all from the critical eye and lets the critical ear be the judge. It is not always the tool, but how you use it. Moore says to all budding Foley artists, “Whatever you think is going to work, try it. You just never know what is going to work.” Remember: Foley is an art. Artists have to be bold and trust their it safe, they may never find the most artful and creative sound.

Robert Mott described what he calls the “watching directors.” In the days of making live sound effects for television news, some of the directors would watch him work and be concerned about what he was using. “They used to listen with their eyes,” Mott remarked with an impish smile. The lesson is simple: watch the film and listen to the prop. Do not watch the prop. It affects one’s perception.
