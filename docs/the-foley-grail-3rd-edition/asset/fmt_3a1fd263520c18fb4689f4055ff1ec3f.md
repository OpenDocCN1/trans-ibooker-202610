
Andrea Bloome is considered by the rest of the team to be “the brains behind the business.” She strives to have a positive interaction with all of her clients and believes the key to a successful company is a truly collaborative approach to the work. She remarks, “you’ve got to hire the right people; the right fit … my job is to facilitate their creativity. I remove the roadblocks … we need to be a cohesive unit.” Husband Leslie, a 30-year veteran Foley artist, worked in Los Angeles with various artists such as Paul Holzborn, John Post, Rick Partlow, and Kitty Malone and also spent time working at the Saul Zaentz Film Center in Berkeley in the late 1990s with Foley artists Margie O’Malley and Marnie Moore. “The budget is the real issue,” he says, and he concerns himself with “what we can bring out and how can we help the story sonically.” He emphasizes that “building the team is everything to us. Everyone has been a fantastic addition.” The facility has two Foley stages with control rooms, one convertible Foley-ADR studio, and editing rooms, along with Foley prop storage areas and a main reception area. The stages have areas of “live space, dead space, and really dry space,” laughs Leslie.

Joanna Fang, a graduate of New York University in film and music, has worked at Alchemy as a Foley artist for over six years; she relates that she was not taught a lot about sound while at the university. Most of her experience was working on student and independent films. She performed the tasks of sound editor, Foley and rerecording mixer, and Foley artist. She found Foley to be the most intriguing and has found a home at Alchemy. While she says that, at film school, “they don’t teach you adaptability … it is interesting how quickly things become dogma,” a great thing about working at Alchemy is “we’re constantly evolving our sound … we inherently know what the other person needs.” She adds that there is a “measured openness” to new ideas.

Nick Seaman, lead Foley editor, says, “a lot of people can wear multiple hats,” to which Ryan Collison, Alchemy’s lead Foley mixer, adds, “I had to put everything aside, my pride, my sensitivities,” because it is essential that “everyone is on the same page.” Collison had worked at Sound One in New York City, and he now commutes from his home in Queens to Peekskill. Laura Heinzinger, who works closely with Seaman as a Foley editor, discusses their approach to Foley cueing, “The budget comes into play.” She likes to pick out the key plot points of the film, while concerning herself with “what the mixer is looking for” in the Foley session. Collison agrees that Foley sessions run smoothly when cues are “specific and consistent, names spelled properly,” and he likes information about the surface and even shoe type. Details such as if a surface is wet or dry, for example, are helpful. Fang adds, “every person knows every shoe, every prop.” Apprentice Annie Taylor, a graduate of Savannah College of Art and Design (SCAD), interned with Leslie Bloome on the Foley stage for three months and was hoping to be a Foley artist. When the opportunity to work full time at Alchemy arose, provided she would be willing to be an editor instead, she was more than happy to adjust her expectations. She found cueing and performing Foley to be much different than she had experienced at SCAD, which she found to be more theoretical. Cueing for a real show is stricter than in the sound design program at SCAD, and she also muses that, at SCAD, Foley shoes were “communal.”

Leslie Bloome, Fang, and Heinzinger are active musicians. Bloome plays bass, Fang is a guitarist, and Heinzinger is a pianist. Annie Taylor, the newest addition to the team, plays bassoon. Taylor laughs, “the bas-soon is the cello of the woodwinds.” Both Fang and Heinzinger have also been involved with film scoring.

What is notable about the sound team at Alchemy Post is the noncompetitive and supportive work environment they enjoy. As Fang explains, both Bloomes brought their collective business philosophies from their other experiences to Alchemy to create a special work environment. As Leslie Bloome expresses it, “I wanted to create sounds without boundaries.”

## FILM SCHOOL FLOWS

Both Anna Gramlich (*The Goldbergs*, *Madame Secretary*) and Shaun Burdick (*Empire*, *The Blacklist*) graduated from SCAD in 2010 from the MFA in Sound Design program. They moved to Los Angeles shortly afterward and have been roommates ever since. They both had to find piecemeal work by looking on Craig’s List for any kind of independent projects. By 2014, both had found employment at Sony Studios in Culver City. Burdick and Gramlich credit professors John Sisti and David Stone for their tutelage at SCAD. Both feel that having these two film sound professionals as teachers was very helpful. Gramlich appreciates them for instilling the notion that they would have to be realistic about “pounding the pavement” and recalls being told, “it’s not going to be handed to you.”

The two are both Foley editors and work on different episodic series (often with Foley artists Sarah Monat Jacobs and Robin Harlan), and they have found the workflows to be very different than the more fluid workflows they practiced at SCAD. “We knew what our weaknesses were, “ remarks Gramlich, and she and Burdick helped each other at first so they could refine their skills. While Gramlich prefers editing sound effects and music, Burdick likes editing Foley and dialogue.

What Gramlich and Burdick discovered as they transitioned from students to working professionals is the greater emphasis on collaboration in the workplace. Because both had come from theatre backgrounds, they were glad to discover that working at a film studio includes a sense of teamwork rather than the more independent emphasis they experienced in school. Nonunion work, most often on independent films, had afforded more creativity, but the discipline required to meet deadlines and satisfy producers at Sony has been an essential and welcome adjustment. Burdick and Gramlich agree that the generational differences present some challenges. Gramlich observes that the older professionals do not always like being questioned. “They hold their ground,” she explains.

While the duo assert that they experienced too much emphasis on the tools at SCAD, they quickly admit that the technology speeds up the process, which can be both good and bad. Gramlich likes to work on shows where she can be more creative and likes to consider, “what is it I think it sounds like,” and Burdick found working on *Blacklist* to be “weird … throws really cool curve balls.”
