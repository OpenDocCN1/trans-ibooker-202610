
Matelan’s entry into radio play sound was in 2011 for a production of *The Elephant Man*. She specializes in horror plays and is known as “the one with the cowboy boots,” as she considers herself a true Texan. She sees Foley for audio drama as “an extension of the characters you are illustrating.” She was influenced by Disney sound effects expert Jimmy MacDonald, and she prefers an “economy of movement” when performing Foley for either radio theatre or films.

When the two work on film projects, she performs her Foley in Griffin’s home studio, where he uses Pro Tools. Using cue sheets, Matelan will often add cues to the session to give the client “the luxury of not liking things.” When working on her theatrical radio plays, Matelan utilizes several microphones for a tabletop and the floor and uses a wooden riser. She often has an assistant, and the two combine sounds for more complicated cues. She concerns herself with how many “expendables” she might use for the show and meticulously plans her performance with the discipline of a theatre actor.

During my years as the Telecommunications Department Endowed Chair at Ball State University in Muncie, Indiana, I was privileged to be the Foley sound artist for several Christmastime radio dramas presented at the university for broadcast on Indiana Public Radio. These radio dramas were presented to a live audience at Sursa Hall at Ball State and broadcast simultaneously; they were broadcast again nearer to the holidays. The rehearsal process was similar to that of a theatre production, with the director leading the cast in table readings, theatre improvisations, and the more practical aspects of using voice as the primary mode of expressing character and relationships, rather than visual blocking and movement. Two directors, Jennifer Blackmer and Mathew Reader—both from Ball State’s theatre department—rotated duties year to year. While their directing styles were different, both Blackmer and Reader had a deep appreciation for what the Foley sound person could bring to the story.

## Impressionism for the Ears

I had participated in radio dramas before and had established my personal methodology for creating sounds using the cinematic sensibilities I had honed over my decades in film sound, along with my experience as a professional actor. My approach was to consider what the radio listener would “fill in” aurally when combining the dialogue spoken by the actors, the music played by the orchestra (yes, a live orchestra), and the sounds I contributed. Thus, I found I was creating sounds with the oddest combinations of props to make convincing “aural plot points” to support the narrative. Whether it was vocalizing animal sounds, walking on surfaces to replicate grass, snow, concrete, dirt, wood, or carpeting, or creating environmental sounds and prop sound effects, I wanted to engage the imagination of the listener with the poetry of impressionism.

An example of this was creating a car sound for the taxi in *It’s a Wonderful Life*. I decided to give the listener a sense of being inside the taxi, rather than doing the predictable engine motor, gear shifts, brakes, etc. that one would anticipate. Instead, I focused my efforts on creating the *inside* of the taxi with the sense of movement and sound the inhabitants would experience. It was my sense that hearing the dialogue while the characters were riding in the taxi would be more intimate and avoid the issue of sound recordings or the predictable car sounds. My props were a skateboard, the top of a tackle box, and a leather massage square for seat creak. I placed the skateboard atop the tackle box top and manipulated a rickety sense of fast travel with my left hand; with my right hand, I creaked the leather against my torso. I recall that director Reader was curious about this contraption I had designed, but hearing it back over the speakers during the performance and later on the radio broadcast, he found the choices quite convincing.

## Character Signifiers

Another example of an alternative prop choice I utilized was for *A Christmas Carol*. In this radio drama, the ghosts of past, present, and future were all created strictly from their dialogue with Scrooge. It occurred to me to design a signature sound for each one that would alert the listener to the magic and apparition-like presence of each ghost. It seemed to me that each ghost needed a different leitmotif that distinguished it from the others. I used bells and jingles for ghost past, a bell tree for ghost present, and ominous creaks and squeaks for ghost future. For Marley’s ghost, I utilized several sets of chains of varying pitches and weight and hit them continually against the door or my body, depending on the story location, to imply a sense of entrapment in his own relentless sense of doom. Sound evokes emotion and a haptic sense of identity in the listener. Paul Brown, producer of Ball State University’s PBS station, directed the documentary *Comfort and Joy—An IPR Radio Drama Retrospective*, including interviews with many of us who were involved with these projects.

## Cast and Crew

In 1999, I was asked to play the part of the sound person for the musical production *Wicked City Blues*, by Cornell Christianson and Norman Thalheimer, for a performance at the Tiffany Theatre in Hollywood. The musical is a send-up of a film noirish detective story played by radio actors, and my role was as the sound person who was also one of the characters. We did this show again in 2000 at the Norris Theatre in Palos Verdes, and once again for a backer’s audition at the York Theatre in New York City in 2004. Since that time, the show has been performed in other places with other casts and has been reworked.

In our various productions of this show, my role would grow to include some physical comedy and even a tap dance (Norris Theatre production), which put focus on the artistic contribution of the sound (Foley) artist in the radio play. The New York production included Tony nominee Jenn Colella (*Come From Away*) as the leading lady.

What was important for me to understand in this role was that I was both the performer and the actual sound creator for the radio drama part of the story. It was an interesting paradox to be both cast and crew. What any Foley artist may discover when doing sound for a radio drama is that it is necessary to be aware of one’s onstage presence, while knowing when not to distract from the story being told. This nuanced balancing act requires a real understanding of theatre, as one is contributing both visual and audio components, being part of the cast, but not stealing focus from the characters on stage.
