
In *The Lion King* (1994), as we watch the hyenas and Scar banter back and forth, we notice that all of the footsteps and body movements are evident. However, as the song “Be Prepared” begins, the focus of the sound, including Foley, changes. We are now attending to the song, as is the Foley. Instead of hearing every footstep, we hear only the prominent ones. We hear the marching hyenas and a few of Scar’s more obvious footsteps. We hear the bones of dead animals, played heavily and brightly over the music. We hear only the loud body hits and falls. The song is the star, not the Foley. When the song finishes, the sound effects and Foley become, once again, more prominent. The sound effects, Foley, and final mixing choices are altered to accommodate the music.

*The Triplets of Belleville* (2003) is a wonderfully creative film with an offbeat story, unusual characters, inventive animation, and a soundtrack that supports writer-director Sylvain Chomet’s vision. The Foley is sparse, as is the animation style. There is almost no dialogue in the entire film, so the story relies totally on the animation and the sound.

A young boy lives with his grandmother and becomes a bicyclist. The grandmother has a clubfoot. In one sequence of scenes, we watch the young boy open a box and take out his new puppy. The box is cardboard and has paper in it. The prop work is underplayed and artful, yet it is not overly precise. The dog footsteps are prominent as we discover that the dog’s main goal is to bark at the train. He travels up and down the stairs several times a day to give the riders their daily salutation. The grandmother, while making the boy’s bed, discovers a scrapbook showing his passion for bicycles. She buys him a tricycle, which causes him to clap his hands with glee.

As the sequence progresses, we see the passing of time as the dog, now aged, goes up the stairs to bark at the metro, but more slowly now. We watch the grandmother following her grandson, who is now grown up, as he trains on his bike. She spurs him on with her whistle. After the ride, he flops onto the dining table while his grandmother uses ordinary household items—a vacuum cleaner, eggbeater, and lawnmower—to relax and sooth his muscles.

The film has a charm unlike other animated films. The style is indicative, rather than detailed and lush, and the characters have only the most important and exaggerated characteristics etched on their faces and bodies. We are voyeurs, watching and listening to them proceed through their lives. The Foley is never obtrusive or lifelike. It is like the animation—illustrative and whimsical.

It is essential to mention *WALL-E* (2008). Not only is the film special for its wonderful vocals and sound design by Ben Burtt, but the special inventive Foley for the futuristic dystopian yet lighthearted animated feature, created by Foley artists Dennie Thorpe and Janna Vance, is masterful in its execution.

Today’s animated films are completed in postproduction sound much the way live-action films are. The sound effects and Foley are added in after most of the animation is completed or at least sketched in. This, however, was not always the case. In the early days of sound effects in animated films, the sounds—and there were very few—were recorded like the voices: before the animation was drawn. The animators had to draw the characters and movement synchronized to the voices and the sound effects. A crewmember, called the track reader, was responsible for analyzing, frame by frame, where the sounds would be located. The animators relied on the track reader and drew in the specific mouth movements to match the voices and the action to match the sound effects. Before sound effects were part of the soundscape in animation, music was the aural accompaniment in cartoons. The use of various stingers, drum hits, and other expressive orchestration emphasized the action in the narrative. The term “Mickey Mousing” came to mean the music literally mimicked the action on screen. In film scoring, composers like Max Steiner would use this device to parrot the action on screen in early sound films.

## [Historical Tracings of Animation Foley](#content.xhtml#bck_sec11_1_1)

Once sound effects were recorded for use in animated films, many of the witty and inventive sounds we have come to identify in animation hail from Jimmy Macdonald, who worked at Disney Studios. Macdonald, the voice of Mickey Mouse after Walt Disney and a sound expert at Disney, altered our expectations of sound effects in animation. Macdonald had been a drummer, and he was also an engineer. Because of his unusual background, he was often called on to create his own sound “gadgets,” which later became revered props in the Disney prop room. They were invented by a man whose whimsy and imagination, and whose skill as a musician, engineer, and voice actor, contributed to his playful and rhythmic perspective on sound. Macdonald—originally from Scotland—constructed over 200 contraptions, all nicely categorized alphabetically in a card catalogue.

Although he was not specifically a Foley artist, Macdonald did do some of what Foley artists do. He used specially made props for specific sounds for animation. The conventions he set with this special gift of his changed the way Foley artists approached animation Foley forever. Instead of the reliance on music for sound effects, as had been the tradition in theatrical cartoons for many years, the audience was becoming accustomed to special “bings and boops” and many other indescribable sounds that present-day artists create as well. The irony is that, after Macdonald retired and there was yet another regime change at Disney, many of his props were thrown in the dumpster. The old prop room was being cleaned out for rebuilding, and those who were doing the dumping did not know what they had. Fortunately, Macdonald found out about this from his colleague Joe Herrington and actually retrieved many of these props from the dumpster. Presently, Herrington is the official curator for Jimmy’s inventions.

Treg Brown was to Warner Brothers what Jimmy Macdonald was to Disney. The animated sound effects, so familiar to audiences who are fans of Warner Brothers cartoons, are the legacy of Treg Brown. Brown was a musician who had played with, among other bandleaders, Red Nichols. During the Depression, he worked at Paramount as a music editor on features for Cecil B. DeMille. Eventually, Brown went to Warner Brothers and worked with Carl Stalling on the Warner Brothers cartoons. While Stalling composed music with sound effects sensibilities, Brown, as a musician, added sound effects with his musical sensibility. The Warner Brothers cartoon sound effects style—famous for its musi cality and humor—was due to the collaboration of Brown and Stalling. Both men had the unique ability to anticipate when the other would contribute the aural gag.

What is really essential to understand about Macdonald and Brown is that the sound effects we have come to expect to hear in animated films hail back to inventive imaginations from the early days of animation. The conventions, styles, and even the timing of animation and television cartoons are historically linked to the first animated shorts, which were accompanied by music and a few well-placed sound effects that were added in for specific dramatic and comedic purposes.
