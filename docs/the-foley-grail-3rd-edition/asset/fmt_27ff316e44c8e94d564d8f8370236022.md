
# Mix and Match

## The Foley and Rerecording Mixers

Ask a Foley artist who their best friend at work is, and almost invariably it will be the Foley mixer. An artist can be gifted, professional, and efficient yet create disappointing Foley for a project. The reason is simple: the mixer and the artist work in collaboration, and both the artist(s) and the mixer work more cooperatively when they have similar philosophies about the work as well as compatible personalities on the stage. The relationship between Foley artist and Foley mixer is so symbiotic that these partnerships can last for many years and produce exceptional Foley with a signature sound that is recognized and respected throughout the professional sound world, as well as be responded to viscerally by the film-going public. It is not unusual for a supervising sound editor to rely on a favorite team of Foley artist(s) and Foley mixer to provide consistent quality and style year after year.

Foley artists can have frustrating experiences with mixers, and navigating a project can become difficult. On a Foley stage, the artist(s) and mixer are spending day after day in a dark room, making noises for a film or episodic series for a supervisor who may have a short postproduction sound schedule or one that includes many film editing changes. Trust between artist and mixer ensures that all challenges are easier and more expeditious, with as little conflict as possible. Thus, it is important at this point to address the role of the Foley mixer in detail and explain how the mixer can best support and work in concert with the Foley artists assigned to the show. Additionally, it is essential for the Foley artist to understand how to be the best partner for the mixer.

## THE MIXER IS ANOTHER SET OF EARS

Ordinarily, the Foley artists are the primary concern when a supervising sound editor (SSE) is putting the Foley team together. Whether the decision about the artists is based on a past relationship, a specific genre of film, or a contractual deal with a company, it is usually the case that the SSE is concerned with who the Foley artists are. Normally, the “lead” Foley artist is the first point of contact for the SSE, and the responsibility for the second artist may be left to either the primary Foley artist or the editor. Additionally, sometimes the SSE has a preferred Foley stage for the Foley sessions. Sometimes the artists come attached to a particular stage. Rarely, however, does an SSE decide to have the Foley performed based solely on who the Foley mixer is; however, rerecording mixers have preferences that might be influential. That being said, there is nothing like a great Foley mixer who knows how to make the most of the room, the acoustics, the microphones, the technology, and, at times, even the artists. The best Foley mixers have the added quality of knowing how to evoke the best art from the talent on the stage. A wise Foley artist values the input of the Foley mixer and lets the mixer know it.

Almost every Foley artist has a favorite mixer. Some have several they really enjoy working with, while others will work only with one. All artists have different preferences for what they like in their mixer. But they all prefer to work with mixers with whom they have a rapport. The same can be said for the mixers. Foley mixers work hard to make the Foley artists’ work sound as good as possible. Even a beginning mixer has a lot to offer the Foley artist. To begin with, the mixer is another set of ears.

## MORE THAN SIMPLY RECORDING

The Foley mixer is responsible for actually recording the sounds the Foley artist creates. What is usually confusing to those not in the field is why the person recording the sounds is not called a recordist or why they are not called an engineer, as the counterpart in the music industry is called. The job title has to do with the union to which the mixer belongs.

In production, the person who records the sound in the studio or on location is called the production mixer. This is because this person is actually mixing the production dialogue, not just recording it. Thus, the convention has been extended to include the person who records the Foley and the ADR and rerecords in the final dub. Anyone who records sound in film is called a mixer, except for the field recordist, whose specialty is to go out and record customized sound effects for a particular project. There used to be actual recordists on the Foley stage. When Foley was recorded on magnetic film or 24-track tape, a recordist would listen for pops, cracks, or any magnetic tape dropouts from flaws in the recording medium. Additionally, the recordist would keep cue sheets and set up the studio. But today, with all equipment being digital, there is no need for a recordist any longer. There are no cue sheets to shuffle and mark, there are no magnetic film reels to change over, and there is no need for another set of ears to listen for sound problems. Digital recording has ensured that the unwanted sounds will show up in a wave glitch that is visible to the mixer. Regardless, the mixer does the actual recording.

## FOLEY MIXING IS AN ART

The Foley mixer has usually worked their way up the ladder by being a recordist and, in the days of magnetic film, a loader. The loader actually loaded the magnetic film onto the recorder. The recordist would sit in a separate room wearing headphones, would listen for any problems in the track, and would support the mixer in any way necessary. The Foley mixer would be allowed to sit in that chair only after learning those two previous jobs.

Foley mixing is an art, but a mixer needs some technical knowledge. Today, because of the technology involved with mixing, the mixer needs to know a great deal about Pro Tools and some of the plug-ins that can be used with the Pro Tools software (or a parallel editing and mixing system). But the main qualities of a good mixer include having a good ear for aesthetics, an excellent eye for sync, a skilled hand at recording and punching in for special cues, and an understanding of the job of the artist. As has been para phrased by many professional Foley artists, the Foley artist’s job is on one side of the window, and the mixer’s job is on the other side. They need to understand what each other does, but not interfere.

Before digital recording and mixing on the Foley stage became the standard, mixers recorded most Foley without adding perspective in the recording. There was an expectation that the Foley mixer was recording the sounds flat and that all perspective or reflection in the sound would be added during the final dub. This industry standard led to the practice of focusing on clean mixing, with very little extra character added to any particular sound. The goal was to sound realistic, with a few exceptions. The task of the rerecording mixer included predubbing the Foley to better complement the production track, sound effects, ADR, and music. Any enhancements or nuances added to the Foley were left to the skills and aesthetic taste of the rerecording mixers
