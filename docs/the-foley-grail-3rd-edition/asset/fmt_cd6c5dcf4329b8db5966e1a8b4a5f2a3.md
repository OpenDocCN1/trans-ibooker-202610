
Animated features are treated with the same budgetary and artistic considerations as live-action feature films, and they have similar post-production schedules. This is not necessarily true when considering episodic animation in comparison to episodic live-action shows, whether they are long-story formats or weekly shows. Cartoons are made on short schedules and tight budgets. A half-hour animated television show will be given one day for the complete Foley treatment. Additionally, most cartoon shows have a budget for only one Foley artist. The work is fast and furious. Rather than create the story world fresh, there is a codified setting that is repeated weekly with known characters and progressive actions or storylines.

Often, television animation is not edited. The Foley mixer will try to create enough life on the stage so the rerecording mixers do not have to do too much predubbing. However, there is not much prepping before the tracks go to the dub stage.

## Combining Animation With Live Action
Director Kevin Lima always wanted to be an animator. With his background in theatre and puppetry, in 1980 he enrolled in Cal Arts, where many animators learn their craft. “That’s where Disney animators were made,” muses Lima. He adds, “I’m an actor who hides behind a pencil.” After working at Disney as a character animator, Lima’s directorial debut was *A Goofy Movie* (1995). As supervising Foley editor, I worked closely with Lima, his picture editor Greg Perler, and SSE David Stone, and I learned a great deal about Lima’s interest in sound for animation. “You were my teachers,” Lima revealed to me, as he discussed working with Stone and his sound crew during his first real entry into sound for animation. Lima believes sound for animation is when “you’re building everything from the ground up,” with an “aural landscape of our own choosing.” That is the salient point about sound effects and Foley for animation: the sound is specific to the narrative imagined by the animators. Beginning with the voices of the actors, which drive the emotional character of the animation, the story world is created from scratch.

When Lima transitioned to the hybrid movie *Enchanted* (2007), he recalls, “I had those tools in my box, and the emotional journey of the characters and the sound worked hand in hand.” Lima was mindful to have his story world move from a real-life world to the animated world, and he even considered how the footsteps of the characters would gradually transition from animation to live action. What Lima learned from working on his various projects with different sound teams was that “some think deeply, some think literally.” Regardless of the approach, Lima is not one to impose his perspective automatically. “It’s such a collaborative artform. You can’t rule with an iron fist.” Lima believes he is only one piece of the puzzle; “we are all storytellers,” says Lima. What I learned from Lima is how much pleasure a sound professional can experience when working with a gifted, open-minded, and funny director. When anyone asks me what some of my favorite films were to work on, without exception I always include *A Goofy Movie*. Lima’s word to the wise is “too much sound becomes distracting.”

## GAMING THE SYSTEM
![](Images/fig0021.jpg)

Today’s interactive games, whether online, apps, or consoles, are very sophisticated. Games are complicated and utilize the most advanced techniques in animation and sound design. There are detailed storylines with many decisions for the players. Along with these decisions come distinct sound choices. Once again, genre plays a part. If the game is more playful, the sound choices have a light and whimsical quality. The more mysterious games utilize sounds that evoke unsettling emotional responses. What is important to note is that Foley is used within the games in varying degrees. A lot of game sound design relies on library sounds that are programmed in to respond to the player’s decisions. A Foley artist performs the scenes with the same purpose as that applied to the most expensive and dramatic films. The difference is in the length of the sequences. In games, the sequences are quite short, as a rule. Each alternate one is performed with specificity and detail, and a great deal of attention is paid to the style and genre of the game. The more choices afforded the player, the more Foley that is included in the action. What is more intriguing is the cross-pollination between games and films as entertainment on the big and small screens. Sound becomes equally important to both. However, as games become more complex, fewer of the sounds are from libraries and more sounds are specially designed. This affords more opportunities for sound design and Foley. The question often arises as to whether Foley can be replaced by digital means. This point can be argued several ways. As the book continues, I will offer perspectives on why it is unlikely that the ideal way to utilize Foley will be to digitize it. If one recalls that Foley is a performance, and an organic one at that, then while economics makes it enticing to replace it with a mechanical or digital technique, the difference between what can be edited and what can be performed will always be just that—the difference.

As we move into the next phase of sound in media, whether it is film, advertising, television, animation, the internet, or games, there will be a constant adjustment to the use of Foley. It is impossible to predict how technology or cultural styles will affect how sound in general and Foley specifically will be utilized. As with all good education, we study the classical methodology and learn to work with the variations according to the circumstances. However, it is essential to stay flexible as the convergence culture becomes ever more pervasive. Television, games, animation, and film are now all possible to consume on the iPad, Nook, Kindle Fire, smartphone, and other such personal electronic devices. In no time at all, will we be able to personally choose how we wish the narrative to proceed? How will that change what the art of Foley will be responsible for?

If you are someone who games, either using software or online, at some point, you have probably wondered how the sound is integrated into the games. If you are an expert at gaming, then it is more likely that you have some knowledge about sound drivers and how the interactive choices you select alter the abundance of sound choices embedded in the game. What may not be as clear to you is how Foley is cued, performed, designed, and selected for the games you play. Perhaps you are curious about how Foley in games differs from Foley in films or episodic television.
