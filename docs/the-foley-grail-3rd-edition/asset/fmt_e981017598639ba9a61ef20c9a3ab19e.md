
Later, as technology changed, recording to hard drives became the norm, and more bells and whistles were available to the Foley mixer. As a result, it was only a matter of time before some mixers began to expand their role on the Foley stage. Add to this the normal effect of the marketplace preferring the least expensive and most productive use of labor, along with the modifications of professional sound unions to allow more crossover in editing and mixing tasks, and the result has been an expanded role for the Foley mixer. Many Foley mixers now utilize tools that were previously the territory of the rerecording mixer. Addition ally, sound editors can predub on their digital workstations, and mixers can perform minor editing tasks with Foley before forwarding the recorded tracks to the editor and/or rerecording mixers.

Foley mixers are as different in their approach to their work as Foley artists are. No two mixers or artists perceive their role exactly as someone else does, nor do they practice their craft in the same way. Their specific personalities, backgrounds, and aesthetic preferences vary greatly. The common thread between all of them is the workflow and professional process that is expected of them.

## THE CLASSICAL APPROACH

Robert Deschaine started mixing at Gomillion Sound in Hollywood in the early 1970s. Ted Gomillion needed a studio manager, so Deschaine was hired. At this point, he had no experience with microphones, cables, recording, or mixing films at all. He decided to learn all the jobs that were performed at the studio. “Back in those days there were a few studios—Producers Sound, Cine Sound, Gomillion Sound—where people could get training. We didn’t get much money, but we worked long hours and we learned our craft,” Deschaine remembers.

Before long, Deschaine was mixing Foley, ADR, and final dubs. He knew every job in the postproduction sound recording and mixing world. Because he would be the rerecording mixer on the films and television shows where he mixed Foley and ADR, he had a unique perspective on how to best mix the Foley. Deschaine remembers someone at Gomillion telling him, “Foley is supposed to be there, and you’re not supposed to pick it out.” With that in mind, he became very aware of how to mix Foley so that it fit in with the scene. He found that all Foley artists had different styles and approaches: “Sometimes there were people who were heavy on their heel-toe or toe-heel, and I could always tell that someone wasn’t doing it right when I got it on the dub stage.”

Deschaine learned to mix at a time when Foley was entering a period of developing specialization in the field. While a lot of the editors were still performing their own Foley, a new breed of Foley walkers was being introduced into the mix. Also, at this time, Foley was recorded onto three tracks of 35-mm magnetic film called a full coat. Deschaine would get the picture and cue sheets for the Foley at 8:00 a.m., and it had to be completed by 6:00 p.m. so it could be dubbed for the next airdate:

> I saw a lot of people \[Foley artists\] who were very talented and could do it quickly and get it in sync, and we didn’t have to cut it. We made sure that when it left the stage everything was in sync because they \[the editors\] didn’t have time to cut it.

### Good Storytelling With Sound Should Be Foremost in the Mixer’s Mind

Because Deschaine had been involved with production and was not yet a rerecording mixer, he was aware of making the Foley sound as much like production sound as possible: “I had to learn perspective and moving the microphone where you think it would be if they were on location.” He was careful about “not getting it too close to the mic or too hot and hopefully you won’t distort.”

Deschaine also mixed ADR, and because he had been an actor himself, his past profession influenced his approach to both Foley and ADR. He has a real admiration for Foley artists who are well prepared and do their homework for each project.

> I would watch these people and they would put their minds into whoever the actors or actresses were, and they would emulate these people. When you would finally put it all together, you would think the actors or actresses were really walking that scene.

Deschaine also recalls, “I would walk in my mind. I would visualize that I’m into their feet. I’d have to walk with them so I could punch in when their foot is in the air, so I didn’t erase anything.” Of course, technology has come such a long way since the days of magnetic tape or 24-track, so mixers have different methodologies for punching and editing on the stage. But because Deschaine trained during a period of more rudimentary equipment, his skill was honed to be specific and efficient.

A mixer with a background as vast and as long-lived as Deschaine can bring to the Foley stage a sensibility of filmmaking that many who have honed their craft on digital systems may need more time to develop. The tools will always change. But good storytelling with sound should be foremost in the mixer’s mind. Sound serves the narrative. Foley is rarely the star of the film, and it is critical to remember the lessons that a veteran mixer like Deschaine can pass on to the younger Foley mixer who is enthralled with technology. Learning on less sophisticated tools empowered Deschaine to refine his skill to a point where he was not reliant on digital when he made the transition. He could make a beautiful and seamless punch in between footsteps and never make the Foley artist repeat the cue. That is what his well-trained skill allowed him to do for his artists.

### Pushing the Envelope

Deschaine is one of the most experienced mixers around and also one of the most empowering. A devoted team player who believes in collaboration, Deschaine has trained several Foley and ADR mixers with respectable careers who began as recordists with him. One of those mixers is David Jobe, who has been mixing for about 25 years. He began his sound career at a small company that worked in electronic newsgathering. He then worked his way into a job at Warner Hollywood in the late 1980s as a “dummy loader.” He loaded the recorders with magnetic film for those who mixed on dubbing stages. As his career progressed, his swiftness at doing his tasks, his intelligence, and his amiable personality led him to a position as an ADR recordist for Deschaine at Skywalker South. It was not long before Deschaine recognized Jobe’s intelligence and discipline and allowed him to mix a few cues on the ADR stage while Deschaine watched and coached. This used to be the traditional method for training new mixers in Hollywood. The studio was a training ground for new mixers. If a recordist seemed to have talent and a good work ethic, they would be encouraged to mix.

Jobe caught on quickly, and before long, he would come into work and be told he had to mix ADR because Deschaine was sick. Jobe still does not know if that was the true story, as Deschaine is known for being generous with his time and his mixer’s chair. Either way, Jobe was moved up to mixer. Thus, he was baptized by fire. Shortly after, he was assigned his own stage at Skywalker South and began his career mixing Foley. He mixed there for several years before going to Warner Brothers in Burbank, where he has been mixing Foley for 14 years.
