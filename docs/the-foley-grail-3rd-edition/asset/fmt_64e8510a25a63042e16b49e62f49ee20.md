
Jim Ashwell, originally from Topeka, Kansas, began his career in music recording. After taking music engineering workshops at the Dick Grove School of Music in Los Angeles, he began practicing his skills at various music studios in area. One studio, called One Step Up, provided Ashwell the opportunity to work as the second engineer, where he worked with such artists as The Eagles. “I hung out and refused to go home,” Ashwell muses. He also worked at The Record Plant, where he did what he calls “overflow” work. By 1982, he was recording sessions on his own and began to transition into being the Foley recordist for some Foley sessions. In 1986, Ashwell became a Foley mixer and worked with many of the more experienced Foley artists in Los Angeles at the time. One of these Foley artists was Dan O’Connell, with whom he had a long professional relationship. O’Connell partnered with John Cucci and started his own Foley stage, coincidentally named One Step Up. The irony is not lost on Ashwell. In 2012, Ashwell, O’Connell, and Cucci went to Fox Studios, where the two Foley artists still work and Ashwell has retired.

### [Less Is More](#content.xhtml#bck_sec7_6_1)

As schedules get tighter and more is asked of those on the Foley stage, the mixer spends more time tweaking the Foley with some edits. Digital recording makes this relatively easy for the mixer, and even union mixers are asked to do more of this than ever before. Boundaries between editors and mixers are blurring quickly. Editors are premixing and mixing and doing quick fix edits. New Foley mixers will have no ethical issue with this. But it is good to remember that this is a relatively recent development and comes primarily from two factors: technological changes and economic imperatives. As producers find more ways to cut budgets, they rely on the technology to dictate the terms of employment rather than allowing the actual craft to create the appropriate employment boundary. Without making any judgments regarding this transition, which is a consequence of digital convergence, it does put more pressure on the mixer to also know how to edit Foley without taking the life out of it. Be cautious and undercut rather than being exuberant with the editing tools. Less is more.

Ask a Foley mixer about microphone preferences and, while the opinions vary, there is a consensus that most mixers have a few favorites and do not spend a lot of time auditioning microphones. A music engineer, conversely, will pick the exact microphone that brings out the best in each instrument and singer individually. There are some standard microphones that will usually appear in the Foley mixer’s repertoire. The Sennheiser 416 is a bright microphone and has good midrange. That is the microphone of choice for Walter Spencer of Hi Fi Foley. Richard James Hinton, a Foley artist in Bristol, England, relates that his mixer prefers the Sennheiser MK 60. That is also Foley artist Julien Naudin’s favorite microphone for many of his cues. Neumann is a familiar microphone brand, as is Schoeps. The preference is personal and very dependent on the sound quality of the stage. Jobe likes the Neumann because it has a large diaphragm. He uses the Sennheiser as a room microphone because it “packs a punch,” and he will often use a Schoeps microphone as well. “You want a quality, warm microphone,” he says.

How a mixer works with the stage and its peculiar qualities is an important aspect of mixing. Mixers need to have the patience to work with whatever issues come up with the sound floor on the stage and not be deterred by them. Whatever concerns occur with the actual recording are not the problem of the artists. So, a good mixer always makes the distinction between when the recording is less than acceptable because of the equipment, room, or pilot error and when the artist’s performance needs improvement. A great mixer is always sure to communicate the reasons for problems with a recording, because the natural tendency of a Foley artist is to change what she or he has done if the cue has to be redone. If the mixer wants to ensure that the artist repeats the same performance, they need to communicate that.

### [It’s a Marriage](#content.xhtml#bck_sec7_6_2)

Another quality of a great Foley mixer is respect, if not adoration, for the artists. The mixer should have a great understanding of how artists approach their craft so that any misunderstandings can be avoided. It is important to understand that the mixer’s job is to accommodate the sounds. The Foley artist creates the sounds. Therefore, simple syllogism dictates that the mixer’s job is to accommodate the artist. All Foley artists are different and approach the work differently. A good mixer is flexible and tries to learn the secret language of each artist so that the day goes smoothly. It also helps to make for a great session if the mixer truly wants the artists to feel free to experiment.

The same is true of the artists toward the mixers. It is helpful if the artist is concerned with the challenges of the mixer. Foley mixers are ultimately responsible for the quality of the recording, which involves working with the room’s sonic personality and troubleshooting any problems that arise. Whereas the relationship between one Foley artist and another while working on a film is like a marriage, so too is the relationship between the artists and the mixer. It is complicated, nuanced, and, at its best, pure magic.

Some Foley artists might find themselves feeling impatient with mixers. If this is the case, it is best to remember that the mixer is busy with so many technical aspects of the job that she or he cannot always pay attention to every issue that comes up on the stage itself. So, the artist that is patient with the mixer and tries to accommodate the mixer’s technical needs will get the best collaboration as a result.
