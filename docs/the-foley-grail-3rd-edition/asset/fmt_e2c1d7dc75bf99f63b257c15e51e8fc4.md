## Walking in Their Shoes
### Performing the Footsteps

DOI: [10.4324/9781003008439-12](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003008439-12)

![](Images/fig0014.jpg)

It would come as no surprise to any film fan who is aware of the role of postproduction sound that, at some point, someone is replacing the footsteps of an actor in a scene. That someone is the Foley artist. It may seem logical that the audience would need to hear the character in the scene walk. However, as we examine the actual dramatic needs in a scene, this may or may not be the case.

Let’s first look at the actual purposes of replacing or enhancing footsteps. When the Foley artist or editor is viewing the scene, many of the footsteps in the scene may be present and sound clear. There may be no reason to do anything at all to the footsteps. Perhaps the character is walking and there is no dialogue, and the scene has production sound that is clear and of high quality. If this is the case, then the Foley artist does not necessarily need to replace the footsteps. Even if the film is going to be released in a foreign market and language is going to be replaced, there is most likely no need to replace the footsteps unless there are ambient sounds from production that the dialogue editor will not be able to clean out or mask. If the production track is fairly clean and there is no dialogue, the footsteps and props from production can remain.

However, there may be dialogue while the characters are walking, footsteps from various edited characters walking on a surface that is distracting or inappropriate, scenes filmed silently (mit out sound), extraneous noise that interrupts the desired dialogue, or the need to use a foreign language over the footstep action. Now the Foley artist has to think about the proper approach to creating the footstep sounds for the character. The artist has to consider the following: is the character male, female, young, old, fast-paced, sluggish, a heavy walker, light and airy, rushed, plodding, anxious, happy, or encumbered in any way? What quality is the actor bringing to the body language and walk of the character? Is there a quirky or distinctive nature to the character’s walk? What does the way of walking tell the audience about the character? The Foley artist should not just put on a logical pair of shoes and start walking. Some artists will audition many pairs of shoes to see which pair seems the most likely match for what the character is wearing (they may not always actually see the character’s feet, and the production track may not offer much guidance) and on what surface(s) the character walks. The artist may consider the “feel” of the character when they walk. The time period and location become critical concerns for shoe choices. Contemporary surfaces are composed of different materials than those of historic times, and the artist will consider this. Thus, just the selection of shoes, if there is ample time budgeted for the film, can be a major decision for each character in each scene. Some artists keep a log of what shoe is identified with a particular character. If time is short and the artist must make decisions quickly, a brief listen to the production track is helpful, as are any spotting notes given to the artist by the supervising sound editor or Foley editor.

## FOLEY THEORY

If you were to think about how Foley should be approached, what do you suppose you would theorize? Perhaps you should think of Foley as the sonic performance of the character. You are supporting the actor’s performance. Perhaps you would agree with Mark Berger, rerecording mixer, who says the Foley “grounds the actor to the earth” or that Foley “creates a sense of place.” Maybe you would like Walter Murch’s perspective on Foley: that it should not be technically perfect, should fool the listener into thinking it is really the production sound, and should sound fairly random.

Perhaps you would prefer the Hollywood hyperreality style of Foley, or maybe you would prefer the New York naturalistic style. Maybe you would like what supervising sound editor Pat Jackson calls “a huge test kitchen” approach to Foley that she had Margie O’Malley, Jennifer Myers, and Marnie Moore use for *The English Patient* (1996). Perhaps you agree with German Foley artist Niklas Matthaei, who says it is essential to “get the emotion in the steps.” You might confirm the perspective of British Foley artist Peter Burgis, who adds, “you’ve got to understand the body language” of the character. Whatever you might decide, there are many approaches artists take regarding what Foley footsteps should sound like and how they should be performed. Some believe the microphone should be very close. Others prefer the microphone to be a bit farther away. Some like to use two microphones: one for the feet or prop and one for room reflection. Some prefer to record the Foley flat with no reverb. Some use headphones while creating the sounds, while others, like Austin Foley artist Susan Fitz-Simon, find headphones an impediment to the physical experience of performing Foley: “I get too ‘interior’ with headphones on.” You can develop your own theories. You do not have to subscribe to any particular method. Foley is not something you perform according to a rubric.

This being said, there are geographical differences that affect the approach to Foley. The Foley in Hollywood tends to have a quality that is “bigger than life” compared to the New York Foley style. Marko Constanzo, who is a veteran Foley artist in New York, places his microphone at a greater distance from his props or his footstep cues than do the Hollywood Foley artists. The New York style of footsteps tends to be more naturalistic, and the preferred aesthetic is that, in most instances, they sound like production.

When the Saul Zaentz Film Center in Berkeley, California, was an active postproduction sound facility, the Foley was integrated with the edited sound effects and followed the Walter Murch or Alan Splet preference of understatement to an almost imperceptible “felt, but not heard” aesthetic. Across the bay, at Skywalker Sound, the Foley has a more “present” quality and collaborates with the aesthetics of the animated, action, and fantasy films that garner the deserved reputations of sound designers Randy Thom, Gary Rydstrom, Ben Burtt, and, more recently, Ren Klyce.

French Foley artist Julien Naudin relates that, when he works on films made for Denmark, Sweden, or Iceland, he uses very soft-soled shoes because people in those countries walk very softly and wear softer shoes. The aesthetic in that part of Europe is different. When he works on other European films, he uses leather-soled shoes because that is the aesthetic for Western Europe.
