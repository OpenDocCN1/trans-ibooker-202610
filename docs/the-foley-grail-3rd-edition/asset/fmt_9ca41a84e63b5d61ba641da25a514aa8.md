
Foley differs significantly from its inception to its interaction within the game. Foley began as a minor player in the gaming world and now is as necessary in most games as the other elements. Sound in games has advanced considerably, largely due to Charles Deenen. Originally from the Netherlands, at 12 years of age Deenen began playing around in his school’s computer lab and wrote a sound driver for games, which he then sold to companies in Europe. By the time he was a teenager, he had started his own company, The Maniacs of Noise, and was selling composing software and the complementary hardware to game companies. At 20, Deenen went to work for Interplay as the head of their audio department—designing sound for the *Lord of the Rings* games—which turned into an 11-year stint improving game sound quality, audience testing, adopting a more Hollywood model for game sound, and outsourcing the music composing. He then took a short break and worked as a sound designer for films in Hollywood. He followed this with ten years at Electronic Arts (EA) in Vancouver, where he oversaw several teams of sound designers and invented techniques to incorporate more believable soundtracks for games. After ten years with EA, he began free-lancing. Deenen now combines his role as a game sound designer with the role of sound editor on feature films, so his experience with sound effects and Foley has had an unusual trajectory.

## More Work Than a Movie

While many film professionals may scoff at the thought of working on a game, it may be surprising to know that the actual design, scripting, acting, visual rendering, and sound design of a game are endeavors that have more in common with an animated film than a live-action movie. The planning and detailed work that are involved with a game are beyond comprehension for those of us who are “civilians.” For our purposes here, we will focus our attention on just sound in general, and Foley more specifically. However, to give an indication of how much work goes into a game, I want to relate anecdotally a *Star Trek* game story. William Shatner came in to voice his lines for the game and saw many sheets of script. He logically assumed it was the script for the entire game. In reality, what he was looking at was the list of his lines alone. There were 25,000 lines of dialogue for the entire game. It took “weeks and weeks and weeks,” says Deenen, to voice all of the characters in the game.

Early on, a player might have a choice of a few paths to take in a game. Thus, the sound choices were minimal. As memory grew, sound designers were able to make more sound choices, adding to the complexity of the games. To illustrate, a simple sword clank might have had three to five choices in the beginning of game sound. Now, sound designers can have soft clanks, medium clanks, large clanks, and over-the-top clanks and have distance and perspective on each clank. Additionally, each clank can be stretched, shortened, deepened, and so on. The purpose of the myriad Foley cues is to enable the game player to enjoy many choices with interactive play.

Foley, in the beginning of its inclusion, might have had a few footsteps or prop sounds that were used in specific places. Now, the beginning of a footstep, the middle of the footstep, the end of the footstep, the longer footfall, or any part of it can be utilized, stretched, and made to accommodate a plethora of choices. The goal is to make every sound become as infinitely variable as is possible with whatever process ing is available. However, there are some Foley sounds that must be performed in real time “as is” and played that way.

## Foley Is More Often Required Than Optional

Interestingly, the discussions about replacing Foley with digital footsteps and such are not part of the lexicon in the high-end game industry. The reality and organic nature of Foley are very desirable in games. It is the nature of the performed action of the Foley as a vast contrast to the designed sound elements in games that appeals to both game designers and players.

Typically, two Foley artists are required for the more deluxe games. The reasons are different than for feature filmmaking. The energy and physical industry required for the kinds of Foley sounds in games can be exhausting. While only one artist works a cue at a time, that artist might pound something metal at high velocity for many takes and simply need a break. So, the other artist takes over for a stretch of time. In feature films and television, the reasons for working in twos have to do with combining sounds for one take or helping each other with cues. However, in games, it is the type of cues that is tiring. Additionally, there is a great deal of repetition with only slight variations that can be a bit numbing to the brain and the body.

Footsteps for games can be tedious. The Foley artist might be repeating the exact same scene with only minor variations, such as scuffs, leans, or even a toe tap. To all but the most perceptive eye and ear, there is almost no difference. But even the most minor difference has to be Foleyed. A surface change, a room change, a different response to an action, or a different placement of a foot all have to be performed fresh as if seen for the first time. It can be tiring for the artist because the vari ations are so slight. Yet, the energy and the characteristics of the game character have to remain consistent.

Footsteps for action games, on the other hand, are challenging for another reason: the artist has to run and run and run, but each time has to change the direction, or the footing, or the impacts and not lose balance or breath. The changes, once again, are minimal, yet substantial.

Props for games vary as well. In some, they are easier, as in a “car game” that requires a “tumbling of objects or bullet impacts,” says Deenen. However, games that include many types of weapons and a lot of variations in the handling of objects require much more time to Foley. A game might take one hour to Foley, or it might take four weeks. In today’s economic struggles, four weeks is a long time to Foley one project.

At one point, Foley artists were performing sound for games without any visual clues as to what the game would look like. They were essentially performing wild Foley, but with a scene having been previously described to them. However, as Quick Time became available, this aspect of performance became easier. Now, artists have some rendering with which to work. Deenen is largely responsible for developing the sound aesthetic in games that is more like a feature film. He was concerned that the visuals were getting better and the sound was not. After working at Interplay for 11 years, he felt the industry was at a standstill and he needed a break.

After working in features for a while, he developed a sense of what he felt was missing from games, and when he returned, he introduced several changes to game sound. This evolution included inventing techniques that allowed the sound to be more believable, which supported the visuals if they were not convincing. He learned from working in films that great sound design can actually help visuals appear better. Now, he believes strongly in getting the best composers to write the score, the best sound designers to design and edit the sound, and the best Foley artists to perform the Foley. First-rate Foley is expensive. It requires a good-sounding stage, two excellent artists, and a gifted mixer who knows how to record Foley for games. That, Deenen discovered, is the real secret: a great mixer who understands the difference between mixing sound for films and mixing sound for games. “The Foley artist will be made or broken by the mixer recording,” says Deenen.
