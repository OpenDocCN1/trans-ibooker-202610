
When a new Foley artist first starts doing paper cues, the drill is often a trial-and-error approach where the artist will overdo the paper and it will have no specific definition or intention. After hearing the cue with too much noise and too little character, the artist will redo it. Finally, after some frustration, the artist realizes how little needs to be done for paper to “read.” Paper cues may look simple; however, a good paper cue takes discipline. Less is more with paper. The cue needs to be specific, with precise page turns and all paper movement clean and understated, unless it is a debris or background “paper life” cue.

Another common prop is what Foley artists might call “cop gear”: that is, the keys, handcuffs, gun, and club that police officers carry on their belts. Foley artists often use a piece of leather, perhaps a small purse or such, varying keys and handcuffs, some item for a gun, and some kind of small piece of wood if the club is obviously moving. Cop gear is a standard and predictable prop in many films. Foley artists will most likely do one track for a principal player and several tracks for background police. They hold the items in a comfortable position and manipulate the props throughout the scene. The tendency is to do too much of the keys and handcuffs because that is the most interesting and identifiable part of the prop cue. However, metal records “hot” as a rule, so underplaying the metal components and focusing on the leather adds a nicer texture to the prop. It should be said that every Foley artist uses a different ratio of metal to leather. Foley is an art, not a science. There are guidelines and conventions, but not hard and fast rules.

Typically, doors are edited into a scene from a sound effects library or region list, although there are Foley artists who actually take the time to open and shut doors on a stage. There can be a problem with this practice. There are so many types of doors in so many types of rooms. It is a far more effective use of time for the sound editor to audition doors in a library and assign them to the appropriate doors in the scenes and assume the Foley artist will deal with the door handles. Additionally, in television, the same rooms are used in every episode, so the same doors are edited in.

Foley artists typically add “chair life” to scenes. Perhaps a character sits in a wooden chair. The artist makes it creak. He or she will use a chair, board, industrial pallet, or anything else with the right creak. Leather chairs need leather squeaks. The artist might use a chair, a couch, or a purse. Rick Sanchez, a former Foley artist turned editor and mixer, gave me a chiropractor’s block, which I used on a regular basis for many leather sounds. Rocking chairs get creaks added in. Rolling chairs need to roll, and turning chairs need to turn. If it adds drama and interest, Foley artists will find a prop to do it.

Hospital and emergency vehicle stretchers or gurneys are sometimes a “prop character” in a scene. The sound of the gurney is important to relay a sense of urgency or drama to the scene. Rather than thinking of the gurney as just a metal rattling or squeaking sound with wheels, an artist might consider the drama of the scene, the importance of the character, and the surrounding activity occurring at the same time. Even a prop as predictable as a gurney can be designed to contain humor, drama, horror, or even anger, depending on the emotion and the music underscore of the scene.

The typing of a computer keyboard is often edited in, if sync is not a problem. When the keyboard is on camera for a principal, however, a Foley artist most likely does the keystrokes. This takes time and patience, as they cannot predict exactly what the actor is going to do. A mixer who can punch in with precision is very helpful. Keyboards for desktops computers, laptops, tablets, and phones all have a different sound. It is a good idea to have several from which to choose. A discerning ear can easily tell the difference. On *Die Hard* (1988), the character of Theo (Clarence Gilyard, Jr.) is a computer expert who continually types on computer keys. These keystrokes were performed on a cheap computer keyboard on the Foley stage. Although the keyboard was inexpensive, it sounded great. As a side note, the film was re-edited after most of the editors were off the show, and there was a new picture insert that did not match the typing. The lesson here? It is better to keep at least one editor to conform sound edits right up through the final dub.

## PROPS THAT CHALLENGE

Horror films have standard props. Most cineastes know by now that Foley artists often use celery for bones crunching. While edited effects also have sounds to use for this kind of cue, Foley artists often add the celery in because it is easy, sounds great, and is easy to control for timing and dramatic effect. They often use liquid detergent on their hands for the goopy sounds, and they might add in a wet chamois or even sounds they can make with their mouths. Some Foley artists use various meat items from the butcher section for ripping flesh or crunches or such. Some prefer not to use meat. The point is that the sound of the prop can be inventive and offbeat, but it is how the artist uses these standard props to suit the scene. Several tracks of various components can be performed and played together.

Wing flaps for birds are often a Foley effect. Some use old ballet shoes that they hit lightly against their hands. Some use feather dusters. Some use both, with one artist doing one and one doing the other, or they use multiple tracks. Some use mouth sounds.

Glass breaks are sometimes done on a stage and sometimes edited in. A typical approach is to edit in the sound and enhance it on the stage. In *Die Hard*, there is a scene where gunshots hit the glass windows in the high-rise building of Nakatomi Plaza and the windows shatter. The glass breaks were edited in. The glass shards, however, were made on the Foley stage. Robin Harlan and I worked together to create the Foley for the shards. Robin held large panes of glass while I hit the panes with a baseball bat. The microphone was aimed at the shards as they hit the floor. Lee Tinkham, our Foley mixer, had to be mindful to set the volume to a level where the shards recorded without distortion. We wore eye goggles and gloves and were very careful to get out of the way of the impacts. This kind of cue is intrinsically dangerous and requires lots of protection and experience to be secure in the practice.

Debris is a typical Foley effect. Explosions, violent encounters, rock slides and many other events have debris that falls. Foley artists use dirt, rocks, twigs, coins, dice, or anything they can find that works best for the cue. Usually, several tracks are required. Some tracks will have heavier elements, and others will have lighter or brighter sounding elements. It is always important to consider what the elements should land upon. The wrong prop falling on the wrong surface betrays the sound. Once again, it is not what it looks like, but what it sounds like that matters.

Foliage is a prop that is routine in Foley. However, the items used can vary. The tendency now is to try to get fresh foliage. Foley artists will cut branches off of nearby on-site trees and plants, buy flowers and plants, and bring foliage from their own yards. Sometimes, in a pinch, they “supplement” with quarter-inch tape. This practice comes from the early days of Foley when microphones were not as sensitive and analog recording was not so discerning. If they use quarter-inch tape—assuming they can locate it—they use it sparingly. Artists might choose to use the old recording tape for comedic purposes.
