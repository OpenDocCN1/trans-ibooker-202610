
To be as specific as possible without using too many words is another best practice. This is particularly true if the mixer and Foley artists are not going to be using cue sheets but are going to be reading the cues off Pro Tools or another digital system. One illustration of how easy it is to give too little information comes from the cueing of the film *Platoon* (1986). The Foley editor, who was experienced and knowledgeable, cued “man carrying child.” Although this sounds just fine, the scene included several men, each carrying a child.

Once the footsteps are cued, the next step is to start the reel or scene from the top and begin identifying props. It is preferable to keep similar props on similar tracks. Paper cues should be on different tracks from metal cues or leather chairs, for example. When Foley artists are on the Foley stage, the mixer adjusts the microphone to record certain props. Many Foley artists perform all the paper cues in one sitting, so it is easier if they do not have to hunt for each similar kind of cue. It is helpful to think in terms of “food groups,” like paper, hand pats, body falls, metal crashes, cop gear, horse footsteps, saddle and bridle, debris, and so on. The key is to have a clear road map for the artists and mixer, so everyone is “on the same page,” literally and figuratively.

In the day of magnetic film and then 24-track recording, Foley artists would use tracks judiciously. Now, with Pro Tools, there are fewer limitations on track use. The good news is that it is possible to split out cues and layer more. The bad news is that this encourages the less disciplined editor to throw cues in anywhere without much thought. Once again, however, it is essential to consider the workflow. The people who come after the cueing need to have a secure sense of direction and communication. The more precise and methodical the cueing, the more relaxed and specific the Foley session can be. Foley artists are always under intense time pressure. If Pro Tools crashes or the microphone starts humming, time will be lost while the problem is addressed. Organized cueing allows room for mistakes, retakes, and outtakes.

When it comes to cueing Foley, the editor is serving the needs of the Foley artists and the mixer. Foley stages are expensive and usually booked in advance. The number of Foley sessions booked for a project is usually decided before the first spotting session. The editor should cue only what needs to be performed on the stage. If the effects editor is going to use library effects for the cue, then it does not need to be cued for the stage. It is best to prioritize. What must be done on the stage is what should be cued. It is always acceptable to cue some additional cues that are not necessary. If “wish list” cues are going to be added, then it is wise to notate them as such.

In the words of Foley mixer David Jobe, when it comes to the biggest obstacle on the Foley stage, “It’s time, time, time!”

## CUEING ON THE FLY

When cueing for television or episodic shows, the conventions differ for prime time than for cartoons, reality television, or daytime television. There is virtually no time for cueing for cartoons. So, they are cued on the Foley stage “on the fly,” meaning in real time. As the Foley artists are watching what is happening, they are most likely making some notes about what needs to be done, as is the mixer. These shows are done quickly and with formulaic props. Rather than taking time to design a particular sound for each sonic event, the opposite is true. The Foley artist is performing “guerrilla Foley” and getting as much done as quickly as possible. Sync is not as critical nor is quality.

Foley artist Marko Costanzo and mixer George Lara record the Foley for *The Marvelous Mrs. Maisel*, and the show is cued by Steven Visscher, who is the supervising Foley editor. “He saved us a lot of time by being sensible in what they spotted,” Costanzo relates. Cuing is not always that way, as Costanzo says, “it always depends on who is in charge.”

The spotting sessions so carefully described earlier are the scenario for the big budget features, but they have less in common with the prime-time television shows viewers watch regularly, whether on a flat screen, a computer, or a phone. Even if there is cueing for the Foley, it might be little more than a laundry list of “to dos” for the Foley artists. There is typically no coordinated spotting session. It is a formulaic process where the cast resides in the same venues weekly so the sound changes very little.

Games require short spurts of cues that vary only slightly. It takes less time to cue the Foley for a game, and typically there is less detail to cue. The focus on Foley for games comes more during the performance of Foley. The minute changes with each possible choice a player might make can involve dozens of takes of footstep and prop cues that look almost identical. Thus, the cueing is simpler, but the shooting and editing are very specific.

Other countries do not necessarily follow the same conventions for cueing that has been outlined here. For example, Julien Naudin does not like to follow cued Foley sheets. He does everything intuitively and decides the cueing at the moment of performance. Naudin is a third-generation Foley artist and has a well-developed method for how he likes to work. He has a close relationship with the director Lars von Trier, and the two communicate on an instinctual level about how the Foley should work within a film. Naudin travels to Denmark to work with von Trier, where the director has a rare role with the Foley in that he is in the vicinity and available for any questions or clarifications.

There is a valuable lesson here for anyone who performs Foley for any project anywhere. Budget, time, experience, quality, personnel, and exhibition all have some determination in how much preparation will be accomplished before the Foley artist has to perform the sounds. The ability to be flexible, intuitive, and open-minded is a great asset.

## HEARING THE SWEET SPOT: EDITING FOLEY

Sound editors are introduced to the profession with the most modern type of technology available. Before the digital age, editors would have cut on optical sound or magnetic film. The optical soundtrack had a visual line, similar to the waveform of today, with which to decipher where sound would begin and end. The editor had a visual cue as to what to edit. Magnetic film had no visual reference. The editor had to rely solely on the sound itself. When deciding where to edit in a sound or move the sync of a Foley cue, the editor would mark the place to cut on the magnetic film itself with a white china marker, then cut it with a splicer, hang the piece of sound on the rack of a “trim bin,” and perhaps replace that piece of magnetic film at a spot later in the track. She or he might have to “flop” the track, so the sound would not play against the sound head, if the purpose of replacing the tape was for sync only. Otherwise, the leftover sound would be interjected into the new cue.
