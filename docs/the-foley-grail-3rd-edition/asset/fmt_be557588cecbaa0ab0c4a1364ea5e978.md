
However trying the process might have been for all involved, two things are certain. First, the soundtrack itself is a first-rate sound job, and second, human ingenuity allowed for experimentation.

## [WALLS INSIDE WALLS](#content.xhtml#bck_sec6_6)

Walter Spencer bought a house in Echo Park, which is near downtown Los Angeles, and began working as a music engineer and sound editor. He was working on low-budget films and was finding he was doing every task on the sound: editing, Foley, and mixing. He asked himself a question: “Can I turn this house into a place that can do everything?” He decided to try.

He built a Foley stage in his basement, which is 14 feet × 20 feet. He put walls inside of the walls to ensure there were no right angles. He built the pits right into the ground and used decomposed granite and brown dirt for his pits. He has a sandpit, concrete, a full array of props, and a curtain that hides his props. In just a few minutes he can clean the stage, and after pulling a few curtains to isolate sound, he can convert the Foley stage into an ADR recording studio.

His console is a Pro Tools system, which he uses for mixing and editing, and he found his Foley artist, Mike Horton, through a Craig’s List advertisement. He has a 5.1 mixing room as well. The answer to his question, of course, is yes.

## [A FOLEY HOUSE IS ALSO A HOME](#content.xhtml#bck_sec6_7)

Andy Malcolm is a Foley artist in Uxbridge, Canada. Prior to becoming a Foley artist, Malcolm was an assistant sound editor in Toronto and trained with two British sound editors, Al Streeter and Peter Burgis, who had come to Canada from London to kick-start the feature film, postproduction, sound editing business.

Malcolm had studied mime, which turned out to be a helpful prerequisite for work as a Foley artist. Mime taught him how to break down the body into its basic elements and build them up in different configurations to mimic the body actions of the characters on screen. This helped Malcolm to better understand how to give weight and character to the footsteps of the actors.

Malcolm made the move out to Uxbridge to create his own Foley and sound effects studio—on 25 acres—which he has named The Foley Farm. He has converted his actual home—the home he and his wife live in—into a working Foley stage, as well as having added two additional studios to the property. For Malcolm, his work does not include only the traditional Foley that many artists create or even the more design-oriented Foley. Malcolm, instead, sees Foley as encompassing any sound effect he can create or make for a film—including sounds more classically edited in.

Because Malcolm is a well-known Foley artist in Canada, he has designed his own workflow, including a different set of protocols for his team. He has a Foley mixer, Don White, a separate Pro Tools operator, Jenna Dalla Riva, and his Foley artist partner Goro Koyamo.

## [SEVERAL IDENTICAL STAGES](#content.xhtml#bck_sec6_8)

Julien Naudin, originally from Paris, has several Foley stages—in several countries—constructed exactly the same for him to perform his Foley. However, that is not the only unique aspect to his method. Naudin has several different spaces set up as the realistic rooms they represent. He has kitchens with working appliances and running water, living rooms, and even a car with working parts inside. He separates the rooms with curtains. His aesthetic sense, which is different from what the American audience has become accustomed to, is more naturalistic and understated. So, the rooms are set up realistically to have the acoustic “sound-print” that is more typical in a dialogue film.

## [THE REAL MAGILLA](#content.xhtml#bck_sec6_9)

Heikki Kossi, a Finnish Foley artist, lives in a small town outside Helsinki, where he has built his Foley studio. He has several small stages, each set up as a real room. They are set up more traditionally than Naudin’s, as he does not use curtains to separate them but has an actual different room for each type of room. He sets his microphone on a mattress because his Foley pits are dug directly into the ground. Because he lives in a very small town, he feels fortunate to have found someone capable of mixing Foley quite competently.

## [THE NATURAL TOUCH](#content.xhtml#bck_sec6_10)

Misa Jakovljevic prefers to Foley outside sometimes. He does not Foley his animal footsteps inside, and he will often find an isolated area in his Serbian location that allows him to perform his footsteps in the wilderness. He likes the real-world sound that happens out of doors and the natural air on the microphone. However, his studio provides all of the necessary components for interior Foley.

Italian Foley artist Marco Ciorba also prefers to do what he calls “original sound” whenever possible. He likes to go to authentic places to record Foley. Italy has a wonderful array of historic cities and various terrains, and Ciorba likes to take advantage of that aspect of being there.

It should not be forgotten that Walter Murch is infamous for taking his sound associates along to record Foley footsteps at various locales in the San Francisco Bay area. Murch prefers realistic and understated Foley that fits in with production and does not draw attention to itself, nor sound artificial.

## [NEXT FOLEY STOP](#content.xhtml#bck_sec6_11)

While it is important to note at this point that the accepted standard for Foley is still to perform it on a Foley stage and rely on the rerecording mixers to add elements that will enhance the believability of the environment, there will always be those who want to test limits. By breaking away from the comfort zone, an artist might discover a better way to approach the work. There is a plethora of possibilities of how you might want to record Foley. Just remember the basic tenets: an artist needs clean recordings that can work with the production sound and the edited effects, and you need to be able to edit the tracks. Rules were made to be learned, so know which ones can be broken and still get the job done.
