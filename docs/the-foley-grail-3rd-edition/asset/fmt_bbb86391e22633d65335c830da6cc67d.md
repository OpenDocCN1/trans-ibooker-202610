
> He’ll sometimes alert us to some things we miss … but now my goal is to find it first … so what he asks us to do is to take a pass at things, and then he will come in and critique it … he doesn’t tell us what he wants before. It works better that way because sometimes we get it right (laughs).

Sylvester comments on the car race sound effects in *Ford v Ferrari*:

> What is the best way to tell the story? Sound effects is based on character and story. Do they qualify as an integral part of advancing the plot? For example, there is a time and place for loud car sounds. We are very aware of that. I didn’t want to break the rules of “this is not storytelling, this is just sound making.”

While supervising *3:10 to Yuma* (2007), Sylvester relates that as Ben Wade (Russell Crowe) comes down the stairs, the Foley was performed so that “even the stairs were reacting to him \[Crowe\].”

Nowadays, this kind of detailed preparation and aesthetics discussion is rare. It takes time and experience that many do not have in today’s sped-up and squeezed budget realities. But the emphasis is on organization and communication. Maximum efficiency and creativity can be attained if the housekeeping aspects of sound are honored. While this kind of care cannot always be attained, recent historical context can remind us just how far we have gotten away from being mindful of what is really required from a first-rate postproduction soundtrack. Planning is crucial. Whatever the editor, artist, or mixer can do to get a plan down on paper—or on the computer screen—they are wise to do it.

## FOCUSING THE TASKS

The same principles can be followed for smaller or independent films. The key is that there must be a leader. Ostensibly, there needs to be the SSE who functions as the go-to person for everything. An experienced first assistant is a necessity as well. Even if a project has only a few individuals editing an entire film, a strong SSE and first assistant will ensure that the show is executed efficiently.

Mark Pappas relates that spotting sessions are also being modified to accommodate tighter budgets:

> Normally, we hope that the supervisor of a show is able to spot with the director, but that happens less and less nowadays. What happens is the picture editor is whom they may spot with. We get the instructions from the picture editor. Then after the first temp, you actually hear things that the director might say. If there is a second temp or \[we\] go right into pre-dubs, we hope we have it remedied by that point.

Another film Hecker supervised was *Without Limits* (1998), directed by Robert Towne. The film is about Olympic runner Steve Prefontaine, and there are several sequences where the film shows him running. Hecker assumed he would have the Foley (performed by his brother, Gary) cover all the footsteps of Prefontaine. Towne had another idea. He told Hecker he wanted the running to be done with sound design. Instead of a real-world runner with cleats and footsteps increasing with effort (the expected approach), he wanted each race to sound different.

Towne wanted the first race to sound like “fish feeding under water.” He wanted the second race to sound like “birds flapping wings on a plate of glass.” Hecker says, “I started out scared and walked out \[of the spotting session\] thinking, this is going to be cool! \[Towne\] wanted me to take a can opener to the top of my head and think outside the box.” While Hecker’s brother wanted to Foley all of the footsteps in a more traditional fashion, Hecker was firm and held to the concept Towne had expressed.

Greg Hedgepath, SSE for *Poetic Justice* (1993) and *Selma* (2014), has a general philosophy when preparing for a spotting session. He reads the script two times, making sound notes for the overall sound design, and asks himself, “what are the important elements?” He concerns himself with how sound weaves in and out of the scene. “It’s like making a painting,” he remarks. He may take some sounds out, then move them back in. “Is it going to be moving? Is it going to be static?” He considers background sounds to be very important and considers these sounds while making notes as well. For Hedgepath, Foley is best when “it doesn’t call attention to itself,” but is quick to add, “Foley adds life to the scene … it brings a scene back alive again.”

F. Hudson Miller, who says he “never wanted to be a sound guy, but I did want to be a film guy,” studied cinematography in film school and discovered he had an aptitude for sound. After working with veteran sound editors Sandy Berman and David Yewdall, he began editing Foley. Now a SSE, Miller states, “I don’t approach Foley and \[sound\] effects differently. The wonderful thing about Foley is it is performed to picture.” His approach to both cueing and editing Foley is the performance, although he admits he prefers less Foley in a film than some others might enjoy. “The most important thing is how much emotional content is in it … we tell stories for the heart and soul.” While working at Paramount, he fondly recalls his experiences with director Tony Scott, who he describes as “the most precise director I have ever worked with about what he wanted \[from sound\].”
