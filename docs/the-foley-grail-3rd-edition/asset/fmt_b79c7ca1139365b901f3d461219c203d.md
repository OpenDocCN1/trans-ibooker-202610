## CONVIVIALITY: CANADA

Andy Malcolm is a Foley artist in Uxbridge, Canada. Prior to becoming a Foley artist, Malcolm was an assistant sound editor in Toronto and trained with two British sound professionals, Al Streeter and Peter Burgis, who had come to Canada from London to kick-start the feature film, postproduction, sound editing business.

Malcolm had studied mime, which turned out to be a helpful prerequisite for work as a Foley artist. Mime taught him how to break down the body into its basic elements and build them up in different configurations to mimic the body actions of the characters on screen. This helped Malcolm to better understand how to give weight and character to the footsteps of the actors.

Because Malcolm has a reputation as the premier independent Foley artist in Canada, he has designed his own workflow, including a different set of protocols for his team. He has mixers, Don White, Kevin Schultz, and Jack Heren, a separate Pro Tools operator, Foley editor Jenna Dalla Riva, and his Foley artist partners Goro Koyamo and Sandra Fox.

Malcolm returned the favor of having been trained to be the top Foley artist in Canada by training one of Ireland’s Foley artists, Caoimhe Doyle. Doyle had watched Malcolm work in Ireland. She visited Canada to apprentice and train with Malcolm at his facility. Doyle then went back to Ireland to design and build a dedicated Foley stage at Ardmore Sound, where she now works with Foley mixer Jean McGrath.

Chris Szott, a Foley artist in Edmonton, Canada, began his training with Malcolm and Koyamo as an apprentice for six months. He “picked up Andy’s team’s acute attention to detail,” and after completing his work with Malcolm, he proceeded to establish his own solo career. As the sole proprietor of Little Hook Sound, Szott is presently the only artist in Alberta. He and his Foley mixer, Isael Huard, work as a team on features, local productions, documentaries, and some series for Netflix. Szott studied recording engineering and music composition in London, and he asserts that Foley artists are “the jazz musicians of sound effects,” adding “every sound needs an intention.”

For Szott, performing props is a more accessible skill than perfecting the footsteps for a character. He considers the textures and frequencies of the prop cues, and he compares Foley to “learning a new instrument … it’s a life-long pursuit.”

Huard, who studied music technologies at the University of Alberta, considers Foley a highly collaborative task between production and post-production, as well as between artist and mixer. He likes to know what mic was used in production and will record Foley with the same mic if possible. He records using a room mic, in addition to a mic aimed at the footsteps or props. Szott and Huard record the Foley with an ear toward including a premixed track for the final dub (mixing in the box). Because Alberta is a smaller market for films, the team typically works 5–7 days on a feature or 4 days on a Netflix episode. Huard explains, “I use the tools to achieve what I need.” Because the team works on its own, “we have gotten used to what we like and understanding how what we hear gels.”

Both Huard and Szott like “pure Foley,” which translates for them as “the correct sound without relying on plugins.” After almost three years of working together, the musicality and emotion of the Foley is a joint goal. Huard’s training in manipulating sounds with electroacoustic music “informs a lot about how I hear sounds.” Szott adds, “sometimes when you get into the groove, you need to go back to the story.”

## BOLLYWOOD

As one of the most prolific film-producing nations, India has a long history of lavish, music-filled films. Karan Singh, a Foley artist in Mumbai, began his career at the respected BR Studios, where his father had been a security guard. In 1984, at just 12 years old, he learned the world of film sound and would “sneak into the studio” to watch the Foley artists, including Prahlad Salvi, work on their films. Foley artists recorded to one analog track at a time for Foley, which meant all sounds had to be performed at one time and in excellent sync. Singh estimates that there are about 15 Foley artists in India, and most of the Foley work is performed by about three of these Foley artists. While at BR Studios, ADR was recorded during the day and Foley was performed at night. Most artists worked 18-hour shifts, which is an unusual practice compared to other international film communities.

In 2009, after several years as a freelance Foley artist, Singh opened his own studio. Foley tends to be an extensive and elaborate aspect of Indian sound design, and he often works on four or five films a month. In addition to Bollywood films, Singh performs Foley for films produced in Bangladesh. Using the production track as a guide (he refers to it as the location track), he figures out the Foley cues during the session, on the stage: “every shot is in my brain,” he explains. Foley artists might work on four or five films in a month. “We have to come very close to the nature of sound,” says Singh.

Singh measures the amount of time he works on a film in hours, rather than days or weeks, and estimates that he might work 100 hours on a romantic comedy (rom-com) and close to 300 hours on an action film. He focuses on all of the details he can see in the scene: “when I see a film, any film, I can make out that this film, Foley is important, and these sounds are going to be done. That time, then and there, I decide, and I really give attention to the detail.” Singh does not differentiate between the amount of detail for feature films or television. He approaches both types of projects in the same way. “We are making realistic films now, and we have to be as authentic as possible.”

Singh explains that the workflow differs from that of some other countries. The sound designer will also mix the sound for the film, and the associate to the sound designer might mix the Foley. Singh’s Foley mixer, who he refers to as a Foley engineer, is most often Ram Nath. The team typically uses only one microphone for a Foley session, unless the sound designer asks for a second microphone to assist with perspective. Singh’s focus is on balanced tracks and admits, “cloth is the most difficult thing to do.” He performs one cloth track to cover any principal actors and then wears clothes that contribute a bit of cloth rustle for background footsteps. Singh’s commitment to a realistic sound is modified for “superhero” films, which he performs using more loud and stylized methods. “There are some sound designers who want it to be loud,” and he alters his more realistic aesthetic sense to accommodate them. Singh will often pick up props anywhere he sees an interesting piece. An example of this is a stool he discovered at one of his favorite eating spots. The stool had a great sound, and he bought it from the owner of the establishment.
