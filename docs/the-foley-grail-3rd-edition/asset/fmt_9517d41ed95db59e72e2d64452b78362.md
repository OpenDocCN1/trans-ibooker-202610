
Some men have a problem with high heels. Most men spend their lives in flat shoes or boots. Rarely are they in situations to wear the varying types of shoes that female characters might wear. Thus, they are less used to the different heel heights and the adjustments women make to accommodate different types of shoes. Add to that the problem of foot size, and one can see why some men find it hard to locate shoes they can use for “well-heeled” female characters. There are solutions. One is to shop at a store that caters to female impersonator performers or non-binary people. This is easy to do in a city like Los Angeles, but if one is doing film work in another region or in a smaller market that produces independent films, commercials, or games, then these stores are harder to come by. However, many male Foley artists have shoes that sound like heels because of the lighter and brighter sound. They have learned how to manipulate their foot to sound like the shoe is a high heel. Many male Foley artists have conquered this issue easily. Again, gender is truly not an issue. Joanna Fang, a transgender Foley artist at Alchemy Sound in Peekskill, New York, has adapted her shoe selections by tuning her ear to the sound of the shoe and the walking style of the character.

Greg Barbanell, a veteran Foley artist, has been a big guy in the past. While at his heaviest, he could still walk any character with the delicacy and precision of the most agile dancer. His height and weight were irrelevant. This is the case for any professional in the field. Foley artists are all experienced at the sonic portrayal of all kinds of characters, and our physical build is not what is at issue. Far more important for any person who wants to walk Foley well are sync, sensitivity, precision, character intention, and artistic taste. A Foley artist can walk any gender and weight and is not limited by height, build, or gender. Nor does the artist have to be in perfect shape physically. Foley artists can be young, old, fit, flabby, and even physically challenged. Foley artists learn from each other and adapt to their own circumstances.

## CRITTERS
Animal footsteps are a strange hybrid. They are considered footstep cues, yet they are performed like prop cues. Foley artists have lots of tricks for doing animal feet. One is using soft slippers or ballet shoes on their feet. Depending on the surface used, the ear can be fooled. Some Foley artists use gardener’s gloves with tacks on the ends for dogs. Some women have acrylic nails and use their bare hands. However, when doing animal feet in a dirt pit, it might be best to use gloves—one never knows what is in that pit. The important thing is to be impressionistic, rather than focus on the literal movement of the animal. Once again, the picture edits might help or hinder, so it is best to go with the flow and be rhythmic but not overly concerned with precision. This being said, with experience and developed skill, a Foley artist can learn to be more precise, with the caveat that the viewer needs only to be convinced of the authenticity of the animal movement and will not be analyzing every step.

When Foley artists perform the footsteps for cows, goats, or animals with hooves, they can use old film cores from the days of magnetic film or some such device. Most people have heard about using coconuts for horse hooves, and many still do that. Some Foley artists use them with the meat still in them, baked, and then hot-glued to the shell. Some artists have special devices made from plungers or other props. The trick to animal footsteps, however, is the rhythm and the feel of the movements. Animals have many different types of walks or runs, and it is important to bridge the cut without jolting the rhythm. The point here is that Foley artists have various techniques to approach the task of creating animal footsteps and movements. Much like the meth ods animators use for observing animals in real life to adapt their renditions in film, Foley artists can do the same kind of observation and learn the various ways animals move.

Both Richard James Hinton and Meltem Baytok have worked on natural history documentaries for the BBC. As a result, they both have an abundance of experience with animal footsteps. While they do not work together—typically the task is for one Foley artist—they work on similar projects. Baytok is originally from Turkey and learned Foley in the United Kingdom before she could speak English fluently. She relied on reading actors’ faces, emotions, and reactions to understand how to do Foley. “It’s like being deaf and reading their bodies like sign language,” says Baytok. From such an interesting perspective, Baytok enjoys performing the Foley for animals. Some of the creatures she performs footsteps and movements for are foxes, giraffes, wolves, lions, elephants, fish, kangaroos, bears, monkeys, and birds. For one documentary about animals that reproduce, she remembers that she used pineapples for crocodiles having sex in water. Baytok has been known to use cupped tennis balls as well as the old reliable coconuts for various animal footsteps, when she does not use her hands.

Contrary to Baytok, Hinton edits his Foley as well as performs it. His Foley mixers previously worked on a PC-based digital system called Pyramix—from Switzerland—but have made the transition to Pro Tools. “We had to be compatible with the rest of the world and it helps massively when working with other post houses.”

Hinton pays close attention to the microphone technique when he records. In a smaller room, he positions the microphone more closely, but when he works on a larger Foley stage, he likes more distance on the microphone. Hinton stresses that the natural history documentaries normally have bigger budgets, so he can take the time to perform the animals carefully.

When Misa Jakovljevic Foleys animals, he prefers to find appropriate surfaces outside, if at all possible. He does not use the Foley stage for animal footsteps. He likes to find locations that match what he sees on the screen. Sometimes he will perform the footsteps wild and edit them in.

## MIT OUT PEOPLE
The performance of footsteps for characters is a key purpose of a Foley artist. There are computer programs and machines invented to synthesize footsteps and edit them in so as to reduce the need for the live performance of a person supporting the actor’s vision sonically. There will always be someone trying to invent a machine that can replace human labor, and the world of postproduction sound is no different. Although some may hope to cut costs by substituting programmed footsteps for the custom-designed performance of a trained, artistic professional, the result will always pale by comparison. A smart Foley artist is one who knows as many techniques as possible and who is willing to change with new methods and technologies. Change is inevitable. Be a professional that can change gracefully.
