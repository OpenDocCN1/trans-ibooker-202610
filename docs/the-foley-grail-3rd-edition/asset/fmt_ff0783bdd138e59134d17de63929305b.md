
In the San Francisco Bay Area, there have been two primary postproduction sound facilities that have been recognized for their award-winning soundtracks. Skywalker Sound, nestled in Nicasio, Marin County, is a bucolic sanctuary owned by George Lucas, and the Saul Zaentz Film Center, which was located in Berkeley until 2018, was a respected film sound facility and home of Fantasy Records. Both Skywalker and Zaentz have garnered Academy Awards for sound, and both have distinctive approaches to Foley. Skywalker Sound employed Dennie Thorpe [*The Right Stuff* (1983) and *Saving Private Ryan* (1998)] as the lead Foley artist for over 25 years and has progressed to employ several other artists, including Jana Vance and Ronni Brown. Thorpe, who was primarily self-taught, had developed her own style of Foley workflow, utilized headphones as she worked—unusual for Foley artists—and trained Vance herself. Thorpe and Vance had not worked anywhere else, and as Thorpe retired, she passed the lead artist mantle to Vance. Brown had worked with Margie O’Malley, and after an established Bay Area career, she began working at Skywalker Sound in 2008. Presently, Skywalker boasts two Foley teams, with Vance and Brown performing most often as a team together. More recently, John Roesch and Shelley Roden have joined Skywalker Sound as part of the Disney expansion.

The Foley artists who worked at Saul Zaentz Film Center had also worked in Hollywood at times, at Skywalker with Thorpe upon occasion, and within the San Francisco film scene at other facilities as well. While Skywalker is home to a stable of sound professionals, those at Zaentz were often just as likely to work in Hollywood in addition to the Berkeley facility.

Both Murch and Splet were less likely to rely on Foley than other sound designers. They preferred Foley that was understated and would even go out and record themselves doing Foley in natural settings rather than record on a stage. Rerecording mixer Mark Berger [*Amadeus* (1984) and *Mortal Thoughts* (1991)] related a story about Foley on *The Godfather: [Part II](#part2.xhtml)* (1974) that illustrates Murch’s iconoclastic approach to Foley. To capture the sound of the footsteps of someone walking up marble stairs, Murch and Berger had calibrated a metronome at frames per footstep using the flash feature, rather than the tick sound. Murch, Berger, and Pat Jackson then went to a building in San Francisco and put a microphone on the second floor. They started walking up the stairs in sync with the flash. When they got back to the editing room, they found they had walked the scene pretty close to sync. For Murch, this approach to Foleying footsteps, with the unpredictability of a natural setting, was preferable to the sanitizing that might occur on a Foley stage.

#### LES BRUITEURS: FRANCE

Messieurs Pinvin and Plasman were “Bruiteurs,” or “noise makers,” who worked together to create sounds for silent films in France. Pinvin performed the voices for the films, and Plasman created the sounds for the props. Plasman had been burned—his face terribly disfigured—in World War I, so he preferred to work in the shadows. The men worked with one track for all of their sounds. They were experienced circus workers and knew how to use a microphone and work with 16-mm film. The new American films were coming to France, so they were kept busy putting sounds to the films so that French audiences could enjoy these new entertainments.

Everything had to be performed at once. They would perform the voices and sounds, then stop. Then perform, then stop. If a speaker made a mistake, the Bruiteurs would begin again from the top. This process continued until the technology developed to allow editing and multiple tracks for recording.

Julien Naudin is one of France’s veteran Foley artists. His father was one as was his grandfather, who learned from M. Plasman. The Bruiteur profession is one of family tradition. Naudin has trained his brother, his wife, and his daughter to work with him in Foley.

In the Bruiteur tradition, the work of Foley is perceived as more of an art form. Rather than being considered part of an industrial Ford-ist tradition—as in Hollywood—Foley is considered an important part of the narrative, and the artist works closely with the director. Naudin’s father worked directly with Jacques Tati to create the whimsy in the sonic world of Monsieur Hulot. Additionally, the French tradition of Foley relies on stages that have different “rooms” for each area of a home. Rather than recording most Foley in one studio, Naudin’s studio contains areas separated by curtains that have kitchens with running water and a full complement of appliances, car interiors with working steering wheels and dashboards, and furniture appointments and surfaces that match any predictable interior. The consistency of Naudin’s Foley relies on several European studios all with similar configurations of working areas and props.

#### ACROSS THE POND: THE UNITED KINGDOM

The United Kingdom has several Foley artists who work regularly in film, television, and documentaries. One of these artists is veteran Peter Burgis. His background is unusual for a filmmaker, and especially for a Foley artist. Burgis began working in Devon, England, as a professional assisting people with learning difficulties. As a former member of the Royal Air Force, Burgis was attracted to working with troubled youth who were “absolute versions of myself,” and while he was part of a “volunteer scheme,” he began to make public service videos for an editing company in Cornwall. This set of endeavors led to presenting programs on the BBC, where Burgis “fell in love with what went on behind the camera.” While working at a small studio as a runner, he noticed the Foley stage “on the left” and “[it] was like opening Aladdin’s Cave for me.”

Burgis does not consider himself technically minded and prefers the physical creativity of performing Foley. However, he did find himself mixing Foley at a small studio with Foley artists Pauline Benion and Jack Stew. What Burgis admired was “the choreography between the two of them.” Burgis occasionally stepped in to help the two artists with background footsteps. It was Benion who advised Burgis that, “you need to stop recording and you need to start being a Foley artist.” This was in 1993, and while Great Britain was in an economic recession, Benion introduced Burgis to people who would hire him at a time when “old school attitude was the forefront.”

Burgis and his good friend Andie Derrick, an actress, decided to work as a team, and Derrick “practiced for three days in my flat” to prepare for her first day as an artist with Burgis. As an openly gay man, Burgis found some prejudice in the more traditional world of postproduction sound when he began, and he found himself being confrontational in response. However, what Burgis learned as he became more experienced and secure in his craft was “the best way to educate a man was with kindness,” which allowed his career as well as his professionalism to advance him to the status of veteran as postproduction sound in London progressed technologically and aesthetically as well.
