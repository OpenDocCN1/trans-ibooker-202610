
This brings up an interesting issue—that of the digitization of Foley. Will there be a time when Foley is simply edited in? Will Foley artists become unnecessary? Because Foley is a performance and is particular to each film, each character, and each story world, the personal quality of each cue would dictate that Foley artists would still be indispensable. Yet it makes sense that some filmmakers will forgo the Foley artist and try to cut in the effects and footsteps to save money, even if quality is sacrificed. One practical skill that an experienced Foley artist learns is how to fool the eye when a picture edit—for many possible and excellent reasons—leaves a character stepping twice on the same foot. The skill required for a Foley artist to perform a character’s walk so the gait is matched yet appears smooth from one side of the edit to the other side requires decision-making that is specific to the scene and edit. This decision is a human action that exudes artfulness and craftsmanship. Algorithms cannot replace the human component so essential to the craft.

Foley artists use specific techniques to improve sync when performing a cue. When walking a character, watch the cue once. Notice the overall nature of the walk. Is it even and deliberate? Plodding? Mercurial? Every actor has certain noticeable traits when walking, regardless of the film. Now watch the cue again. How does the actor’s body move? Do the shoulders move a lot? Does the head bob up and down? What about the arms swinging? Actors give many cues that help the Foley artist move with the actor.

Often it helps to watch each specific footstep, but sometimes doing this can slow the Foley artist down and the sync can be slightly behind footstep impact. There is usually a rhythm to each walk, except when the transitions occur. The shift of weight, the change in direction, and a sudden distraction can all cause the rhythm to shift.

It is fairly typical for a neophyte Foley artist to walk with a flat footfall. Plop, plop, plop is what it will sound like. Then there is the tendency to over articulate. Too much heel-toe will sound like ta-da, ta-da, ta-da. Neither sounds natural. A character will have some heel-toe footfalls, some plops, some scuffs, and some minor weight shifts. If an artist can master these subtleties, it will help with sync.

Sync is often easier when performing prop cues. Many prop sounds have a specific impact. A book being set down, for instance, is easier to do in sync. There is a precise moment when the book connects with the surface. That is the moment to focus on. An oddity that occurs in Foley is the pickup of a prop. Often the Foley artist will cheat the sound of picking up a glass, book, or some such item. Although in real life we do not always hear the item being picked up, in Foley the artist will enhance that sound to add drama to the prop. The trick is to perform the pickup without too much scrape or swipe, lest it sound artificial and draw too much attention.

## THE AUDIENCE IS WATCHING THE FILM IN REAL TIME

Then there is the place when the prop has an abundance of activity—gun movement or saddle and bridle for a horse, among other possibilities. There is normally a place in the event where the eye and ear meet in harmony and it looks and feels correct. This is the part of the prop to focus on. I call this the “sweet spot,” the term used in sports to describe that perfect place where the ball meets the racquet or bat and just sails away effortlessly.

If, however, the prop is amorphous, like papers moving or leaves fluttering, then a Foley artist will let a natural sense of composition guide the performance. The audience is watching the film in real time. Whatever the audience will catch, the artist should catch. With more practice with these types of cues, Foley artists will automatically see more specific events in every cue.

## PERCEPTION IS REALITY

While sync is critical, the performance of the character is crucial. The feel of the character will actually help with sync. If the Foley performance is truly connected to the actor’s actions, the sync will be good most of the time. However, it really takes a lot of experience performing Foley before sync becomes second nature to the artist. Once sync is more comfortable, the emphasis shifts to performance, feel, and style.

To achieve accurate sync in animation or games is more of a challenge. Whereas a live-action film has a predictable rhythm to it, animation and games do not. The timing is artificial and created for effect. It is hard to anticipate the clothes jumping out of a dresser in *Beauty and the Beast* (1991) or the various permutations of the characters’ actions in games. It takes time and experience to master the peculiar timing issues that arise in computer-generated and interactive formats. Additionally, when one is performing a similar action in a game with only a slight change, it is essential to make the difference the important part of the performance. That is what the player’s choice will evoke. Every choice the player can make requires a slightly different performance to emphasize the game element.

Often, when someone is new to performing Foley, it is hard for that artist to tell when the sync is close enough. Approximate sync can seem just fine. Usually, if a cue is one frame late, only the very trained eye can perceive it. Many can perceive it if a cue is two frames late, and if a Hang It as a Unit cue is three frames late it is obvious to most viewers. Early sync is not so forgiving. Foley that is one frame early “feels” funny. Foley that is two frames early is noticeable to most. Thus, when one is performing a cue, one frame late is not a tragedy. There is some forgiveness in the sync, unless one is “topping production”—that is, layering the Foley on top of the sound already on the production track.

Most experienced Foley artists have had to work on many projects to get good sync. It is a goal worth striving for. It takes time, patience, and a critical eye. In the meantime, the emphasis should be on the feel, character, and rhythm of the performance. Even the best in the craft bless the editors and mixers who aid in their efforts.

## ANIMATION STATION

Foley has become an important part of animation. Originally, animated films had very little sound edited in or used music cues to indicate the sound. In early Warner Brothers’ cartoons, orchestrated music cues were used to telegraph a character’s entrance or mischief. Witness Bugs Bunny as he creates havoc with Elmer Fudd. Pizzicato violins may indicate the rabbit sneakily walking up to Fudd.

Today, animated films and television shows are filled with sound. Edited effects and Foley often dominate the soundtrack. In animated musicals, there are few moments when the audience is not hearing music, effects, or Foley. The soundtracks are lush and creative. Yet there are still some films that stand out as excellent examples of how Foley can be genre-specific.

In *The Incredibles* (2004), we watch as several superheroes attempt to live ordinary lives. The use of sound effects and Foley supports this premise. In one scene, our hero is in his office cubicle explaining the policies of his insurance company while pretending to deny a woman’s claim. We hear the light background sounds of the office: papers, pencils, chairs, and hands on tables. The props and footsteps are very realistic to match the animation. Sound and picture support a live-action view of the world. No magical or playful sounds are used. The world, sonically, is ordinary.
