Foley artists begin their contribution to the sound design on the Foley stage. They collect their shoes and props for the session, go over any cue sheets, sip their coffees, perhaps devour a bagel, and begin to plan the first few cues. Normally, if the project is a live-action or animated film, there will be some time to view the beginning of the show to get a sense of the amount of work required to be completed before the session is finished.

If, however, the session is for a television show, a game, or an independent film that is directed by someone new, it is less predictable what the day—or evening—might entail. The reason for this is simple: features and long-form episodic shows typically have a planning stage, called a spotting session, and hopefully have some kind of codified cueing system. However, this is not as typical for a television show, a weekly cartoon show, a game, or an independent film. As technology allows for the workflow to speed up, nuances and niceties like spotting sessions, cue sheets, and clear communication between editors and Foley professionals can become expedited or even eliminated.

For this reason, there is a conceptual ideal situation for spotting and cuing Foley that can be utilized and can demonstrate why a spotting session and also specific cueing are so important for quality Foley. If the ideal does not occur, that is fine. Making do is par for the course in the entertainment arts. However, the more often standards are maintained, the more adjustments and accommodations can be made when necessary. The balance is to know what to modify and what to retain.

## THE SPOTTING SESSION

The spotting session is where brainstorming meets ordinary details. Ideally, when a supervising sound editor (SSE) is hired to head a sound crew, they meet with the director and the picture editor. Sometimes, if time permits and the budget allows, the first picture assistant and ADR supervisor might attend the session as well. There are rare occasions when the Foley supervisor is at the first spotting session. However, the days of these kinds of spotting sessions are becoming rarer and rarer. We are now in a time when budget hawks do not want to pay for anything they consider extra or even trivial. Even though the spotting session can save thousands of dollars down the road for a typical major feature film, the idea of several sound professionals sitting around discussing sound ideas seems like an unnecessary expense to most accountants. It is not surprising that they would not understand the necessity of the preplanning that can save time and money at the back end of the final mix. Rather than being a frivolous time of playfully toying with sound design ideas, the opposite is more often the case. Most spotting sessions are much more serious and grueling than the budget hawks can even imagine.

The director and editor will usually run the picture and discuss preferences with the SSE. The SSE takes notes and asks about ADR that will be needed, special concerns, and timelines. There will be questions about temp dubs, reshoots, and picture changes. Often, the director will state the obvious to the sound editor about the pedestrian effects. But the key is that the SSE gets a clear sense of what the expectations are regarding sound.

David Stone relates a story about working with Tim Burton on *Batman Returns* (1992):

> So, we were in Chris Lebenzon’s cutting room with Tim, and Rich Anderson and I were at the KEM table, and Chris and Tim were driving, going back and forth and Tim would say, “Oh, there’s a door. We have to get that door. Here’s a this and here’s a that.” And then he just stopped and turned to us and said, “Oh, what am I doing? You guys know what to do. Why don’t I just go to the places where I have concerns?” And Rich and I just turned to each other as though to say, “At last, a director with some common sense and some trust! He knows that we know how to cover the mundane things. That’s why he’s paying us.”

Stone continues,

> Every movie has certain unusual concerns, what we used to call “the sound nobody’s ever heard before” because so many directors would make that point as though they were the first to ever say that. After—you’ve done a few movies, you begin to see these patterns. The supervisor can save the director a lot of time by acknowledging the special concerns and spend the time on the issues that really need to be discussed. We don’t get much time with the director, as a rule, and an empowering director, like Tim, will use our time together wisely.

During spotting sessions, Stone would write Foley notes for his Foley supervisor. He would then present the supervisor—on *Batman Returns* it was me—with a notebook containing a complete written breakdown of each reel, what the sound concerns were, what sound effects would cover, and what Foley would be responsible for performing. He scheduled a session with me to run the film and talk through concept, style, and schedule. I left the session knowing what was expected of Foley, who the effects editors were (in case I had to call about a specific cue), and how much I had to accomplish each day on the stage. This is the more financially high-end approach to spotting.

Stone held similar sessions for ADR. At these meetings, the ADR supervisor was told which lines would be replaced in ADR and which lines the dialogue editors would probably be able to “save” in the production tracks. All editors had a notebook. All editors knew who was on the crew, where they were, and what they were cutting in. It is a seamless approach that allows maximum empowerment for the editors because the communication is so complete.

Each SSE has their own approach for spotting with the crew. Stone says,

> I \[would\] try to approach a piece of work in many passes. So, for instance, I might try to sit with the person in charge of the Foley cueing and first just discuss the “big deal” questions. I like to layer the work.

He continues, “For instance, ‘We’re going to do a jungle picture like *Predator*. Let’s talk about the foliage. Let’s make that a special treatment and get fresh foliage every day.’ Then I would go to another specific issue.”

Supervisor Don Sylvester, recipient of the Academy Award for Best Sound Effects Editing for his work on *Ford v Ferrari* (2019), discusses some of his various experiences with director James Mangold regarding sound:

> Foley is entirely character driven. I always approach things wondering “what would Jim do?” How does it \[sound\] tell the story? Foley is one of Jim’s pet peeves because Foley is entirely character driven. It is character and he knows what the character would do, and if the Foley doesn’t know then he gets angry. In the previous movie Logan, there is a very strong girl, and he tries to take her backpack away from her, and they pull back and forth a few times and finally he rips it out of her hand. That was really difficult to do for us because as Jim explains it, there are two titans fighting and the backpack didn’t have a chance … that was our instruction … we were establishing character and that was a plot point … the backpack ripping and tearing and that isn’t happening on screen … the only way you could sell the way they fight is if you rip the backpack while they’re fighting.
