**3.131** During the examination, the practitioner performs procedures to evaluate whether controls over suppliers and business partners were suitably designed and operated effectively.

## Entity Controls for Addressing Supplier Risks

**3.132** As previously discussed, entity management is responsible for monitoring the effectiveness of controls at a supplier whose controls are necessary, in combination with the entity's controls, for the entity to achieve its principal system objectives.

**3.133** An entity's third-party risk and control assessment activities often include a combination of the following:

-   a. Quality control testing of inputs received
-   
-   b. Testing of controls at the supplier by members of the entity's internal audit function
-   
-   c. Reviewing and reconciling output reports
-   
-   d. Holding periodic discussions with supplier personnel and evaluating supplier performance against established service level objectives and agreements
-   
-   e. Making site visits to the supplier
-   
-   f. Inspecting attestation reports on the supplier's system, such as a SOC for Supply Chain report
-   
-   g. Monitoring external communications, such as complaints from customers; entity communications about system events; and communications from regulators that are relevant to the products or services provided by the supplier

## Complementary Supplier Controls

**3.134** When the entity uses the carve-out method to address controls at a supplier, the practitioner evaluates whether the controls stated in the description and expected to be implemented at the supplier are necessary, in combination with controls at the entity, to provide reasonable assurance that the

entity's principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria (that is, whether the controls are CSCs). For example, if the entity is responsible for developing, testing, and approving program changes, but has outsourced the actual implementation of the changes to a carved-out supplier, controls at the supplier would be necessary to achieve the entity's principal system objectives based on trust services criterion CC8.1, The entity authorizes, designs, develops or acquires, configures, documents, tests, approves, and implements changes to infrastructure, data, software, and procedures to meet its objectives.

3.135 As part of the evaluation of the entity's third-party risk assessment and control activities, the practitioner may perform procedures to obtain evidence about whether the disclosed CSCs are appropriate, including procedures to determine whether (a) the entity has communicated to the supplier the entity's requirements with regard to the CSCs and (b) there is any evidence of deficiencies in the effectiveness of the supplier's controls.

3.136 Although an entity may contract with a supplier to perform functions that are part of the entity's system, entity management still retains obligations to customers with regard to those functions. As a result, the entity's controls include activities to manage the risks associated with suppliers. In evaluating the effectiveness of controls, the practitioner considers the nature and extent of the entity's third-party risk and control assessment activities when determining the nature, timing, and extent of testing to perform. For example, if the entity has obtained a SOC for Supply Chain report from a supplier, the practitioner would review the report to determine whether entity management has adequately evaluated those risks by assessing (a) the relevance of the system description and CSCs to the entity's system and (b) any deviations requiring further evaluation and response by entity management. If entity management is unable to obtain a SOC for Supply Chain report from the supplier, the practitioner should consider how entity management has assessed and evaluated the supplier's controls. However, unless the practitioner is reperforming entity management's tests of the supplier's controls, the practitioner's performance of tests directly on the supplier's controls is not a test of the effectiveness of the entity's controls.

## Considering Controls That Did Not Need to Operate During the Period Covered by the Examination

3.137 The description of the entity's system may include controls that would ordinarily operate during the period covered by the examination. However, in certain circumstances, some controls may not need to operate during the period because the circumstances that warrant the operation of the controls do not occur. For example, controls related to providing a new user with identification and authentication credentials may not have operated if no new users were added during the period. When entity management informs the practitioner that the circumstances requiring the operation of certain controls did not occur, the practitioner performs procedures to corroborate entity management's statement and describes, in the description of the results of testing, the nature of the procedures performed and the results of such procedures. In such situations, there is no need for the practitioner to modify his or her opinion on the effectiveness of the controls.

## Identifying and Evaluating Deviations in the Effectiveness of Controls

**3.138** The practitioner should accumulate and evaluate identified deviations to determine whether the deviations are indicative of a control deficiency. Once that determination is made, the practitioner considers the significance of the deficiency on the entity's ability to provide reasonable assurance that its principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria. In addition to considering the effect of the deficiency on the opinion on control effectiveness, the practitioner also considers the potential impact of other matters, such as description misstatements.

**3.139** When a control deficiency is identified, it is usually helpful to the entity for the practitioner to discuss it with entity management in a timely manner. If the control deficiency was identified during interim testing, entity management may be able to remediate the deficiency for the remainder of the period. In that case, when assessing whether the deficiency is material, the practitioner would be able to consider the effect of the deficiency during the first part of the period on the ability of the system to provide reasonable assurance of achieving its principal system objectives.

**3.140** In other situations, entity management may be unable to correct the deficiency (for example, in instances in which the operation of the control has already occurred). In such a case, timely notification permits entity management to remediate the deficiency on a go-forward basis.

**3.141** Paragraph .33 of AT-C section 205 states that the practitioner should respond appropriately to fraud or suspected fraud and noncompliance or suspected noncompliance with laws or regulations affecting the subject matter that are identified during the engagement. If the practitioner becomes aware that any identified deviations have resulted from fraud, the practitioner should assess the risk that the description does not represent the system that was designed and implemented in accordance with the description criteria and that controls were not effective. Paragraphs 3.164–.165 discuss actions that may be appropriate for the practitioner in these circumstances.

**3.142** When performing procedures, the practitioner may become aware of a system incident that has affected a system other than the system being examined. For example, the entity may experience a breach in an IT system that is not a component of the system under examination. In such situations, the practitioner needs to understand the nature and cause of the breach because it may have occurred as a result of ineffective controls shared between the affected system and the system being examined. If that is the case, the practitioner may reassess the risks of material misstatement. In addition, if the system incident is related to a security breach, the practitioner may consider whether the inherent risks of the environment connected to the system are significantly different than what was originally assessed, or whether controls within the system may have been compromised due to an advanced persistent threat that was not previously detected. As a result of the reassessment of risk, the practitioner may determine that additional procedures need to be performed or that entity management needs to identify additional controls to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria.
