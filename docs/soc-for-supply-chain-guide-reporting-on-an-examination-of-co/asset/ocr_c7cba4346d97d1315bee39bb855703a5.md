An entity may be able to correct a deficiency in the operation of a control, for example, by providing additional training to the employee who performs the control. However, if the design of the control is deficient, the control will not operate effectively regardless of who performs it. For that reason, the practitioner often would not test the operating effectiveness of a control that is not suitably designed. Instead, the practitioner generally would consider the design of other controls, if any, that address the same risks.

3.87 In some situations, two or more controls are suitably designed only when operating in conjunction with each other. In these situations, the practitioner evaluates the suitability of the controls together in order to reach a conclusion.

3.88 Paragraph 4.75 presents an example of a separate paragraph that might be added to the practitioner's report when the practitioner determines that controls were not suitably designed to provide reasonable assurance that one or more of the entity's principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria.

# Obtaining Evidence About the Operating Effectiveness of Controls

3.89 Controls that operate effectively provide reasonable assurance of achieving the entity's principal system objectives based on the applicable trust services criteria. The practitioner tests the operating effectiveness of the controls stated in the description by performing procedures (known as tests of controls) to obtain evidence about how the controls were applied, the consistency with which they were applied, and by whom or in what manner they were applied. When an entity uses the inclusive method to present the services and controls of a supplier, the practitioner also applies tests of controls to the controls at the supplier.

3.90 If the practitioner has identified controls that were not suitably designed, the practitioner generally would not test the operating effectiveness of those controls. However, in certain circumstances, intended users may expect controls identified in the description to be tested by the practitioner. In such situations, the practitioner may choose to perform such testing and include the results of the testing in the report, if the practitioner determines that including such information would not be misleading.

# Designing and Performing Tests of Controls

3.91 According to paragraph .24 of AT-C section 205, the practitioner should design and perform tests of controls to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about the effectiveness of controls stated in the description of the entity's system. By including those controls in the description, entity management has identified them as part of the system the entity has designed, implemented, and operated to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria.
