3.93 As the inherent risk associated with the control being tested increases, the sufficiency and appropriateness of evidence that the practitioner needs to support an opinion on control effectiveness also increases. Factors the practitioner may consider when determining nature, timing, and extent of tests of controls include the following:

- The nature and materiality of misstatements that the control is intended to prevent, or detect and correct
- Whether the control has a history of deficiencies
- The effectiveness of monitoring activities over that and other controls
- The nature of the control and the frequency with which it operates
- The degree to which other controls are dependent on the effectiveness of the control
- The competence of the personnel who perform the control or monitor its performance and whether there have been changes in key personnel who perform the control or monitor its performance
- Whether the control relies on performance by an individual or is automated
- The complexity of the control and the significance of the judgments that would be made in connection with its operation

3.94 Generally, a conclusion that a control is not operating effectively can be supported by less evidence than is necessary to support a conclusion that a control is operating effectively.

## Nature of Tests of Controls

3.95 The nature and objectives of tests to evaluate the operating effectiveness of controls are different from those performed to evaluate the suitability of the design of controls. When designing and performing tests of controls, the practitioner does one or more of the following:

- a. Makes inquiries and performs other procedures such as observation (for example, of the application of the control), inspection (for example, of documents, reports, or electronic files), or reperformance to obtain evidence about the following:
  - i. How the control was executed (Was the control performed as designed?)
  - ii. The consistency with which the control was applied throughout the period
  - iii. By whom or by what means the control was applied (Is the control automated or manual? Has there been high turnover of the personnel in the position that performs the control, and is the control being performed by an inexperienced person?)
- b. Determines whether the control to be tested depends on other controls and, if so, whether it is necessary to obtain evidence supporting the operating effectiveness of those other controls
- c. Evaluates whether the information produced by the entity is reliable, as discussed beginning at paragraph 3.103
- d. Determines an effective method for selecting the items to be tested to achieve the objectives of the procedure

3.96
Inquiry alone does not provide sufficient appropriate evidence of the operating effectiveness of controls. Some tests of controls provide more convincing evidence of the operating effectiveness of controls than others. Performing inquiry combined with inspection or reperformance ordinarily provides more convincing evidence than performing inquiry and observation. For example, a practitioner may inquire about and observe an entity's physical building security during the initial walk-throughs. Because an observation is pertinent only at the point in time at which it is made, the practitioner would supplement the observation with other procedures to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence regarding the operating effectiveness of the control throughout the period addressed by the examination. For example, the practitioner may inspect the video tapes that monitor the entrance of the facility, select a sample of individuals who enter the building, and determine whether the names of those individuals were included on the entity's list of authorized individuals during that period.

3.97
Because of the nature and methods of data storage used by a system, the practitioner may find the use of analytics to be a highly effective technique in performing his or her procedures, such as in the following examples:

- a. Documentation of authorization of entity management approvals may be stored in an online workflow system, permitting the records from the system to be extracted and analyzed.
- b. System logs may be scanned for unusual activity.
- c. Server security configuration parameters may be scanned and analyzed for consistency with policy.
- d. Access control lists can be analyzed for appropriateness of access rules.

When using analytics, the practitioner would perform procedures to validate the completeness and accuracy of the information received from the entity, as discussed beginning in paragraph 3.103.

3.98
The type of control being tested may affect the nature, timing, and extent of the testing performed by the practitioner. For example, for some controls, operating effectiveness is evidenced by documentation. In such circumstances, the practitioner may inspect that documentation. Other controls may not leave evidence of their operation and, accordingly, the practitioner may need to test their operating effectiveness at various times throughout the specified period.

3.99
There may be instances in which evidence that would have demonstrated the operating effectiveness of a control may be lost, misplaced, or inadvertently deleted by the entity. In such instances, the practitioner evaluates the other types of evidence available and whether the operating effectiveness of the control can be tested through other procedures, such as observation, that would provide sufficient appropriate evidence throughout the period. However, depending on the control and its significance to the achievement of the entity's principal system objectives, tests such as observation alone may not provide sufficient appropriate evidence. If the practitioner is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence in support of effectiveness of such controls, paragraph .68

of AT-C section 205 indicates that the practitioner should modify the opinion (for a scope limitation) if, in the practitioner's judgment, the effect of the matter is or may be material. If the practitioner concludes that the possible effects on the opinion on control effectiveness are or may be material (but not pervasive), the practitioner would modify the opinion for a scope limitation. In some circumstances, before modifying the opinion for a scope limitation, the practitioner may need to consider whether absence of evidence was a result of the failure of a control to operate effectively rather than the inadvertent destruction of evidence (for example, the destruction of the computer hard disk on which the evidence was stored).

3.100 In addition to procedures performed to directly test the operating effectiveness of a control, the practitioner may also perform procedures to obtain evidence that might contradict management's assertion that one or more controls functioned to prevent or detect errors or fraud. For example, the practitioner might obtain a listing of the system incidents identified throughout the period and compare the vulnerabilities exploited to the controls implemented to identify control deficiencies in the related control activities. This testing may identify deficiencies in specific controls designed to prevent or detect those incidents in a timely manner, permitting the practitioner to evaluate the effectiveness of the specific controls.

## Testing Review Controls

3.101 As part of evaluating and testing the design of a review control, the practitioner may need to obtain a sufficient understanding and document conclusions about the following matters:

- How the control is performed, including the precision with which it is performed and the specific steps involved in executing the review
- What the control owner considered when performing the review
- The criteria or thresholds used to trigger further investigation or other follow-up
- The steps involved in investigating and resolving matters identified by the review

3.102 The practitioner's test of the precision of each review control may include evaluating the same aspects of the control to determine whether the control operated the same way each time it was tested. The practitioner may need to determine whether the evidence gathered through the tests performed demonstrates that the review control consistently identified appropriate items for follow-up and that matters identified for investigation were resolved in a timely manner. Without documented instances of the review control identifying appropriate items for follow-up, the practitioner may not be able to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence that the review control operated as designed.
