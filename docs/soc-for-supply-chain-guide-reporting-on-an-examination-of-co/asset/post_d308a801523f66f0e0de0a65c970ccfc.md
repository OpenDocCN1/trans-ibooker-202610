d. Evidence is more reliable when it exists in documentary form, whether paper, electronic, or other media (for example, a contemporaneously written record of a meeting is ordinarily more reliable than a subsequent oral representation of what was discussed).

e. Evidence provided by original documents is more reliable than evidence provided by photocopies, facsimiles, or documents that have been filmed, digitized, or otherwise transformed into electronic form, the reliability of which may depend on the controls over their preparation and maintenance.

4.11 Evidence obtained from multiple sources or from sources of a different nature ordinarily provides more assurance than evidence obtained from a single source. In addition, conflicting evidence from different sources or of a different nature may indicate that an individual item of evidence is not reliable. For example, corroborating information obtained from a source that is independent of the responsible party may increase the assurance the practitioner obtains from a representation from the responsible party. Conversely, when evidence obtained from one source is inconsistent with that obtained from another, the practitioner should determine what additional procedures are necessary to resolve the inconsistency.

4.12 Whether sufficient appropriate evidence has been obtained on which to base the practitioner’s opinion is a matter of professional judgment. The practitioner’s professional judgment regarding what constitutes sufficient appropriate evidence is influenced by factors such as the following:

- a. The significance of a potential description misstatement or control deficiency and the likelihood that it will have a material effect, individually or when aggregated with other potential description misstatements and control deficiencies, on the presentation of the description of the entity’s system or on the effectiveness of controls
- b. The effectiveness of entity management’s responses to address the known risks
- c. The experience gained during previous consulting or examination engagements with respect to similar potential description misstatements and control deficiencies
- d. The results of procedures performed, including whether such procedures identified specific description misstatements and control deficiencies
- e. The source and reliability of the available information
- f. The persuasiveness of the evidence
- g. The practitioner’s understanding of the entity and its environment

## Describing Tests of Controls and Results of Tests in the Practitioner’s Report

4.16 Reporting guidance and example reports prepared in accordance with this guide include recommended disclosures intended to provide users with information that is likely to be needed to interpret the practitioner’s opinion as it relates to their own specific situations. As set forth in the description criteria, management is required to include in the description of the system information about the detailed controls that the entity has designed and implemented and operates to achieve its system objectives. To complement such disclosures, and to meet intended users’ needs, practitioners may include in their reports information about the practitioner’s tests of controls² and the results thereof. Table 4-1 summarizes information the practitioner would include in the report when describing the practitioner’s tests of controls and results.

## Table 4-1

## Information Describing Tests of Controls and Results

| Information Described in the Report | If No Deviations Were Identified | If Deviations Were Identified |
|---|---|---|
| The controls that were tested | Yes | Yes |
| Whether the items tested represent all or a selection of the items in the population | Yes | Yes |
| The nature of the tests performed, in sufficient detail to enable intended users to determine the effect of such tests on the users’ risk assessments | Yes | Yes |
| The number of items tested | No | Yes |
| The number and nature of the deviations | N/A | Yes |
| Causative factors | N/A | Optional |

² For brevity, the word tests as used hereinafter refers to procedures performed to obtain evidence about the operating effectiveness of controls, also known as tests of controls.

**4.17** Because a practitioner does not have the ability to determine whether an identified deviation will have significance to an individual report user, the concept of materiality is not applicable when reporting the results of tests of controls. Consequently, the practitioner's description of tests of controls and results includes all deviations identified during the examination. If the practitioner has not identified any deviations, the practitioner may document those results with a phrase such as "No exceptions noted" or "No deviations noted." Appendix E, "Illustrative SOC for Supply Chain Report (Including Entity Management's Assertion, Accountant's Report, and Illustrative Description of the System)," contains an example of a description of tests of controls in which no deviations have been identified.

**4.18** The description of tests of controls need not be a duplication of the practitioner's detailed audit program, which might make the SOC for Supply Chain report too voluminous for intended users and provide more than the required level of detail. 4.19 If deviations were identified, users are likely to expect disclosures about the number of items tested and the number and nature of the deviations identified even if, based on tests performed, the practitioner concludes that the controls necessary to provide reasonable assurance that the entity achieved its principal system objectives were effective based on the applicable trust services criteria. In addition, when sampling is used and deviations have been identified, disclosure of both the sample size and population size are likely to be helpful to users.

4.20 When deviations have been identified and disclosed in the description of procedures, additional disclosures about the identified deviations are useful to enable intended users to consider the effect of deviations on their own supply chain risks. Useful disclosures may include, to the extent known, the following information:

- Causative factors for the control deviations
- Controls that mitigated the effect of the control deviations
- Corrective actions taken by entity management
- Other qualitative factors
