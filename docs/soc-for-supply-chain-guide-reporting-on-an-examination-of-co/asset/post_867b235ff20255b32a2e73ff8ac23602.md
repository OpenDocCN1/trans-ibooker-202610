## 4.53

According to paragraph .A105 of AT-C section 205, the term pervasive describes the effects on the subject matter of misstatements, or the possible effects on the subject matter of misstatements, if any, that are undetected due to an inability to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence. Pervasive effects on the subject matter are those that, in the practitioner's professional judgment,

- a. are not confined to specific aspects of the subject matter;
- b. if so confined, represent or could represent a substantial proportion of the subject matter; or
- c. in relation to disclosures, are fundamental to the intended users' understanding of the subject matter.

## 4.54

As with the determination of whether a misstatement is material to the subject matter taken as a whole, the determination of whether a misstatement is pervasive is based on its effect on the subject matter taken as a whole. For instance, when opining on the description, a practitioner may consider whether a description misstatement affects a substantial portion of the subject matter or omits certain disclosures that are fundamental to intended users' understanding of the entity's system. When opining on control effectiveness, a practitioner may consider whether a material control deficiency is pervasive by considering the effect of the deficiency on one or more of the entity's principal system objectives. If the control deficiency affects only the achievement of one principal system objective, and the failure to achieve that objective is unlikely to broadly affect other principal system objectives, customers, or other parties, the practitioner may conclude that the material control deficiency is not pervasive to the subject matter taken as a whole.

## 4.55

Examples of factors that may be considered when determining whether material control deficiencies may have a pervasive effect on the effectiveness of controls include the following:

- a. The effect that entity-level controls have on the operation of other controls. Deficiencies in entity-level controls often have a pervasive effect on other controls.
- b. The extent of the use of segmentation across the entity's networks and systems. The greater the use of segmentation, the less likely it is that deficiencies in the operation of controls in one system will have a pervasive effect on the operation of controls in another one.
- c. The extent to which deficiencies in certain controls have a pervasive effect on other controls. For example, a practitioner is more likely to consider a control deficiency pervasive when the deficiency relates to the ineffectiveness of controls over the detection of system events relevant to security.

## 4.56

When the practitioner has concluded that a material misstatement is pervasive, the practitioner expresses an adverse opinion on both the description and the effectiveness of controls. In this situation, the practitioner's report would be modified by doing the following:

- Including, in a separate paragraph before the opinion paragraph, a clear explanation of the matters giving rise to the modification. When opining on control effectiveness, the practitioner may consider including in that paragraph the effect of the matters on the system’s ability to provide reasonable assurance that the entity’s principal system objectives were achieved.
- Stating in the opinion paragraph that, *because of the significance of the matters referred to in the preceding paragraph*, the description does not present the system that was designed and implemented in accordance with the description criteria and the controls were not effective to provide reasonable assurance that the entity’s principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria, in all material respects.
- Amending the practitioner’s responsibility paragraph to state that the practitioner believes that the evidence obtained is sufficient and appropriate to provide a basis for the practitioner’s *adverse opinion*.

### Scope Limitation

## 4.57

A practitioner may express an unmodified opinion only when he or she has conducted the examination in accordance with the attestation standards. If the practitioner has been unable to apply all the procedures considered necessary in the circumstances, the practitioner would not have complied with the attestation standards. In that case, there may be a limitation on the scope of the examination.

## 4.58

According to paragraph .A107 of AT-C section 205, a scope limitation may arise from any of the following:

- Circumstances beyond the control of entity management. For example, documents may have been accidentally destroyed or documents may have been in the custody of a supplier, whose services are no longer in use.
- Circumstances relating to the nature or timing of the practitioner’s work. For example, a physical process that the practitioner considers necessary to observe may have occurred before the practitioner’s engagement or may not be performed regularly during the examination period. (However, an inability to perform a specific procedure does not constitute a scope limitation if the practitioner is able to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence by performing alternative procedures.)
- Limitations imposed by entity management (or the engaging party, if different). For example, entity management may have imposed a limitation that prevents the practitioner from performing a procedure that the practitioner considers necessary in the circumstances. Limitations of this kind may have other implications for the engagement, such as for the practitioner’s consideration of risks of material misstatement and for engagement acceptance and continuance.

## 4.59

When there is a scope limitation, the practitioner should determine the pervasiveness of the effects or possible effects on the description and on the effectiveness of controls. According to paragraph .70 of AT-C section 205,

the practitioner should express a qualified opinion when the practitioner is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence on which to base the opinion and the practitioner has concluded that the possible effects on the subject matter of undetected description misstatements or control deficiencies, if any, could be material but not pervasive to the subject matter. (Disclaiming an opinion because of a scope limitation is discussed beginning in paragraph 4.62.)

## 4.60

When the practitioner has determined that a qualified opinion is appropriate because of a limitation in the scope of the examination, the practitioner's report would be modified by doing the following:

- a. Including, in a separate paragraph before the opinion paragraph, a clear explanation of the matters giving rise to the modification
- b. Stating, in the opinion paragraph, that except for the possible effects of the matters giving rise to the modification, the description presents the system that was designed and implemented in accordance with the description criteria and controls were effective to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria, in all material respects
- c. Amending the practitioner's responsibility paragraph to state that the practitioner believes the evidence obtained is sufficient and appropriate to provide a basis for the practitioner's qualified opinion

## 4.61

If the practitioner decides to express a qualified opinion because of a scope limitation, and also concludes there were material misstatements in the description or material deficiencies in the effectiveness of controls, the practitioner should include, in separate paragraphs of the report, a clear explanation of both the scope limitation and the matters that cause the description or the conclusion on control effectiveness to be materially misstated.

## Disclaimer of Opinion

## 4.62

Paragraph .74 of AT-C section 205 indicates that the practitioner should disclaim an opinion when the practitioner is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence on which to base the opinion and the practitioner concludes that the possible effects on the subject matters of undetected misstatements, if any, could be both material and pervasive. In such circumstances, the practitioner may also reach a mutual agreement with the engaging party to terminate the engagement or may withdraw from the engagement.

## 4.63

When disclaiming an opinion, the following apply:

- a. The first sentence of the practitioner's report should be revised to state, "We were engaged to examine" rather than "We have examined."
- b. The standards under which the practitioner conducts an examination are identified at the end of the second sentence of the report, rather than in a separate sentence in the "Accountant's Responsibilities" section of the report.
- c. A separate paragraph of the report should state that, because of the limitation on the scope of the examination, the scope of the practitioners' work was not sufficient to enable the practitioner to express an opinion.
