2.55 Paragraph .07 of AT-C section 205 requires the practitioner to agree on and document the terms of the engagement with the engaging party. A written agreement, such as an engagement letter, reduces the risk that either the practitioner or entity management may misinterpret the needs or expectations of the other party. For example, it reduces the risk that entity management may rely on the practitioner to protect the entity against certain risks or to perform certain management functions.

2.56 Paragraph .08 of AT-C section 205 states that the agreed-upon terms of the engagement should include the following:

- a. The objective and scope of the engagement
- b. The responsibilities of the practitioner
- c. A statement that the engagement will be conducted in accordance with attestation standards established by the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants
- d. The responsibilities of the responsible party and the engaging party, if different
- e. A statement about the inherent limitations of an examination engagement
- f. Identification of the criteria for the measurement, evaluation, or disclosure of the subject matter
- g. An acknowledgment that the engaging party agrees to provide the practitioner with a representation letter at the conclusion of the engagement

2.57 Paragraph .41 of AT-C section 205 indicates that, if the practitioner plans to use internal auditors to provide direct assistance, prior to doing so, the practitioner should obtain written acknowledgment from the responsible party (usually entity management) that internal auditors providing direct assistance to the practitioner will be allowed to follow the practitioner’s instructions and that the responsible party will not intervene in the work the internal auditors perform for the practitioner. If the engaging party is the responsible party, the practitioner may wish to include this matter in the engagement letter.

2.58 In addition to the matters discussed in the previous two paragraphs, the practitioner may want to reach an understanding with entity management about other matters, such as the principal system objectives that have been preliminarily identified by management. Additional matters that may affect the practitioner’s understanding of the terms of the engagement and how the terms should be documented in a recurring engagement are discussed in paragraph .09 of AT-C section 205.

2.59 Although not required by the attestation standards, the practitioner would ordinarily request a signed engagement letter from the engaging party. The engaging party’s refusal to provide the practitioner with an engagement letter may cause the practitioner to question whether a mutual understanding regarding the terms of the engagement has been reached and, in turn, may affect the practitioner’s decision about whether to accept or continue the engagement.

## Accepting a Change in the Terms of the Examination

**2.60** After the engagement agreement is executed but prior to the completion of the engagement, entity management may communicate a desire to change the scope of the engagement. For example, a change in scope may occur when there is a change in the trust services category or categories to be addressed by the examination; in the product, production line, or production facility to be addressed by the examination; in the boundaries of the system being examined; or in the components of the system covered by the examination.

**2.61** When entity management requests a change in the scope of the engagement, paragraph .29 of AT-C section 105 states that the practitioner should not agree to the change in the terms of the engagement unless there is reasonable justification for the change. Examples of situations in which there may be reasonable justification for a change include the following:

- *a.* Misunderstanding concerning the nature and scope of the examination originally requested.
- *b.* Change in the informational needs of intended users.
- *c.* Identification of additional system components, principal system objectives, or expansion of the boundaries of the system to be included in the description to enhance the presentation of the description.
- *d.* Determination that certain system components, which were preliminarily identified as part of the system, are not relevant to the products produced, manufactured, or distributed.
- *e.* Determination that the system the entity uses to manufacture certain products addressed in the description is not relevant to intended users.
- *f.* For an initial engagement, management's discovery, as a result of the practitioner's initial planning, that it has not been retaining evidence supporting the functioning of several key controls. As a result, management requests that the starting date of the examination period be shifted back by three months in order for it to correct its evidence-retention practices.

**2.62** Other changes to the scope of the engagement, however, may not be considered reasonable if they relate to information that is incorrect, incomplete, or otherwise unsatisfactory. Examples of situations in which there may not be reasonable justification for a change include the following:

- *a.* A request to change the period covered by the examination or to exclude portions of the system from the scope of the examination to avoid a modified practitioner's opinion.
- *b.* A request to change from an examination that addresses the effectiveness of controls to an examination that addresses only the suitability of design of implemented controls, when the intention is to avoid disclosure of a material system incident identified during the period.
- *c.* For a recurring engagement, the practitioner determines he or she is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence supporting the functioning of several key controls due to staffing changes at the entity, which led to the discontinuation of the retention of key evidence. Management requests that the starting date of the examination be shifted back by two months in order for it to correct its evidence-retention practices.

2.63 If, after using professional judgment, the practitioner believes there is reasonable justification to change the terms of the engagement from those originally contemplated, the practitioner would adjust the scope of the engagement accordingly and assess whether further procedures are necessary to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence to support an opinion. When issuing that opinion, the attestation standards do not require the practitioner’s report to include a reference to (a) the original engagement, (b) any procedures that may have been performed, or (c) scope limitations that resulted in the changed engagement. The practitioner may decide to document the change in the engagement in an addendum to the written agreement with the client to evidence the parties’ agreement to the change.

2.64 However, if the practitioner and the engaging party are unable to agree to a change in the terms of the examination, the practitioner and the engaging party may agree to continue the engagement in accordance with the original terms or mutually agree to terminate the engagement. If the engaging party does not accept either of these alternatives, the practitioner may disclaim an opinion or withdraw from the engagement.

## Establishing an Overall Examination Strategy for and Planning the Examination

2.65 When planning the examination, the engagement partner and other key members of the engagement team develop an overall strategy for the scope, timing, and conduct of the engagement and an engagement plan, consisting of a detailed approach for the nature, timing, and extent of procedures to be performed. Adequate planning helps the practitioner devote appropriate attention to important areas of the engagement, identify potential problems on a timely basis, and properly organize and manage the engagement to make sure it is performed in an effective and efficient manner. Adequate planning also assists the practitioner in properly assigning work to engagement team members and facilitates the direction, supervision, and review of their work. Furthermore, if the work of internal auditors, other practitioners, or specialists is used in the engagement, proper planning helps the practitioner coordinate their work.

2.66 Paragraph .11 of AT-C section 205 requires a practitioner to establish an overall engagement strategy that sets the scope, timing, and direction of the engagement and guides in the development of the engagement plan. In establishing the overall engagement strategy, the practitioner may consider the following:

- a. Understanding the characteristics of the engagement that define its scope, including the terms of the engagement, the entity’s principal system objectives, the characteristics of the subject matter, and the criteria
- b. Ascertaining the expected timing and nature of required communications
