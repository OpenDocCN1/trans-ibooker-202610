In such situations, the practitioner considers whether changes to a control made after the testing, but prior to the end of the examination period, would change his or her conclusion regarding the effectiveness of the control and performs additional testing as deemed necessary.

## Testing Superseded Controls

3.119 If (a) the entity makes significant changes to controls during the period, (b) the superseded controls are relevant to the achievement of the entity's principal system objectives based on the applicable trust services criteria, and (c) the practitioner believes that deficiencies in the superseded controls would be relevant to the practitioner's opinion, the practitioner should obtain sufficient appropriate evidence about the design and operating effectiveness of those controls. Such procedures may include, if possible, testing the controls to be superseded before the change. Assume, for example, that during the examination period June 1, 20X0, to May 31, 20X1, an entity replaced a manual control with an automated control on December 15, 20X0. The practitioner would test the manual control for the period from June 1, 20X0, to December 14, 20X0, and the automated control for the period from December 15, 20X0, to May 31, 20X1, taking into consideration the nature of the control and the frequency of its operation.

3.120 If (a) the practitioner is unable to test the superseded controls (for example, because the control does not leave evidence of its operation after a period of time or because the practitioner was engaged after the control was superseded) and (b) the controls are significant to the achievement of the entity's principal system objectives based on the applicable trust services criteria, the practitioner discloses that fact in the description of tests performed and results thereof. In such circumstances, the practitioner should determine the effect on the practitioner's report. If the circumstances result in a material scope limitation, the practitioner should modify the opinion. (See the relevant paragraphs within paragraphs .68–.81 of AT-C section 205 for reporting requirements when the practitioner is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence.)

## Using Sampling to Select Items to Be Tested

3.121 If a control operates frequently, the practitioner may consider whether to use audit sampling when testing the operating effectiveness of the control. When determining the extent of testing needed and considering whether sampling is appropriate, the practitioner should consider (a) the characteristics of the population of control to be tested, including the nature of the control; (b) whether the population is made up of homogenous items; (c) the frequency of the control's application; and (d) the expected deviation rate. AICPA Audit Guide *Audit Sampling* may be useful to the practitioner when performing sampling.

3.122 Before deciding to use sampling in a SOC for Supply Chain examination, the practitioner should consider whether sampling is an appropriate strategy for testing a control. The following are examples of situations in which the practitioner might decide that sampling would not be appropriate:

- a. Due to the design of one or more systems, it may not be possible to give every item in the population a chance of being selected for the sample.
- b. The practitioner may determine that a 100% test of the control using data analytics is necessary because even a one-time failure of the control could result in a material deficiency in the effectiveness of controls.
- c. The practitioner may conclude that it is more efficient and more effective to perform a 100% test of the data evidencing the effective operation of the control than selecting and testing a sample.

3.123 In such circumstances, the practitioner needs to consider whether the assumptions for sample-based testing have been met in applying professional judgment regarding the extent of testing.

## Selecting Items to Be Tested

3.124 For tests of controls using sampling, the practitioner determines the tolerable rate of deviation and uses that rate to determine the number of items to be selected for a particular sample.

3.125 When selecting sample items, the practitioner's selection should result in a sample that is representative of the population covering the reporting period, as addressed in paragraph .31b of AT-C section 205. Random selection of items represents one means of obtaining such samples.

## Additional Risk Considerations Related to Suppliers and Business Partners

### Controls That Suppliers Expect the Entity to Implement

3.126 When an entity obtains raw materials, components, or services from a supplier, the supplier may expect the entity to implement certain controls in order for the supplier to fulfill its commitments to the entity. The controls to be implemented may be communicated in an authoritative communication, such as a contract, or as CCCs in a SOC for Supply Chain report obtained from the supplier. If the supplier's SOC for Supply Chain report identifies the need for CCCs at the entity, and the controls stated in the description do not include controls that are necessary to address the CCCs identified, there is a risk that the controls at the entity are not suitably designed. Entity management usually considers the need for such controls during its risk assessment. For example, an entity that outsources aspects of its technology infrastructure to a service provider may find that the supplier's description of its systems includes the following CCC:

> Customers should have controls in place to restrict access to system resources to appropriate customer personnel.

In such a situation, entity management would identify the risk of inappropriate access to the supplier system and would design controls responsive to that risk.

**3.127** Suppliers, business partners, and other third parties with access to the entity's system may access confidential information through the system or transmit information between themselves and the system. For example, an entity may obtain data used in calculations via an automated transmission of data initiated by a supplier accessing the entity's system. The supplier's access results in vulnerabilities that could be exploited by others and, consequently, increases the risks that could threaten the achievement of one or more of the entity's principal system objectives.

**3.128** In response, entity management needs to understand the nature of such risks and assess their likelihood and magnitude. The controls that entity management designs, implements, and operates to mitigate those risks depend on whether the third party's controls are necessary, in combination with controls at the entity, to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives are achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria. For example, a managed services provider might be responsible for monitoring server capacity and usage and for projecting future capacity demands based on historical trends. In that scenario, controls at the service provider are likely to be necessary, in combination with the entity's controls, for the entity to achieve its principal system objectives based on the applicable trust services criteria.

**3.129** Alternately, the entity may design and implement control activities that address the risks represented by interactions with the managed service provider, or the entity may have designed and implemented processes and controls to monitor the activities of the service provider. If so, the service provider's controls may not be necessary for the entity to achieve its principal system objectives based on the applicable trust services criteria. In the same data center hosting example noted in the preceding paragraph, assume the entity independently performs high-level capacity monitoring activities and reviews the future capacity demands projected by the service provider for appropriateness. In this scenario, the entity's controls alone may be sufficient to provide reasonable assurance that its principal system objectives are achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria.

**3.130** Processes and controls the entity may perform to mitigate the risks associated with interactions with a supplier, customer, or business partner are outlined in trust services criterion CC9.2 and include all or a combination of the following:

- a. Establishing specific requirements for supplier and business partner arrangements that include scope of services and product performance specifications, roles and responsibilities, compliance requirements, and service levels
