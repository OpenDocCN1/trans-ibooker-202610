3.34 The practitioner may identify in the description statements indicating that one or more specific controls exist when such controls have not been implemented. In such instances, the practitioner will often conclude that such statements constitute a misstatement and may ask entity management to revise the description. If entity management does not make the necessary revisions, the practitioner would consider the effect of the misstatement on his or her opinion.

3.36 Although entity management may describe the controls in the description, it also might refer to a table of controls presented in a separate section of the report. If the description refers to a table of controls, the table is considered part of the description and, therefore, is also addressed by the practitioner's examination. Often, the practitioner describes the tests of controls performed and the results thereof in the same table. Guidance on the types of information to be included in the practitioner's description of tests of controls and the results thereof is discussed beginning in paragraph 4.16.

3.37 An entity may have controls it considers to be outside the boundaries of the system addressed by the description, such as controls related to the conversion of new customers to the entity's systems. To avoid misunderstanding by report users, the description is expected to clearly delineate the boundaries of the system included within the scope of the examination. If the description does not contain such information, the practitioner may conclude that the description is misstated. Determining the boundaries of the system is discussed beginning in paragraph 2.31.

3.38 The practitioner may perform a variety of procedures to obtain evidence about the nature and extent of disclosures related to individual controls and the applicable trust services criteria, including a combination of the following:

- a. Reading contracts with customers and business partners, such as performance or service level agreements, marketing materials distributed to customers and business partners or posted on the entity's website, and other available documentation to evaluate whether the controls the entity has implemented are suitably designed to achieve the entity's commitments to customers (For example, reading service level agreements may help the practitioner understand the specific product performance specifications with which a product must comply as well as other production, manufacturing, or distribution specifications with which the entity has agreed to comply.)
- b. Observing controls or other activities performed by entity personnel
- c. Reading documents about the entity's security awareness and training programs, communication of code of conduct, employee handbooks, security policies, incident notification procedures, and other available documentation to understand the entity's processes for communicating to entity personnel their responsibilities for system security and other related matters
- d. Reading policy and procedure manuals, system documentation, flowcharts, narratives, asset management records, and other system documentation to understand IT policies and procedures and controls over data loss prevention, access provisioning and deprovisioning, user identification and authentication, data destruction, system event monitoring and detection, and backup procedures
- e. Reading sample contracts with suppliers (for example, contract templates or a selection of contracts) and associated performance or service level agreements and other documentation to understand how the entity's contracting process addresses security-related matters; the interrelationship between the entity and its suppliers, including the entity's process for assessing and managing system risks associated with those suppliers; the process the entity uses to identify its responsibilities for implementing controls that have been communicated by a supplier of goods or services (for example, customer responsibilities or complementary customer controls [CCCs] identified in a supplier's contract); and the procedures the entity performs to monitor the effectiveness of controls performed by suppliers, when complementary supplier controls (CSCs) have been identified
- f. Reading incident response and recovery plan documentation to understand the entity's processes for recovering from identified system events, including its incident response procedures, incident communication protocols, recovery procedures, alternate processing plans, and procedures for the periodic testing of recovery procedures
