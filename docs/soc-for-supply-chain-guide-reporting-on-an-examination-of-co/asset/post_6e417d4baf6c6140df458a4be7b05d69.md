## Considering Entity-Level Controls

2.107 Entity management designs, implements, and operates controls at the entity level that are necessary to support the achievement of its principal system objectives and affect the operation of system-level control activities. Controls that address the trust services criteria for the control environment component of internal control (CC1.1–1.5) are examples of entity-level controls. Although entity-level controls can also address the achievement of principal system objectives based on the applicable trust services criteria for the communication and information (CC2.1–2.3), risk assessment (CC3.1–3.4), and monitoring (CC4.1–4.2) components of internal control, entity management often addresses those criteria by designing, implementing, and operating controls that are performed at the system level. As an example, assume that the entity performs an enterprise-wide risk assessment and assesses its information security risk and its infrastructure risk at the system level. Because the latter two assessments are likely to be more relevant to the SOC for Supply Chain examination, the practitioner ordinarily devotes more time and attention to obtaining an understanding of those assessments than to understanding the enterprise-wide risk assessment.

2.108 Nevertheless, effective entity-level controls, particularly those designed and implemented to meet the trust services criteria related to the control environment, may enable the practitioner to place greater confidence in the processes and controls the entity has designed, implemented, and operated to provide reasonable assurance that its principal system objectives were achieved. Thus, effective entity-level controls may reduce the extent of the procedures the practitioner believes are necessary to perform to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence about the operating effectiveness of the controls stated in the description to support the opinion. Effective entity-level controls may also affect decisions related to when such procedures are planned to be performed.

2.109 In contrast, deficiencies in entity-level controls often have a pervasive effect on the effectiveness of other controls. If the practitioner determines that certain entity-level controls did not operate effectively, the practitioner may be able to adjust the nature, timing, and extent of procedures performed to obtain evidence about whether the controls stated in the description were effective. In some situations, however, deficiencies in the operation of entity-level controls may lead the practitioner to conclude that controls did not operate effectively. For example, consider an entity that has been unable to retain knowledgeable employees. In that situation, the practitioner may decide to increase the extent of testing of controls that prevent and detect system incidents (for example, inspection of security configurations and event management scan logs) to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence about whether the controls stated in the description operated effectively.

2.110 The practitioner may find it necessary to understand the root cause of any identified deficiencies in entity-level controls in order to assess the impact they may have on the effectiveness of the related controls stated in the description. Ways in which a practitioner may respond to ineffective entity-level controls in an examination include the following:

+   a. Selecting different types of procedures, or changing the timing of planned procedures, to obtain evidence about the effectiveness of controls
+   b. Obtaining more extensive evidence about the effectiveness of controls

2.111 Because of the important effect that entity-level controls may have on the effectiveness of controls stated in the description, the description of the system often includes disclosures about the entity-level controls designed, implemented, and operated to address the risks that threaten the entity's achievement of its principal system objectives. The description of the entity's system presented in appendix E illustrates such disclosures. It also illustrates, in section 4 of the description, examples of the tests the practitioner may perform to determine whether entity-level controls applicable to the trust services criteria relevant to the control environment were effective throughout the period.

## Understanding the Internal Audit Function

2.112 An internal audit function performs assurance and consulting activities designed to evaluate and improve the effectiveness of the entity's governance, risk management, and internal control processes. In some entities, activities like those performed by an internal audit function may be conducted by functions with other titles (for example, a business unit that performs quality assurance over certain aspects of the manufacturing process or an internal control function that routinely assesses principal system objective control activities). Some or all of the activities of an internal audit function may also be outsourced to a third-party service provider. For example, an entity may engage a service provider to perform (a) certain functions or tests that the internal audit function does not have the competency or qualifications to perform (for example, performing the IT internal audit function or penetration testing) or (b) a special, one-time controls assessment at the request of the board of directors. Neither the title of the function nor whether it is performed by the entity or by a third-party service provider is a sole determinant of whether the practitioner can use the work of internal auditors. Rather, it is the nature of the activities, the extent to which the internal audit function’s organizational status and relevant policies and procedures support the objectivity of the internal auditors, the competence of internal auditors, and the systematic and disciplined approach of the function that are relevant. References in this guide to the work of the internal audit function include relevant activities of other functions or third-party providers that have these characteristics.

2.113 Activities of the internal audit function that may be relevant to the SOC for Supply Chain examination include those that provide information or evidence about whether the description is presented in accordance with the description criteria or whether controls were effective. If the internal audit function does not perform activities related to the examination, or if the entity does not have a function that performs similar activities, the practitioner may need to consider the effect on his or her conclusions regarding the effectiveness of monitoring activities.

2.114 If, after obtaining an understanding of the internal audit function, the practitioner concludes that (a) the activities of the internal audit function are not relevant to the examination or (b) it may not be efficient to consider the work of the internal audit function, the practitioner does not need to consider the work of the internal audit function.

2.115 The practitioner may determine, however, that the examination can be performed more effectively or efficiently by using the work of the internal audit function or obtaining direct assistance from internal audit function personnel. The phrase "using the work of the internal audit function" usually refers to using work designed and performed by the internal audit function, in accordance with an internal audit plan, to obtain evidence to support the achievement of the entity’s principal system objectives. This differs from work the internal audit function performs to provide direct assistance to the practitioner, including assistance in performing tests of controls that are designed by the practitioner and performed by members of the internal audit function under the practitioner’s direction, supervision, and review. When members of the internal audit function provide direct assistance, the procedures they perform are like work performed by the engagement team.⁶

2.116 When the practitioner expects to use the work of the internal audit function in obtaining evidence or to provide direct assistance, paragraphs .39–.42 of AT-C section 205 describe procedures the practitioner should perform to determine whether the work can be used for purposes of the engagement. Chapter 3 provides further guidance on the practitioner’s considerations when performing procedures on that work.

2.117 The extent to which the practitioner plans to use the work of the internal audit function is a matter of professional judgment. Because the practitioner has sole responsibility for expressing an opinion on the description and on the effectiveness of controls, the practitioner makes all significant judgments
