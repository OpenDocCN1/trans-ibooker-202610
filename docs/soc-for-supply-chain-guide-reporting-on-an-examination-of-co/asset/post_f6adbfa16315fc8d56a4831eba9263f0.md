## Subsequent Events and Subsequently Discovered Facts

3.184 Events or transactions may occur after the period of time covered by the examination, but prior to the date of the practitioner’s report, that could have a significant effect on the description or on the effectiveness of controls. In such circumstances, disclosure of those events and transactions in the description or in entity management’s assertion may be necessary to prevent intended users from being misled.

3.185 The following are examples of events that could affect the description of the entity’s system or entity management’s assertion:

- a. After the period covered by the examination, entity management discovered that, during the last quarter of that period, the IT security director provided all the programmers with access to the production data files, enabling them to modify data.
- b. After the period covered by the examination, entity management discovered that a security breach occurred during the period covered by the practitioner’s report.

3.186 Paragraph .48 of AT-C section 205 requires the practitioner to inquire of entity management (and, if different, the engaging party) about whether it is aware of any such events. If such events exist, the practitioner should apply appropriate procedures to obtain evidence regarding the events. For example, the practitioner may obtain evidence by inquiring about and considering information regarding the operating effectiveness of controls by inspecting the following:

- a. Relevant internal auditors' reports issued during the subsequent period
- b. Other practitioners' reports issued during the subsequent period
- c. Relevant regulatory agencies' reports issued during the subsequent period
- d. Reports on other professional engagements for that entity

3.187 Paragraph .48 of AT-C section 205 does not require the practitioner to perform any procedures regarding the description or the effectiveness of controls after the date of the practitioner’s report. However, paragraph .49 of AT-C section 205 clarifies that the practitioner is responsible for responding appropriately to facts that become known after the date of the practitioner’s report that, had they been known as of the report date, may have caused the practitioner to revise the report.

3.188 After obtaining information about an event, the practitioner determines whether the facts existed at the date of the practitioner’s report and, if so, whether persons who would attach importance to these facts are currently using, or likely to use, the SOC for Supply Chain report (which includes the description, entity management’s assertion, and the practitioner’s opinion). The practitioner may do this through discussions with entity management and other appropriate parties and through the performance of additional procedures that the practitioner considers necessary to determine whether the description, assertion, and practitioner's opinion need revision or whether the previously issued report continues to be appropriate.

### 3.189
Specific actions to be taken at that point depend on a number of factors, including the time elapsed since the date of the practitioner's opinion and whether issuance of a subsequent report is imminent. Depending on the circumstances, the practitioner may determine that notification of persons currently using or likely to use the practitioner's report is necessary. This may be the case, for example, when

- a. the SOC for Supply Chain report is not to be relied on because the description, entity management's assertion, or the practitioner's report needs revision or the practitioner is unable to determine whether revision is necessary; and
- b. issuance of a subsequent practitioner's report is not imminent.

### 3.190
If the practitioner believes the event is of such a nature and significance that its disclosure is necessary to prevent intended users from being misled, the practitioner should determine whether information about the event is adequately disclosed in the description or in entity management's assertion. For example, assume that, after the period covered by the examination but prior to the date of the practitioner's report, entity management learns of a security breach. After investigation, entity management determines that the incident stemmed from an otherwise unknown vulnerability in its system; furthermore, that vulnerability existed during the examination period. In this example, the practitioner ordinarily would expect management to disclose the matter in the description and assertion. If it is not, the practitioner's course of action depends on the practitioner's legal and ethical rights and obligations, and the practitioner may consider seeking legal advice before deciding on a course of action. Appropriate actions may include

- a. disclosing the event (including a description of the nature of the event and its effect on the description, assertion, or report) in the practitioner's report and modifying the related practitioner's opinion; and
- b. withdrawing from the engagement.

## Subsequent Events Unlikely to Have an Effect on the Practitioner’s Report

### 3.191
The practitioner may have determined that the event discovered subsequent to the period covered by the examination would likely have had no effect on the description or on the effectiveness of controls because the underlying situation did not exist until after the period addressed by the SOC for Supply Chain examination. However, the matter may be sufficiently important to warrant disclosure by entity management in its description and, potentially, emphasis by the practitioner in the practitioner's report. The following are examples of such events:

- a. The entity was acquired by another entity.
- b. The entity experienced a significant operating disruption or other extraordinary event such as an event caused by weather or other natural disaster.
- c. A data center hosting entity that provides applications and technology to enable customers to perform essential business functions made significant changes to its information systems, including a system conversion or significant outsourcing of operations, after the date of the SOC for Supply Chain report.

## Documentation

### 3.192
Paragraphs .34–.41 of AT-C section 105, *Concepts Common to All Attestation Engagements*, provide requirements regarding the documentation that should be prepared for an attestation engagement. Those paragraphs address matters such as the timeliness of the documentation, how to make necessary changes to the documentation after the original preparation date, retention of engagement documentation, confidentiality of documentation, and the need to document situations in which the practitioner judges it necessary to depart from a relevant presumptively mandatory requirement.

### 3.193
Additionally, paragraphs .87–.89 of AT-C section 205 discuss the practitioner's responsibilities for preparing and maintaining documentation that is appropriate to an examination. The practitioner's documentation in a SOC for Supply Chain examination is the principal record of attestation procedures applied, information obtained, and conclusions or findings reached by the practitioner. The quantity, type, and content of documentation are matters of the practitioner's professional judgment. However, the documentation should be sufficient to determine the following:

- a. The nature, timing, and extent of the procedures performed to comply with AT sections 105 and 205 and applicable legal and regulatory requirements, including the following:
  - i. The identifying characteristics of the specific items or matters tested
  - ii. Who performed the engagement work and the date such work was completed
  - iii. The discussions with entity management or others about findings or issues that, in the practitioner's professional judgment, are significant, including the nature of the significant findings or issues discussed and when and with whom the discussions took place
  - iv. When entity management will not provide one or more of the requested written representations or the practitioner concludes that there is sufficient doubt about the competence, integrity, ethical values, or diligence of those providing the written representations, or the written representations are otherwise not reliable, the matters in paragraph .55 of AT-C section 205 (see discussion beginning in paragraph 3.180)
  - v. Who reviewed the engagement work performed and the date and extent of such review
- b. The results of the procedures performed and the evidence obtained
