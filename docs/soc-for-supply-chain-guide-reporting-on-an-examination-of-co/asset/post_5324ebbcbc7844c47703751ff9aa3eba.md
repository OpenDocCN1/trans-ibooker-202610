## Responding to and Communicating Known and Suspected Fraud, Noncompliance With Laws or Regulations, Uncorrected Misstatements, and Deficiencies in the Effectiveness of Controls

### Known or Suspected Fraud or Noncompliance With Laws or Regulations

3.163 As discussed in chapter 2, the practitioner has a responsibility to consider known or suspected incidents of fraud⁵ and noncompliance with laws or regulations relevant to the subject matter of the examination. Such incidents may include, for example, the intentional bypassing of controls or the intentional misstatement of one or more aspects of the description. As discussed in paragraph 3.144, when a control deficiency or deviation is the result of an intentional act, particularly one perpetrated by a member of senior entity management, it is likely to be considered material; a deficiency or deviation caused by an unintentional act may not be considered material. The practitioner determines the effect of such incidents on the description, the effectiveness of controls, and the practitioner's opinion. Additionally, the practitioner communicates such information to appropriate parties.

3.164 When incidents of fraud or suspected fraud are identified during the examination, paragraph .33 of AT-C section 205 requires the practitioner to respond appropriately. For example, unless prohibited by law, regulation, or ethics standards, appropriate responses may include the following:

> 5 Paragraph .10 of AT-C section 105, Concepts Common to All Attestation Engagements, defines fraud as an intentional act involving the use of deception that results in a misstatement in the subject matter or the assertion.

- a. Discussing the matter with senior entity management (and the engaging party, if different) and other appropriate parties, unless senior entity management is suspected to have committed the fraud
- b. If the practitioner suspects fraud involving senior entity management, communicating those suspicions to those charged with governance and discussing with them the nature, timing, and extent of procedures necessary to complete the examination
- c. Requesting that senior entity management (and the engaging party, if different) consult with an appropriately qualified third party, such as the entity's legal counsel or a regulator
- d. Considering the implications of the matter in relation to other aspects of the engagement, including the practitioner's risk assessment and the reliability of written representations from entity management (and the engaging party, if different)
- e. Obtaining legal advice about the consequences of different courses of action
- f. Communicating with third parties (such as a regulator)
- g. Withdrawing from the engagement

3.165 The actions noted in paragraph 3.164 may also be appropriate in response to identified or suspected noncompliance with laws or regulations relevant to the examination. In addition, the practitioner may decide to describe the matter in a separate paragraph in the practitioner's report, unless the following apply:

- a. The practitioner is precluded by entity management (or the engaging party, if different) from obtaining sufficient appropriate evidence to evaluate whether noncompliance that may be material to the conclusion about the effectiveness of controls has, or is likely to have, occurred. In this situation, there is a scope limitation that precludes the practitioner from expressing an opinion on control effectiveness. Accordingly, the practitioner would disclaim an opinion.
- b. The practitioner concludes that the noncompliance results in the failure of the entity's controls to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria. In this situation, the practitioner expresses a modified opinion.

### Communicating Incidents of Known or Suspected Fraud, Noncompliance With Laws or Regulations, Uncorrected Misstatements, or Internal Control Deficiencies

3.166 In addition to responding to known and suspected fraud and noncompliance with laws or regulations, the practitioner should communicate information regarding those matters, along with information regarding any uncorrected description misstatements or material deficiencies, to the appropriate levels of entity management (and to the engaging party, if different). The practitioner may also consider whether to communicate other matters.

3.167 If the practitioner identifies or suspects noncompliance with laws or regulations that are not relevant to the subject matters of the SOC for Supply Chain examination, the practitioner should determine whether he or she has a responsibility to report the identified or suspected noncompliance to parties other than entity management (and the engaging party, if different).

3.168 The practitioner may be precluded from reporting such incidents to parties outside the entity because of the practitioner's professional duty to maintain the confidentiality of client information. However, the practitioner's legal responsibilities may vary by jurisdiction, and in certain circumstances, the duty of confidentiality may be overridden by statute, law, or courts of law. A duty to notify parties outside the entity may exist

- a. in response to a court order or
- b. in compliance with requirements for examinations of entities that receive financial assistance from a governmental agency.

3.169 Because potential conflicts with the practitioner's ethical and legal confidentiality obligations may be complex, the practitioner may decide to consult with legal counsel before discussing noncompliance with parties outside the entity.

### Obtaining Written Representations

3.170 During the examination, entity management (usually the responsible party) makes many oral and written representations to the practitioner in response to specific inquiries or through the presentation of the description and entity management's assertion. Such representations from entity management are part of the evidence the practitioner obtains. However, they cannot replace other evidence the practitioner could reasonably expect to be available, nor do they provide sufficient appropriate evidence on their own about any of the matters with which they deal. Furthermore, the fact that the practitioner has received reliable written representations does not affect the nature or extent of other evidence that the practitioner obtains.

3.171 It is up to the practitioner to determine the appropriate person or persons within entity management or the governance structure with whom to interact, taking into consideration which person or persons have the appropriate responsibilities for and knowledge of the matters concerned. In certain circumstances, the practitioner may obtain written representations from parties in addition to entity management, such as those charged with governance.

3.172 In some cases, the party making the assertion may be indirectly responsible for and knowledgeable about specified matters covered in the representations. For example, an entity's chief information officer may be knowledgeable about certain matters through personal experience and about other matters through employees who report to her. The practitioner may request that individuals who are directly or indirectly responsible for and knowledgeable about matters covered in the written representations provide their own representations.

3.173 Written representations ordinarily confirm representations explicitly or implicitly given to the practitioner, indicate and document the continuing appropriateness of such representations, and reduce the possibility of a misunderstanding concerning the matters that are the subject of the representations.
