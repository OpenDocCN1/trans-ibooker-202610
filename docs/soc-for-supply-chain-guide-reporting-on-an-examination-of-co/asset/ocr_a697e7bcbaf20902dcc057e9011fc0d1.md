# Revising the Risk Assessment

3.158 Paragraph .34 of AT-C section 205 states that the practitioner's assessment of the risks of material misstatement may change during the course of the examination as additional evidence is obtained. If the practitioner obtains evidence from performing further procedures, or if new information is obtained, either of which is inconsistent with the evidence on which the practitioner originally based the assessment, the practitioner should revise the assessment and modify the planned procedures accordingly. Such further procedures may include asking entity management to modify the description, as necessary.

# Evaluating the Sufficiency and Appropriateness of Evidence

3.159 Sufficient appropriate evidence is necessary to support the practitioner's opinion. Such evidence is cumulative in nature and may come from sources inside or outside the entity. Evidence comprises both information that supports and corroborates aspects of the subject matter and any information that contradicts aspects of the subject matter. In some cases, the absence of information (for example, refusal by the responsible party to provide a requested representation) may also constitute evidence to be considered.

3.160 The practitioner should evaluate the sufficiency and appropriateness of the evidence obtained in the context of the engagement and, if necessary, attempt to obtain further evidence. Concluding on the sufficiency and appropriateness of evidence is discussed beginning in paragraph 4.08. As discussed in paragraphs .46–.47 of AT-C section 205, if the practitioner is unable to obtain necessary further evidence, the practitioner should consider the implications for the practitioner's opinion. Such implications are discussed in paragraphs .68–.84 of AT-C section 205.

# Evaluating the Results of Procedures

3.161 The practitioner evaluates the results of all procedures performed and conducts both a quantitative (for example, rates of deviations in testing a control using a sample-based testing strategy) and qualitative analysis of whether identified description misstatements result in a description that is not presented in accordance with the description criteria or whether identified control deficiencies result in an incorrect conclusion about the effectiveness of controls. As an example, assume that, when investigating the follow-up and resolution of two identified system incidents, the practitioner determined that the resolution took two days longer than the management-prescribed resolution requirement to complete. However, management indicated that exceeding the resolution requirement was necessary to implement the preferred solution. In such an instance, the practitioner may conclude that the control deficiencies are not material to the opinion on control effectiveness. However, if the practitioner's testing determined that entity personnel failed to follow up at all for the two instances, the practitioner might conclude that such controls were not operating effectively. Therefore, the practitioner would have to consider the effect of that control deficiency on the entity's ability to provide reasonable assurance that one or more of the principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria.
