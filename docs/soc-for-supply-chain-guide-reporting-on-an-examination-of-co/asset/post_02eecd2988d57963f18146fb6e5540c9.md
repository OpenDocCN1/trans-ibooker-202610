**3.71** Suitably designed controls, if operating effectively, provide reasonable assurance of achieving the entity's principal system objectives. Suitably designed controls operate as designed by persons who have the necessary authority and competence to perform the controls. Entity management is responsible for designing and implementing controls to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria, identifying the risks that threaten the achievement of the principal system objectives, modifying the controls as necessary based on new and evolving risks, and evaluating the linkage between the controls and the evolving risks that threaten the achievement of the principal system objectives.

**3.72** Evaluating the suitability of the design of controls involves assessing whether the controls stated in the description adequately address the risks identified by entity management. This assessment is a two-step process:

- 1. Obtaining an understanding of and assessing the robustness of entity management's risk assessment process to identify, design, and implement effective controls
- 2. Assessing whether controls identified, designed, and implemented by entity management are suitably designed

**3.73** When evaluating management's risk assessment process, the practitioner does the following:

- a. Obtains an understanding of entity management's risk assessment process, which includes the following:

- i. Consideration of how entity management

- (1) identifies and assesses the risks that threaten the achievement of the entity's principal system objectives based on the applicable trust services criteria
- (2) identifies information used by the system to produce, manufacture, or distribute the product and how it determines the threats to that information
- (3) incorporates information from its monitoring activities, including new threats and exploits, evidence about the operating effectiveness of controls that indicated there was a deficiency in the design of the controls, system events, and circumstances not previously considered
- (4) identifies its responsibilities for implementing controls that have been communicated by a supplier of goods or services (for example, customer responsibilities or CUEs identified in a supplier's contract)

- ii. Evaluation of the frequency with which entity management updates the risk assessment and supporting processes and controls

- iii. Consideration of whether entity management uses a recognized security framework for managing its system processes and controls (for example, the National Institute of Standards and Technology's "Framework for Improving Critical Infrastructure Cybersecurity" [NIST cybersecurity framework], the Consortium for Information and Software Quality [CISQ] standard for information and software reliability and security, or International Organization for Standardization and International Electrotechnical Commission [ISO/IEC] Standards 27001 and 27002) as part of its assessment and entity management process

- iv. Consideration of whether the risk assessment also takes into account the risk of fraud, such as entity management override of identified controls at the entity, misappropriation of assets by entity personnel, creation by entity personnel of false or misleading documents or records, and inappropriate physical and logical access to the system and the underlying infrastructure through social engineering attacks or similar measures

- b. Assesses the completeness and accuracy of entity management's identification of risks that threaten the achievement of the entity's principal system objectives

- c. Evaluates the linkage between the controls stated in the description and those risks

- d. Evaluates the adequacy of precision and reliability of the controls as designed, based on the risks they are meant to mitigate

## 3.74 When evaluating whether controls are suitably designed, the practitioner may consider the following:

- a. The frequency or timing of the occurrence or performance of the control
- b. The authority and competence of the individual responsible for performing the control
- c. The tasks within the control being performed and the precision and sensitivity of those tasks (for example, the results of reviews and related follow-up activities)
- d. Evidence that contradicts the assertion that the control is functioning as designed, such as the rate of system incidents identified related to the control
- e. Whether the control, when considered with other controls, addresses each of the risks that threaten the achievement of the principal system objectives based on the trust services criteria to which it relates

## 3.75 The likelihood and magnitude of the realization of a risk will also affect the practitioner's judgment on the suitability of the design of controls. For example, a manufacturer may permit personnel on the production floor to sign onto its computer system with a shared user ID that displays only the day's production schedule. The practitioner may assess the risk associated with the loss of accountability in this instance to be low and conclude that the control is suitably designed. However, if a shared user ID were used to move software changes to production at the same manufacturer, the practitioner would likely conclude that the loss of accountability is a control deficiency that results in the failure to mitigate the risk that unauthorized changes to software result in products that do not meet product specifications.

3.76 Factors such as the size and complexity of the entity are also important considerations when evaluating the suitability of the design of controls. A smaller, less complex entity may be able to address risks that threaten the achievement of its principal system objectives by using a different set of controls than a larger, more complex entity. For example, a smaller, less complex entity may

- a. have policies and procedures that are less formal and detailed but sufficient for the practitioner to evaluate;
- b. have fewer levels of entity management, which may result in more direct oversight of the operation of key controls; and
- c. make greater use of manual controls versus automated controls.

## Multiple Controls Are Necessary to Address an Applicable Trust Services Criterion

3.77 The entity may have multiple controls in place to address the risks that threaten the achievement of the entity's principal system objectives based on an applicable trust services criterion. In this case, the practitioner may need to consider the suitability of each of those controls when determining whether controls are suitably designed to address each of the risks associated with a particular criterion.³ For example, trust services criterion A1.2, The entity authorizes, designs, develops or acquires, implements, operates, approves, maintains, and monitors environmental protections, software, data backup processes, and recovery infrastructure to meet its objectives, addresses, among other things, the risk that a server will not be able to support system availability in the event of a distributed denial of service attack. The entity can address one aspect of this risk (and thus one element of that criterion) by, for example, designing and implementing a control that provides redundant load-balanced infrastructure protected by mechanisms for detecting and dropping access attempts. In this situation, when evaluating the suitability of design, the practitioner would also have to consider the other controls the entity has designed and implemented to achieve the other aspects of that criterion.

3.78 The practitioner may conclude that there are no controls in place to support one or more aspects of an applicable trust services criterion.
