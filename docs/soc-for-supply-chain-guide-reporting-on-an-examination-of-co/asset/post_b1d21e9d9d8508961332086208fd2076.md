## More Than One Control Addresses a Particular Risk

3.79 The entity may have more than one control that addresses a particular risk that threatens the achievement of its principal system objectives. In that situation, if the practitioner determines that one of the identified controls is not suitably designed, the practitioner would ordinarily consider whether the other control has been designed. If the other control has been suitably designed, the practitioner would test the effectiveness of that control. In addition, the practitioner would ask entity management to revise the description to exclude the control that is not suitably designed and include the control or controls that are suitably designed based on the applicable trust services criterion.

## Procedures to Obtain Evidence About the Suitability of Design of Controls

3.80 The practitioner evaluates the suitability of the design of controls by using evidence and other information gathered when

- a. obtaining an understanding of the entity's system and the controls within that system and
- b. determining whether the description of the system presents the system that was designed and implemented in accordance with the description criteria (including evidence obtained from performing walk-throughs)

3.81 To supplement such evidence and other information, the practitioner generally performs a combination of the following procedures:

- a. Inquiry of entity personnel about the design and operation of applicable controls and the types of system events that have occurred or that may occur
- b. Inspection of documents produced by the entity
- c. Performing additional walk-throughs of control-activity-related policies and procedures
- d. Reading applicable and supporting system documentation

- e. Determining whether the entity has adequately addressed widely used attacks and vulnerability exploitations (including those identified publicly by organizations such as the United States Computer Emergency Readiness Team) and emerging risks and threats

3.82 Entity management may document controls in a variety of ways. The nature and extent of documentation usually varies, depending on the size and complexity of the entity and its monitoring activities. In some cases, the practitioner may be able to obtain and inspect entity management's documentation of controls, including its identification of risks and evaluation of the linkage of controls to those risks. In that case, the practitioner may use the documentation as a starting point when evaluating the completeness, accuracy, relevance, and timeliness of entity management's identification of risks and the suitability of design of the controls implemented to mitigate those risks.

## Additional Considerations When the Carve-Out Method Is Used for a Supplier

3.83 If the entity uses the carve-out method for a supplier, the practitioner evaluates whether the types of controls expected to be implemented at the supplier, if operating effectively in combination with the controls at the entity, are necessary to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives would be achieved. The practitioner also considers whether evidence exists that the entity has communicated to the supplier the entity's requirements with respect to the types of controls that are expected to be implemented and whether there is any evidence that control deficiencies exist at the supplier.

3.84 Examples of procedures that may be performed to obtain such evidence include the following:

- a. Reading contracts and other communications with the supplier to determine whether they identify the types of controls expected to be implemented at the supplier
- b. Obtaining an understanding of the procedures in place at the entity to evaluate and monitor the implementation, suitability of design, and the operating effectiveness of controls at the supplier (for example, the performance of tests at the supplier by entity personnel)
- c. Obtaining and evaluating a SOC for Supply Chain report on the supplier's system

3.85 As an example, assume an entity develops, tests, and approves program changes but has outsourced the actual implementation of the changes to the supplier. In this case, the practitioner would conclude that controls at the supplier are necessary to achieve the principal system objectives based on trust services criterion CC8.1, The entity authorizes, designs, develops or acquires, configures, documents, tests, approves, and implements changes to infrastructure, data, software, and procedures to meet its objectives.

## Evaluating Deficiencies in the Suitability of Design of Controls

3.86 A deficiency in the design of a control occurs when a risk that threatens the achievement of one or more of the principal system objectives has not been sufficiently mitigated by one or more controls. In contrast, a deficiency in the operation of a control exists when a properly designed control does not operate as designed. An entity may be able to correct a deficiency in the operation of a control, for example, by providing additional training to the employee who performs the control. However, if the design of the control is deficient, the control will not operate effectively regardless of who performs it. For that reason, the practitioner often would not test the operating effectiveness of a control that is not suitably designed. Instead, the practitioner generally would consider the design of other controls, if any, that address the same risks.

3.87 In some situations, two or more controls are suitably designed only when operating in conjunction with each other. In these situations, the practitioner evaluates the suitability of the controls together in order to reach a conclusion.

3.88 Paragraph 4.75 presents an example of a separate paragraph that might be added to the practitioner's report when the practitioner determines that controls were not suitably designed to provide reasonable assurance that one or more of the entity's principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria.

## Obtaining Evidence About the Operating Effectiveness of Controls

3.89 Controls that operate effectively provide reasonable assurance of achieving the entity's principal system objectives based on the applicable trust services criteria. The practitioner tests the operating effectiveness of the controls stated in the description by performing procedures (known as tests of controls) to obtain evidence about how the controls were applied, the consistency with which they were applied, and by whom or in what manner they were applied. When an entity uses the inclusive method to present the services and controls of a supplier, the practitioner also applies tests of controls to the controls at the supplier.

3.90 If the practitioner has identified controls that were not suitably designed, the practitioner generally would not test the operating effectiveness of those controls. However, in certain circumstances, intended users may expect controls identified in the description to be tested by the practitioner. In such situations, the practitioner may choose to perform such testing and include the results of the testing in the report, if the practitioner determines that including such information would not be misleading.

## Designing and Performing Tests of Controls

3.91 According to paragraph .24 of AT-C section 205, the practitioner should design and perform tests of controls to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about the effectiveness of controls stated in the description of the entity's system. By including those controls in the description, entity management has identified them as part of the system the entity has designed, implemented, and operated to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria.
