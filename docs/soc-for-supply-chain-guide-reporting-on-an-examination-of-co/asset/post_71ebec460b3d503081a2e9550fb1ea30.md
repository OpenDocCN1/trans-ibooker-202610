
2.42 If controls at third parties are necessary, in combination with the entity's controls, to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives are achieved, entity management identifies and assesses the risks of doing business with those third parties. If those risks represent a significant threat to the achievement of the entity's principal system objectives (for example, because of the nature of those parties' access to the system, the provision of critical raw materials or components, or the controls they operate on behalf of the entity), third-party risk and control assessment activities addressing the third parties' activities are necessary to enable entity management to determine whether the processes and controls performed by those third parties effectively mitigate the identified risks. For example, third-party risk and control assessment activities for a supplier may include a combination of the following:

- Quality control testing of raw materials, components, or other inputs received
- Testing of controls at the supplier by entity personnel or third parties
- Holding periodic discussions with supplier personnel and evaluating supplier performance against established service-level objectives and agreements
- Making site visits to the supplier to evaluate its processes
- Inspecting attestation reports (such as type 2 SOC 2® reports) on the supplier's system
- Monitoring external communications, such as complaints from customers, entity communications about system events, communications from regulators, and regulatory activities that relate to the products or services provided by the supplier

When such third-party risk and control assessment activities do not exist or they appear inadequate, it may be difficult for entity management to conclude that it has a reasonable basis for its assertion.

2.43 If the practitioner believes the subject matter is not appropriate (because entity management does not have a reasonable basis for its assertion) or expects that evidence needed to support the opinion is unlikely to be available, AT-C section 105 indicates that the practitioner should not accept the examination.

## Assessing the Suitability and Availability of Criteria

2.44 As discussed in chapter 1, two distinct sets of criteria are used in the SOC for Supply Chain examination: description criteria and trust services criteria. The description criteria in supplement A and the trust services criteria in supplement B were promulgated by the Assurance Services Executive Committee. As discussed in chapter 1, such criteria meet the requirements in paragraph .25bii of AT-C section 105 for criteria that are both suitable and available for use in the examination discussed in this guide.

## Determining Whether the Entity’s Principal System Objectives Are Reasonable in the Circumstances

2.45 As discussed in chapter 1, the trust services criteria are used to evaluate the effectiveness of controls to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives were achieved. Consequently, the practitioner's evaluation of the suitability of the criteria depends on whether the entity's principal system objectives are reasonable under the circumstances. The practitioner should understand the preliminary principal system objectives identified by entity management. The principal system objectives are those that are relevant to the trust services category or categories addressed by the examination and that could reasonably be expected to influence the relevant decisions of the intended users of the report. In addition, early evaluation can help the practitioner identify potential risks to the successful completion of the engagement and establish a more thorough understanding of the engagement scope and risks prior to engagement acceptance.

2.46 An entity's principal system objectives generally include those that address customers' needs and expectations and usually relate to (a) meeting the product's performance specifications and requirements for production, manufacturing, and distribution processes and (b) conforming with other matters, including standards and contractual or other commitments related to a product, such as its availability. For example, entity management may identify a principal system objective for commitments made to customers about the availability of its products in the quantities and at the times agreed on with customers, when such commitments are common to most customers. If, however, entity management makes a different commitment about product availability to an individual customer, that system objective ordinarily would not be disclosed in the description because information about that specific customer commitment is unlikely to be relevant to intended users.

2.47 When considering whether the principal system objectives established by management, and identified in the description, are reasonable in the circumstances, the practitioner may consider the following questions:

- Do the principal system objectives relate to the types of risks that the system presents to intended users to which controls at the entity could reasonably be expected to relate (for example, cybersecurity risks to the user that are affected by logical access controls to prevent or detect unauthorized access to the system)?
- Are the principal system objectives complete? An examination based on a complete set of principal system objectives can provide intended users with the information they need to assess the effect of the entity's controls on the risks associated with doing business with the entity. However, the practitioner ordinarily will not be able to determine how the entity's controls specifically address the risks specific to each intended user; accordingly, the practitioner cannot consider completeness from the perspective of individual intended users. Instead, individual intended users are expected to assess whether the principal system objectives disclosed in the description included those that are relevant to them.

2.48 If the practitioner believes the principal system objectives identified by entity management and stated in the description do not appear reasonable, the practitioner may need to discuss the matter with entity management. If entity management is unwilling to revise the description of its principal system objectives, the practitioner may refuse to accept the engagement. Alternatively, the practitioner may decide to accept the engagement but agree to restrict the use of the report to the subset of intended users who can be presumed to understand the limitations of the report caused by the failure to disclose certain principal system objectives and the risks associated with achieving them. Chapter 3, "Performing the SOC for Supply Chain Examination," discusses considering the disclosures that entity management makes about its principal system objectives as part of the evaluation of whether the description presents the system that was designed and implemented in accordance with the description criteria. It also discusses the situation when, after accepting the engagement, the practitioner obtains evidence that causes him or her to believe that the entity's principal system objectives are not appropriate for the examination.

2.49 Although the practitioner evaluates the reasonableness of the principal system objectives during the engagement, the practitioner's opinion does not directly address the reasonableness of those objectives.

## Requesting a Written Assertion and Representations From Entity Management

2.50 Paragraph .10 of AT-C section 205 requires the practitioner to request a written assertion from the responsible party[^5] that addresses whether (a) the description presents the system designed and implemented in accordance with the description criteria and (b) the controls were effective to provide reasonable assurance that the entity's principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria.

2.51 Entity management's assertion is included in the SOC for Supply Chain report along with the description and the practitioner's opinion. Because management's assertion plays an important role in the engagement, it may be useful for the practitioner to provide entity management with an example of a written assertion prior to engagement acceptance. However, entity management is responsible for drafting its written assertion and may word the assertion in accordance with its practices, as long as the assertion addresses management's conclusions about each of the subject matters discussed in paragraph 2.50 and is not materially inconsistent with the subject matter or the practitioner's opinion.

2.52 An illustrative example of a management assertion is presented in appendix C, "Illustrative Management Assertion in a SOC for Supply Chain Examination." Appendix E, "Illustrative SOC for Supply Chain Report (Including Entity Management's Assertion, Accountant's Report, and Illustrative Description of the System)," also contains an illustrative management assertion.

2.53 If entity management refuses to provide a written assertion, paragraphs .82-.83 of AT-C section 205 require the practitioner to withdraw from the engagement, when withdrawal is possible under applicable laws and regulations. Consequently, it is advisable to obtain entity management's agreement to provide the written assertion prior to engagement acceptance.
