
## Disclosures About Significant Changes to the System During the Period

3.58 Description criterion DC10 requires disclosure of significant changes to the entity's system during the period addressed by the description that are relevant to the achievement of the entity's principal system objectives. Significant changes to be disclosed consist of those that are likely to be relevant to intended users.

3.59 DC10 also contains implementation guidance that is intended to assist practitioners when evaluating the nature and extent of disclosures about significant changes to the system.

## Evaluating Description Misstatements Identified During the Examination

3.60 As discussed in paragraph 2.88, the term description misstatement is used to describe differences between the description and the description criteria or omissions in the description. The following are examples of description misstatements:

- a. Inclusion of inappropriate information. For example, the inclusion of information that is not objectively measurable, such as unsubstantiated advertising claims
- b. Omission of necessary information. For example, omission of information about relevant subsequent events or changes to controls, relevant principal system objectives, CCCs, or CSCs
- c. Misstatements of fact. For example, the inclusion of controls that have not been implemented or principal system objectives that are incomplete

3.61 When the practitioner identifies a material misstatement in the description, the practitioner ordinarily discusses the misstatement with entity management. In many situations, entity management would elect to revise the description to correct the misstatement.

3.62 If the description is not revised, the practitioner would assess whether the identified description misstatement is material by considering whether the effect of the misstatement, in relation to the description taken as a whole, could reasonably be expected to influence relevant decisions made by intended users of the report.

3.63 When making that assessment, paragraph .A15 of AT-C section 205 indicates that materiality is considered in the context of qualitative factors and quantitative factors. Because the description criteria call for primarily nonfinancial disclosures, most descriptions are narrative presentations. Thus, the practitioner's materiality considerations are mainly qualitative.

3.64 Qualitative factors the practitioner may consider when making this assessment include the following:

- Whether the misstatement results in the failure to meet one or more specific description criteria — such failure may be indicative of a material misstatement.
- Whether there are indicators that the potential effect of the misstatement is material to relevant decisions made by intended users based on the description.
- The effect of the misstatement or potential misstatement on the description as a whole (see paragraph 3.62).
- The seriousness of the consequences of the misstatement to non-compliance with laws or regulations. As an example, assume a wholesale produce distributor claims it has effective system controls to meet system requirements that support its compliance with certain laws and regulations over food safety. However, the practitioner's tests indicated that such controls were not effective, and entity management refused to revise the description. When considering whether the misstatement is material, the practitioner may consider the consequences of the failure to comply with such laws and regulations, which could involve both loss of reputation and monetary fines. Alternatively, because of the serious potential consequences of foodborne contamination on human health, the practitioner may consider a misstatement involving compliance with food safety regulations more significant to intended users than a misstatement related to matters other than food safety.
- Whether the misstatement is the result of an intentional or unintentional act.

3.65 The following are some examples related to materiality with respect to the description of the entity's system:

- a. Example 1. Example Entity uses a service provider to perform its back-office functions and elects to use the carve-out method. The description includes information about the nature of the services provided by the service provider and describes the third-party risk and control assessment activities and other controls performed at the entity with respect to the processing performed by the service provider. The description includes such information because it is likely to be relevant to intended users and, therefore, such information would be considered material to the description of the entity's system.
- b. Example 2. Example Entity manufactures running shoes at three different facilities within the United States: Coos Bay, Oregon; Naperville, Illinois; and Iowa City, Iowa. The Oregon and Illinois facilities use the same manufacturing control system, 1Run. The Iowa facility uses a separate control system, Hawkeyes, that is segmented from the 1Run system. In total, 80% of Example Entity's shoes are manufactured in the Oregon facility, and 18% are manufactured in the Illinois facility. The Iowa facility manufactures less than 5% per year, and for the last three years has been averaging just 2% of shoe production. Thus, Example Entity management has described the Oregon and Illinois facilities in the description as being within the scope of the examination, but it has determined that the Hawkeyes system, which it uses in the Iowa facility, is not material to the overall manufacturing of running shoes. As such, it disclosed that fact in the description and stated that the Hawkeyes manufacturing control system is not included within the boundaries of the system being examined.

3.66 Paragraph .45 of AT-C section 205 requires the practitioner to accumulate description misstatements identified during the engagement, other than those that are clearly trivial, to evaluate whether, individually or in the aggregate, those misstatements are material when forming the practitioner's opinion.

3.67 If the practitioner determines that the effects of identified description misstatements, individually or in the aggregate, are material with respect to the description, based on consideration of materiality as discussed beginning in paragraph 3.60, the practitioner should modify the opinion on the description. When modifying the opinion, the practitioner's understanding of the nature and cause of the description misstatements enables the practitioner to determine how to appropriately modify the opinion. Chapter 4 discusses modifications of the practitioner's report.

## Considering Whether the Description Is Misstated or Otherwise Misleading

3.68 Paragraph .60 of AT-C section 205 requires the practitioner to evaluate whether the description is misleading within the context of the engagement based on the evidence obtained. Because a description in accordance with the description criteria is designed to meet the common informational needs of intended users, this paragraph focuses on whether there are additional disclosures, beyond those called for by the description criteria, that are necessary to prevent intended users from being misled about the system described in the description. For example, if a processing facility was destroyed by fire shortly after the examination but before the issuance of the practitioner's report, disclosure of that event may be helpful to intended users.

3.69 If the practitioner believes additional disclosures beyond those required by the criteria are necessary, the practitioner may ask the responsible party to amend the description. Such additional disclosures may be present in the description (in which case, they are subject to the practitioner's examination procedures) or as other information. The practitioner's responsibility for other information presented in a SOC for Supply Chain report is discussed beginning at paragraph 4.82. If the responsible party refuses to add the disclosures, the practitioner should consider the effect on the practitioner's report, including modifying the opinion on the description, adding explanatory language to the practitioner's report, restricting the use of the practitioner's report to those with more comprehensive understanding of the issue, or withdrawing from the engagement.
