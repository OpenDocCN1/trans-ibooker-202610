2.17 The "Independence Rule" (ET sec. 1.200.001)⁴ of the code establishes independence requirements for attestation engagements. The "Independence Standards for Engagements Performed in Accordance With Statements on Standards for Attestation Engagements" subtopic (ET sec. 1.297) of the "Independence Rule" establishes special independence requirements for a practitioner who provides services under the attestation standards. In addition, the "Conceptual Framework Approach" subtopic (ET sec. 1.210) of the "Independence Rule" discusses threats to independence not specifically detailed elsewhere. The "Independence Rule" is followed by interpretations of the rule that assist the practitioner in assessing independence. The code specifies that, in some circumstances, no safeguards can reduce an independence threat to an acceptable level. For example, the code specifies that a covered member may not own even an immaterial direct financial interest in an attest client because there is no safeguard to reduce the self-interest threat to an acceptable level. A member may not use the conceptual framework to overcome this prohibition or any other prohibition or requirement in an independence interpretation.

2.18 When performing engagements in which independence is required in accordance with the attestation standards, the practitioner needs to be independent with respect to the responsible party (or parties), as defined in those standards. If the inclusive method will be used to present information about the design and operation of controls at a supplier in the entity's description, supplier management is also a responsible party. Consequently, the practitioner should also be independent of the supplier. Use of the inclusive method is discussed beginning at paragraph 2.140.

2.19 When the practitioner is not independent but is required by law or regulation to accept the engagement and report on the subject matter, the practitioner should disclaim an opinion and should specifically state that the practitioner is not independent. The practitioner is neither required to provide, nor precluded from providing, the reasons for the lack of independence; however, if the practitioner chooses to provide the reasons for the lack of independence, the practitioner should include all the reasons for the lack of independence.

## Competence of Engagement Team Members

2.20 Chapter 1, "Introduction and Background," of this guide discusses quality in the examination. Maintaining appropriate quality in the engagement involves having the work performed by engagement team members with the appropriate competence and capabilities. For that reason, as discussed in paragraph 1.72, the practitioner should not accept the examination unless he or she has determined that the individuals who would perform the engagement have the appropriate competence and capabilities to perform it.

2.21 When considering the competence and capabilities of engagement team members, the engagement partner should be satisfied that the team assigned to the engagement (including any practitioner's external specialists) collectively has the appropriate competence or capabilities. Such competencies and capabilities include the following:

- a. An understanding, or the ability to obtain an understanding, of systems used to produce, manufacture, or distribute goods, including an understanding of the operation and security of such systems, gained either through training or experience with engagements of a similar nature and complexity
- b. Knowledge of the entity's industry and business, including whether the industry in which the entity operates is subject to specific types of risks
- c. An understanding of production, manufacturing, distribution, and supply chain processes and related controls relevant to the entity's business and industry
- d. Knowledge of any common technologies used by the entity to produce, manufacture, or distribute goods and any uncommon technologies or industry-specific technologies used by the entity
- e. Knowledge of relevant information and technology systems and processes, such as CPUs, industrial control systems, computer numerical control (CNC) machinery, software, infrastructure, and networking
- f. Experience evaluating the effectiveness of controls relevant to security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy
- g. An understanding of professional standards and the ability to apply professional skepticism and judgment in the examination
- h. An understanding of legal and regulatory requirements relevant to the examination

2.22 The competencies and capabilities required for the engagement discussed in this guide are likely to differ from those required for a SOC 1 or SOC 2 engagement in certain key respects. The systems used to produce, manufacture, or distribute goods typically involve processes and system components that are significantly different from those used by a service organization to deliver its services. Most service organizations' systems are primarily focused on processing data in the form of transactions or providing computing resources; therefore, their processes and controls relate primarily to data processing. A manufacturer's process includes the handling of physical materials throughout the production cycle. As a result, an examination of a production system may require a multidisciplinary engagement team that includes the following competencies: engineering, manufacturing, embedded software, and supply chain.

2.23 The engagement partner should be satisfied that team members are informed of their responsibilities, including the objectives of the procedures that they are to perform and matters that may affect the nature, timing, and extent of such procedures. The engagement partner should also be satisfied that engagement team members have been directed to bring to the partner's attention any significant questions raised during the engagement.

2.24 The engagement partner may decide to supplement the knowledge and skills of the engagement team with the use of specialists. Planning to use the work of a practitioner's specialist is discussed in paragraph 2.120.

## Preconditions of the Engagement

2.25 A practitioner should accept or continue an engagement only if the preconditions for an attestation engagement identified in paragraphs .24–.25 of AT-C section 105 are met:

- a. The practitioner is independent in accordance with the AICPA Code of Professional Conduct. (See paragraph 2.16.)
- b. Entity management accepts responsibility for the
    - i. preparation of the description in accordance with the description criteria and
    - ii. effectiveness of controls to provide reasonable assurance that the entity’s principal system objectives were achieved based on the applicable trust services criteria.
- c. The subject matters of the examination are appropriate. (Defining the system to be examined is discussed beginning at paragraph 1.22; determining the appropriateness of the subject matter is discussed beginning at paragraph 2.26.)
- d. The criteria used to prepare and evaluate the subject matters are both suitable and available to users of the report. (The suitability and availability of the description criteria and the trust services criteria are discussed at paragraphs 1.45 and 1.48, respectively; the reasonableness of the principal system objectives stated in the description is discussed beginning at paragraph 2.45.)
- e. The practitioner expects to be able to obtain the evidence needed to arrive at an opinion on the description and the effectiveness of controls and will have
    - i. access to all information relevant to the measurement, evaluation, or disclosure of the subject matter;
    - ii. access to additional information that he or she may request; and
    - iii. unrestricted access to entity personnel.

## Determining the Appropriateness of the Subject Matter

2.26 According to paragraph .A37 of AT-C section 105, subject matter is appropriate if it is (a) identifiable, (b) capable of consistent measurement or evaluation based on the criteria, and (c) can be subjected to procedures for obtaining sufficient appropriate evidence to support an opinion. In a SOC for Supply Chain examination, the subject matters are the description of the entity’s system and the effectiveness of the controls stated in the description. Entity management is responsible for identifying the specific subject matter (that is, defining the system, related controls, and the boundaries of the system) to be examined.

2.27 To understand the subject matters, the practitioner first needs to understand the system. This begins with identifying the major processes within the system and the boundaries of the system. The identification of the boundaries of the system determines which components of the system are to be included in the examination, including certain control activities that may be the responsibility of customers or suppliers.
