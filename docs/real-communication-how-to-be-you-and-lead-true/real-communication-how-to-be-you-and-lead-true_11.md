### CHAPTER 6 SHARE PERSONAL STORIES

About ten years ago, I was running a workshop for a senior leadership team. I was told that the CEO was sceptical about the power of storytelling in business, but she eventually agreed to the workshop.

It became clear within minutes that the whole organisation was suffering within a toxic culture. In over 35 years of working in business, I have never seen such bullying by anyone, let alone the CEO to her direct reports. It was an environment of intimidation and fear, and was extremely unpleasant to witness as a facilitator.

At one point the CEO’s aggression moved from her team to me. She crossed her arms in a show of disdain for everything I was saying, and declared she had no need to learn how to use stories to communicate. She wanted the facts and figures, and not anything else. She challenged me that if I could not provide her with one valid reason to stay and learn storytelling, she would leave.

### Real Communication

So I went around the room asking her team how they liked to be communicated to. Did they prefer:

- facts and stats
- stories and examples?

As expected, the responses varied between the two options. Some of us are more left-brain aligned with our thinking, so we like lots of data, research and factual information. Others are more right-brain aligned, and like stories and examples that provide meaning to the data. But very rarely do we only rely on just one side. Most of us want a combination of both facts and stories.

I explained that this is the reason, when it comes to communicating, that we need to think less about how we like being communicated to and more about how the audience prefers to be communicated to. Just because we rely on logic, for example, this does not mean our audience does.

Now, if you have such a variety of communication preferences in a room of 12 people, imagine the variety in an audience of thousands. This is why communicating only in your preferred style is not only inconsiderate, but also ineffective.

After morning tea, everyone returned for the rest of the session, including the reluctant CEO.<sup>1</sup>

> If we want to communicate effectively, we can’t just focus on our own preferred style of communication; we need to be more balanced.

### Just give me the facts

In business, we tend to lean towards focusing on logic and logic alone. We have been taught, perhaps all our career, that being professional is about being data driven and providing just facts and figures. We have probably been told by managers in the past to ‘just show me the facts’ or ‘business is not personal’. You may have even been told not to show any emotion.

<sup>1</sup> Fair to say, I have never worked with this company since ... nor do I want to.





### — 分享个人故事 —

现实是，作为人类，我们是情感生物，虽然逻辑在商业中有其地位，但大多数时候，逻辑只是提供信息，而不能影响或激励人。

重要的是，逻辑通常不能影响或改变行为。

情感在消费者行为中有多大影响，已有大量文献记录。例如，在《笛卡尔的错误》一书中，南加州大学神经科学教授安东尼奥·达马西奥（Antonio Damasio）表明，当我们评估品牌时，购买决策是基于个人感受和经验，而不是基于更具逻辑性的信息，例如产品特性和优势。

早在 2500 年前，亚里士多德就指出，当你试图影响和说服他人时，需要三样东西：

- 1. Logos：严密的逻辑
- 2. Ethos：个人可信度
- 3. Pathos：情感连接。

这一点在今天依然适用。

现在，我认为我们不需要在商业沟通中使用逻辑方面的额外帮助。我认为我们已经通过饼图、柱状图和 Excel 电子表格将其掌控了。我们需要更多说服的是故事的力量。

你需要严密的逻辑、个人可信度 **和** 情感连接，才能吸引受众。

### 故事的魔力

故事是吸引受众最有力的方式之一，因为当讲述得当时，它们会：

- 激发吸引并引起他们兴趣的情感
- 让他们对讲述故事的你产生连接感
- 帮助建立信任和可信度
- 影响他们做出决定
- 帮助他们更好地理解并记住信息，且记忆更持久。

这种益处的组合让我想起，当丽莎·辛普森告诉荷马，培根、火腿和猪排都来自同一种动物时，荷马回答说：“是啊，丽莎。一种奇妙的‘神奇’动物”。

故事确实是一种奇妙的神奇野兽，以下是原因。

### 建立连接并增加信任

在第 3 章中，我谈到了我们都渴望彼此更好地连接。嗯，好故事能让我们感受到这一点。

神经经济学家保罗·扎克（Paul Zak）对讲故事时催产素对大脑的强大影响进行了惊人的研究。催产素常被称为“信任激素”，当我们与所爱和信任的人在一起时，身体会自然释放它。扎克的研究表明，当我们听故事时，它也会被释放。因此，根据扎克的研究，听到好故事时释放的这种催产素，能帮助我们与讲故事的人建立连接。

> 随着商业中信任的整体下降，使用讲故事与受众建立真实连接比以往任何时候都更加重要。

### 影响受众做出决策

如前所述，神经科学家安东尼奥·达马西奥的研究表明，情感在人们做决策时起着重要作用。

达马西奥的研究涉及检查额叶受损的人。这是大脑负责形成情感的区域。

除了无法感受或表达情感外，达马西奥研究组的参与者在记忆、注意力持续时间、语言理解和表达方面的智力正常。然而，关键的是，他们也无法轻易做出决策，例如决定吃什么。

达马西奥表明，这种优柔寡断源于参与者不断分析每个决策的利弊，以至于无法做出决策。参与者无法感受情感，去除了决策的情感一面。没有这个情感因素，他们就无法做决定。（如果你曾经说过，“我知道我应该吃沙拉，但我想吃汉堡”，你就是做出了情感决策。）

另一位神经科学家、纽约时报畅销书《智慧部落：团队如何共同变得出色》（Smart Tribes）的作者克里斯汀·科马福德（Christine Comaford）也表示，90% 的人类行为和决策是由我们的情感驱动的。

不理解情感在决策中的作用，会让我们作为领导者、销售人员甚至父母的生活变得极其令人沮丧。畅销书作者戴尔·卡耐基（Dale Carnegie）说得好：“与人打交道时，让我们记住，我们不是在与逻辑生物打交道。我们是在与情感生物打交道。”

所有研究都表明，讲故事在与他人建立情感连接时至关重要。

> 如果你希望更有效地影响和沟通，你必须平衡逻辑（事实）与情感（故事）。

### 帮助受众理解信息

如果你还需要更多关于故事神奇力量的说服，让我们再看看保罗·扎克关于催产素和讲故事的研究。他的研究还表明，当我们听一个带有紧张感的故事时，大脑会产生压力激素皮质醇，这有助于我们集中注意力。

### — 真实沟通 —

扎克的研究表明，能引发情感的以人物为主导的故事，能更好地理解故事中的关键信息。更重要的是，听众几周后能更好地回忆起主要观点。

关键在于 *以人物为主导* 的故事——即关于人，而非公司的故事。这就是为什么案例研究和商业示例在与受众建立情感连接，以及让他们记住你的信息方面，并不是很好的故事。案例研究可以是宝贵的信息来源，但它们仍然是基于逻辑的。

故事影响我们的决策过程，同时帮助我们与讲故事的人建立连接，并增加对他们的信任。

> *引发情感的故事比逻辑信息更能帮助我们有效地记住信息。*

### 让你的故事个性化

那么，我们如何在工作中实际使用故事呢？

最简单的方法是想一个你生活中的个人故事，并将其与商业情境联系起来。² 你可以讲述你小时候的故事，或者关于你的孩子、朋友或父母的故事。

举个例子，以下是来自 NAB 转型总经理安·贝内特（Anne Bennett）的故事。几年前我与安合作，当时她想与团队分享一个关于诚信和做正确事情的个人故事。

这是她分享的故事：

> “我父亲年轻时是一名精英游泳运动员，十六岁时，他参加了仰泳决赛，希望入选 1964 年东京奥运会澳大利亚代表队。

² 我之前的著作《工作中的故事》（*Stories for Work*）中有 50 多个例子，展示了人们如何在商业中分享个人故事并取得良好效果。

父亲一出发就领先于所有对手，  
但当他接近 50 米处的转身时，他没碰到池壁。  
知道自己没碰到，他游回去，碰了碰池壁，  
然后继续比赛。

那天父亲获得第七名，未能入选奥运代表队。比赛结束后，裁判告诉他，他们没看到他没碰壁（这在技术记录之前很久），如果他继续游下去，他本会获得第一名，并有可能打破世界纪录。

父亲总是告诉我和我妹妹，他从未后悔那个瞬间的决定，尽管裁判没看到没碰壁，但他知道自己没碰到，也知道游回去是正确的做法。对我来说，做正确的事是父亲那天展现给我的一生功课，以及他所展现的诚信。

当我想到诚信，我想到我的父亲。在商业中，我们常常面临需要决定是否回去碰壁的情况。在这些时刻，我总是会问自己，我父亲会怎么做。

安在这个故事中运用了类比的力量。也就是说，她讲述了父亲做了什么，然后将其与我们在商业情境中能做的事情联系起来。最好的故事使用这种可引起共鸣的类比和隐喻来连接和激励，并阐述更大的观点。安的故事向她的团队表明，诚信对她很重要。她的听众显而易见的认识是，这是她成长的方式。诚信是她父母对她的期望，也是她对自己和她所领导的人的期望。

安说，分享故事后，她注意到团队行为发生了变化。当需要做出决定时，她常常听到团队中的一人说：“这是我们回去碰壁的时刻”，这不仅做出了正确的决定，还让整个团队都支持它。

> 当你分享个人故事时，你会与受众建立更真实的连接。





### Write it out: Stories in written communication

Stories don’t always have to be told verbally—in face-to-face meetings, for example, or presentations. They can also be included in written formats.

My next example comes from a real estate agent, and her newsletter no less. When you hear ‘newsletter from a real estate agent’, I am guessing you’re not filled with excitement or anticipation of what it could contain. However, this one is different.

This is a newsletter from Erin Rush, a real estate agent at Harcourts in Wellington, New Zealand, that I was alerted to by a client.

> > Good grief my husband is watching George and Mildred on the telly. It’s so bad it’s good. The episode he’s watching is where George attempts to sell their house without telling Mildred. There are the expected misunderstandings, double entendres, and the eventual reveal. The great line when Mildred cottons on and asks George if he’d like to be buried or burned. Gold.

> > It’s very clear today—if there is more than one name on the title of a property, it’s in a trust or a company—all owners have to sign both the agency agreement and the sale and purchase agreement. Perhaps back then (1976) it would not have been common to have the missus on the title. I must ask my mum and dad.

> > In 1976 I was six, the youngest of four kids. Mum was 28. She and Dad had gotten busy. I remember her surprise 30th because we got dressed in our church clothes and pretended nothing was happening when she got back from netball practice. Mum said she thought something was up when we were sitting unnaturally still in the lounge and not hitting each other. Then she went into the kitchen and all the friends yelled ‘surprise’ and there was a huge party, which I don’t remember, except for me and my 9-year-old brother getting up in the morning and sneaking the dirty fag-ends out of the ashtrays and having a quick puff, and Mrs O’Malley wearing an orange kaftan remarkably like that worn by Mildred in tonight’s episode.

> > Which brings me nicely to the 70s two bedroom townhouse I have for sale today in Maupuia. A great buy for a first home buyer, retiree, investor or those looking for a small home. I wonder how many kaftans have graced that home? Have a wonderful week and do let me know if there’s anything you see here you’d like to know more about, and if I can show you through. Cheers! Erin

I love how this story leads you in and then includes just a subtle sell at the end. And it’s written as if it’s being told verbally, using a conversational tone (and even admitting some perhaps questionable childhood decisions). How refreshing, different and real is that?

When I contacted Erin about her newsletter, she told me she’d read other newsletters where there was no connection—‘you might as well be looking at a newspaper ad’. So she focused on finding that connection. As she said, her ‘job is actually about people, not houses. And people buy from people they like’. Perfect!

I know from experience that I receive the most feedback and interaction from my audience when I share personal stories in my weekly blogs. I have also heard, time and time again, the same result from others who do the same. (Erin even has people stay on her newsletter list, long after they have bought a house, just for a laugh. Though you can bet who they’ll be contacting when they next want to move …)

Stories can be shared in so many different forms. You can share stories in a one-on-one situation or on stage speaking to 1000 people. You can use them internally with your team, with people you mentor, with your kids and with your clients.

They can also be in a verbal or written format, shared face to face, over teleconference, in videos and in blogs and newsletters.

### The real point is to use them!

Both stories and facts have a powerful part to play when it comes to real and authentic communication, so develop a suite of stories you can use, and prepare and practise them. (See chapter 8 for tips on delivering your stories with confidence and impact. Also see my previous book, Stories for Work, for help with developing a range of stories for different contexts and purposes.)

Remember, stories are like fish ’n’ chips—you can’t have one without the other. (Well, you can but obviously it is not as good.)

To create real connection with your team, your peers and your stakeholders, you need to build trust and credibility—fast. Sharing personal stories will help you do just that.

### Get REAL now

- Balance your facts and stats with stories and examples.
- Learn how to bring in emotion instead of just logic when trying to communicate in a more engaging way.
- Use stories to build trust, and to influence and persuade others.
- Use stories if you want your message to be better remembered and recalled.
- Share stories verbally and in written format.
