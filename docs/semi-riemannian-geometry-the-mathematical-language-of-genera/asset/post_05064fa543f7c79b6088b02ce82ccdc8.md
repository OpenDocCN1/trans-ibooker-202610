
In this section, we provide modern versions of several of the classical theorems of integral vector calculus.

Let \(\lambda(t) : [a,b] \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}^3_0\) be a smooth curve, and let \(X = (\alpha^1,\alpha^2,\alpha^3)\) be a vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^3_0)\). The classical line integral of \(X\) is denoted by \(\int_C X \cdot ds\) (among other notations) and defined by

\[
\int_C X \cdot ds = \int_a^b \langle X_{\lambda(t)}, d\lambda/dt(t) \rangle\, dt,
\]

where \(C\) is the image of \(\lambda\). The next result shows that, other than a switch from a vector field to an equivalent covector field, the classical line integral and the line integral introduced in Section 16.8 are essentially the same.

### Theorem 21.2.1 (Classical Line Integral)

Continuing with the above setup, let \(\omega\) be the covector field in \(\mathfrak{X}^*(\mathbb{R}^3_0)\) given by

\[
\omega = \alpha^1 d(x) + \alpha^2 d(y) + \alpha^3 d(z).
\]

Then

\[
\int_C X \cdot ds = \int_\lambda \omega.
\]

Proof. Let \(\lambda = (\lambda^1,\lambda^2,\lambda^3)\). We have

\[
\begin{aligned}
\omega_{\lambda(t)}(d\lambda/dt(t)) &= \alpha^1(\lambda(t)) d(x)(d\lambda/dt(t)) + \alpha^2(\lambda(t)) d(y)(d\lambda/dt(t)) + \alpha^3(\lambda(t)) d(z)(d\lambda/dt(t))\\
&= \alpha^1(\lambda(t)) d\lambda^1/dt(t) + \alpha^2(\lambda(t)) d\lambda^2/dt(t) + \alpha^3(\lambda(t)) d\lambda^3/dt(t)\\
&= \langle X_{\lambda(t)}, d\lambda/dt(t) \rangle.
\end{aligned}
\]

The result now follows from (16.8.4).

Let \(U\) be an open set in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), let \(S\) be a set in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), let \(F = (F^1,F^2,F^3) : U \longrightarrow S\) be a diffeomorphism that has compact support, and let \(X = (\alpha^1,\alpha^2,\alpha^3)\) be a vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^3_0)\). The classical surface integral of \(X\) is denoted by \(\iint_S X \cdot dA\) (among other notations) and defined by

\[
\iint_S X \cdot dA = \iint_U \langle X \circ F, \partial F/\partial r \times \partial F/\partial s \rangle\, dr\, ds,
\]

where we note that \(S\) is the image of \(F\).

### Theorem 21.2.2 (Classical Surface Integral)

Continuing with the above setup, let \(\omega\) be the form in \(\Lambda^2(\mathbb{R}^3)\) given by

\[
\omega = \alpha^1 d(y) \wedge d(z) + \alpha^2 d(z) \wedge d(x) + \alpha^3 d(x) \wedge d(y).
\]

Then

\[
\iint_S X \cdot dA = \int_S \omega.
\]

Proof. Let \(\omega\) be the covector field in \(\mathfrak{X}^*(\mathbb{R}^2)\) given by \(\omega = \alpha^1 d(r) + \alpha^2 d(s)\). By Stokes’s theorem,

\[
\int_D d(\omega) = \int_{\partial D} \omega.
\]

A computation similar to that leading to (16.1.2) gives

\[
d(\omega) = \left(\frac{\partial\alpha^2}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial\alpha^1}{\partial s}\right) d(r) \wedge d(s),
\]

and then (16.7.2) yields

\[
\int_D d(\omega) = \iint_D \left(\frac{\partial\alpha^2}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial\alpha^1}{\partial s}\right) dr\, ds.
\]

The result follows.

The setup for the next result is the same as that for Theorem 19.11.4, except that \((M,\epsilon|_M)\) is assumed to be compact, something needed to ensure certain integrals exist.

### Theorem 21.2.4 (Classical Stokes’s Theorem)

Let \((M,\epsilon|_M)\) be a compact orientable Riemannian hypersurface with boundary of \(\mathbb{R}^3_0 = (\mathbb{R}^3,\epsilon)\), let \(V\) be a unit normal vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}_\epsilon(M)\) (as given by Theorem 19.11.1), and let \(\mathcal{D}_M\) be the orientation of \(M\) induced by \(V\), so that \((M,\epsilon|_M,\mathcal{D}_M)\) is a compact oriented Riemannian hypersurface with boundary of \(\mathbb{R}^3_0\). Let \(\Omega_M\) be its volume form. Let \(W\) be the unique outward-pointing unit normal vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\partial M)\) (as given by Theorem 19.11.3), and let \(\partial_M\) be the (Stokes) orientation induced by \(W\), so that \((\partial M,\epsilon|_{\partial M},\mathcal{D}_{\partial M})\) is an oriented Riemannian hypersurface of \((M,\epsilon|_M,\mathcal{D}_M)\). Let \(\Omega_{\partial M}\) be its volume form, and let \(U = V \times W\). If \(X\) is a vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^3_0)\), then

\[
\int_M \langle \operatorname{Curl}(X), V \rangle\, \Omega_M = \int_{\partial M} \langle X, U \rangle\, \Omega_{\partial M}.
\]

Proof. By Stokes’s theorem,

\[
\int_M d(X^\flat) = \int_{\partial M} X^\flat. \tag{21.2.1}
\]

We seek alternative expressions for the integrands in (21.2.1). For the left-hand side, Theorem 19.11.2(b) and Theorem 20.5.1 give

\[
d(X^\flat) = i_{\operatorname{Curl}(X)}(\Omega_{\mathbb{R}^3_0})|_M = \langle \operatorname{Curl}(X), V \rangle\, \Omega_M, \tag{21.2.2}
\]

where \(\Omega_{\mathbb{R}^3_0}\) is the volume form on \(\mathbb{R}^3_0\). For the right-hand side, by Theorem 19.11.4(e),

\[
\Omega_{\partial M}(U) = \pm 1_{\partial M}. \tag{21.2.3}
\]

Since \(X^\flat|_{\partial M}\) is a covector field in \(\mathfrak{X}^*(\partial M) = \Lambda^1(\partial M)\), it follows from Theorem 3.3.3, Theorem 15.8.5, and Theorem 19.10.2 that there is a uniquely determined function \(f\) in \(C^\infty(\partial M)\) such that

\[
X^\flat|_{\partial M} = f\, \Omega_{\partial M}. \tag{21.2.4}
\]

We have

\[
\begin{aligned}
f &= f\, \Omega_{\partial M}(U) && [(21.2.3)]\\
&= X^\flat|_{\partial M}(U) && [(21.2.4)]\\
&= \langle X|_{\partial M}, U \rangle. && [(19.4.1)]
\end{aligned} \tag{21.2.5}
\]

Then (21.2.4) and (21.2.5) give

\[
X^\flat|_{\partial M} = \langle X|_{\partial M}, U \rangle\, \Omega_{\partial M}. \tag{21.2.6}
\]

Substituting (21.2.2) and (21.2.6) into (21.2.1) gives the result.

### Theorem 21.2.5 (Classical Divergence Theorem)

Let \((M,\mathfrak{e}|_M,\mathfrak{O}_M)\) be a compact oriented Riemannian 3-submanifold with boundary of \(\mathbb{R}^3_0 = (\mathbb{R}^3,\mathfrak{e})\), so that \((\partial M,\mathfrak{e}|_{\partial M})\) is a Riemannian hypersurface of \((M,\mathfrak{e}|_M,\mathfrak{O}_M)\), and let \(X\) be a vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}(M)\). Let \(V\) be the unique outward-pointing unit normal vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\partial M)\) (as given by Theorem 19.11.3), and let \(\partial M\) have the orientation \(\mathfrak{O}_{\partial M}\) induced by \(V\), so that \((\partial M,\mathfrak{e}|_{\partial M},\mathfrak{O}_{\partial M})\) is an oriented Riemannian hypersurface of \((M,\mathfrak{e}|_M,\mathfrak{O}_M)\). Let \(\Omega_{\partial M}\) be its volume form. Then

\[
\int_M \operatorname{Div}(X)\, \Omega_M = \int_{\partial M} \langle X, V \rangle\, \Omega_{\partial M}.
\]

Proof. We have

\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_M \operatorname{Div}(X)\, \Omega_M &= \int_{\partial M} i_X(\Omega_M) && [\text{Th 20.4.5}]\\
&= \int_{\partial M} \langle X, V \rangle\, \Omega_{\partial M}. && [\text{Th 19.11.2(b)}]
\end{aligned}
\]

## Chapter 22 Applications to Physics

### 22.1 Linear Isometries on Lorentz Vector Spaces

Let \((V,\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{O})\) be an oriented Lorentz vector space, and let \(\mathcal{E} = (e_1,\dots,e_m)\) be an orthonormal basis for \(V\) that is positively oriented with respect to \(\mathfrak{O}\). We denote by \(\mathsf{L}_m\) the set of linear isometries on \(V\):

\[
\mathsf{L}_m = \{A \in \operatorname{Lin}(V,V) : A \text{ a linear isometry}\}
\]

where we recall that \(\operatorname{Lin}(V,V)\) is the vector space of linear maps from \(V\) to \(V\).

### Theorem 22.1.1

\(\mathsf{L}_m\) is a group (under composition of linear maps), called the *Lorentz group on \(V\)*.

Proof. Let \(A\) be a map in \(\mathsf{L}_m\). It follows from Theorem 1.1.9 and Theorem 4.4.1(a) that the inverse \(A^{-1}\) exists and is in \(\operatorname{Lin}(V,V)\). Let \(v,w\) be vectors in \(V\). Then

\[
\langle v,w \rangle = \langle A(A^{-1}(v)), A(A^{-1}(w)) \rangle = \langle A^{-1}(v), A^{-1}(w) \rangle,
\]

so \(A^{-1}\) is in \(\mathsf{L}_m\). The rest of the verification that \(\mathsf{L}_m\) is a group is straightforward.

Let \(A\) be a map in \(\operatorname{Lin}(V,V)\). For brevity, we denote \([A]_{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{E}}\) by \([A]\), and likewise omit \(\mathcal{E}\) from the notation for other matrices. Corresponding to (4.4.1) and (4.4.2), we have

\[
[A] = \begin{bmatrix} [A]_1^1 & [A]_1^2 \\ [A]_2^1 & \gamma \end{bmatrix}
\]

and

\[
[A]^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} ([A]_1^1)^\mathrm{T} & -([A]_1^2)^\mathrm{T} \\ -([A]_2^1)^\mathrm{T} & \gamma \end{bmatrix},
\]

respectively, where

\[
[A]_1^1 = \begin{bmatrix} a_1^1 & \cdots & a_{m-1}^1 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_1^{m-1} & \cdots & a_{m-1}^{m-1} \end{bmatrix},
\]

\[
[A]_2^1 = \begin{bmatrix} a_m^1 \\ \vdots \\ a_m^{m-1} \end{bmatrix},
\]

and

\[
[A]_1^2 = [a_1^m \cdots a_{m-1}^m].
\]

The ring isomorphism given by Theorem 2.2.3(b) restricts to a (multiplicative) group isomorphism between \(\mathsf{L}_m\) and a subgroup of the general linear group \(\operatorname{GL}(m) \subset \operatorname{Mult}_{m\times m}\). In what follows, but without further mention, this group isomorphism is used to prove assertions about the group structure of \(\mathsf{L}_m\). In particular, we rely on the following: a subset of \(\mathsf{L}_m\) is a subgroup if and only if the corresponding subset of \(\operatorname{GL}(m)\) is a subgroup.

### Theorem 22.1.2

If \(A\) is a map in \(\mathsf{L}_m\), then:

- (a) \([A]_1^1([A]_1^1)^T = I_{m-1} + [A]_2^1([A]_2^1)^T = I_{m-1} + ([A]_1^2)^T [A]_1^2\).
- (b) \(\gamma[A]_2^1 = [A]_1^1([A]_1^2)^T\).
- (c) \(\gamma[A]_1^2 = ([A]_2^1)^T [A]_1^1\).
- (d) \(\gamma^2 = 1 + ([A]_2^1)^T [A]_2^1 = 1 + [A]_1^2([A]_1^2)^T\).

Proof. (a): This follows from parts (a) and (d) of Theorem 4.4.6.
(b): This follows from Theorem 4.4.6(b).
(c): This follows from Theorem 4.4.6(e).
(d): This follows from parts (c) and (f) of Theorem 4.4.6.
