
## 2.4 Determinant of Matrices

### Theorem 2.4.5

If \(P\) and \(Q\) are matrices in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\), then:

- (a) \(\det(PQ)=\det(P)\det(Q)\).
- (b) If \(P\) is invertible, then \(\det(P^{-1})=\det(P)^{-1}\).
- (c) \(\det(P^{\mathsf T})=\det(P)\).

**Proof.** (a): Let us define a function \(\Delta:(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times 1})^m\to\mathbb R\) by

\[
\Delta(R_1,\ldots,R_m)=\det([PR_1\ \cdots\ PR_m])
\]

for all matrices \(R_1,\ldots,R_m\) in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times 1}\). It is easily shown that \(\Delta\) is a determinant function. We have

\[
\begin{aligned}
\det([PQ_{(1)}\ \cdots\ PQ_{(m)}])
&=\Delta(Q_{(1)},\ldots,Q_{(m)})\\
&=\Delta(E_1,\ldots,E_m)\det([Q_{(1)}\ \cdots\ Q_{(m)}]) \qquad [(2.4.1),\text{ Th 2.4.3}]\\
&=\det(P)\det(Q).
\end{aligned}
\]

(b): We have from (2.4.1) and part (a) that

\[
1=\det(I_m)=\det(PP^{-1})=\det(P)\det(P^{-1}),
\]

from which the result follows.

(c): Let \(P^{\mathsf T}=[p_j^i]^{\mathsf T}=[q_j^i]\). Then

\[
\det(P^{\mathsf T})
=\sum_{\sigma\in S_m}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,q_{\sigma(1)}^1\cdots q_{\sigma(m)}^m
=\sum_{\sigma\in S_m}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,p_1^{\sigma(1)}\cdots p_m^{\sigma(m)}
=\det(P),
\]

where the first and last equalities follow from (2.4.1), and the second equality from the observation that \(q_{\sigma(i)}^i=p_i^{\sigma(i)}\) for all permutations \(\sigma\) in \(S_m\).

Let \(P\) be a matrix in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\). The \(ij\)-th cofactor of \(P\) is defined by

\[
c_j^i=(-1)^{i+j}\det\left(P^{(1,\ldots,\hat i,\ldots,m)}_{(1,\ldots,\hat j,\ldots,m)}\right),
\]

where the hat indicates that an expression is omitted. Thus, \(P^{(1,\ldots,\hat i,\ldots,m)}_{(1,\ldots,\hat j,\ldots,m)}\) is the matrix in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{(m-1)\times(m-1)}\) obtained by deleting the \(i\)th row and \(j\)th column of \(P\). The adjugate of \(P\) is the matrix in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\) defined by

\[
\operatorname{adj}(P)=[c_j^i]^{\mathsf T}.
\]

### Theorem 2.4.6 (Column Expansion of Determinant)

If \(P=[p_j^i]\) is a matrix in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\) and \(1\le j\le m\), then

\[
\det(P)=\sum_i c_j^i p_j^i,
\]

which is called the expansion of \(\det(P)\) along the \(j\)th column of \(P\).

**Proof.** We have

\[
P=\begin{bmatrix} P_{(1,\ldots,j-1)} & \sum_i p_j^i E_i & P_{(j+1,\ldots,m)} \end{bmatrix}
\]

hence

\[
\det(P)=\sum_i p_j^i\det\left(\begin{bmatrix} P_{(1,\ldots,j-1)} & E_i & P_{(j+1,\ldots,m)} \end{bmatrix}\right).
\]

We also have

\[
\begin{aligned}
\det\left(\begin{bmatrix} P_{(1,\ldots,j-1)} & E_i & P_{(j+1,\ldots,m)} \end{bmatrix}\right)
&=\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}
P^{(1,\ldots,i-1)}_{(1,\ldots,j-1)} & O_{(i-1)\times1} & P^{(1,\ldots,i-1)}_{(j+1,\ldots,m)}\\
P^{(i)}_{(1,\ldots,j-1)} & 1 & P^{(i)}_{(j+1,\ldots,m)}\\
P^{(i+1,\ldots,m)}_{(1,\ldots,j-1)} & O_{(m-i)\times1} & P^{(i+1,\ldots,m)}_{(j+1,\ldots,m)}
\end{bmatrix}\right)\\
&=(-1)^{(i-1)+(j-1)}\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}
1 & P^{(i)}_{(1,\ldots,j-1)} & P^{(i)}_{(j+1,\ldots,m)}\\
O_{(i-1)\times1} & P^{(1,\ldots,i-1)}_{(1,\ldots,j-1)} & P^{(1,\ldots,i-1)}_{(j+1,\ldots,m)}\\
O_{(m-i)\times1} & P^{(i+1,\ldots,m)}_{(1,\ldots,j-1)} & P^{(i+1,\ldots,m)}_{(j+1,\ldots,m)}
\end{bmatrix}\right)\\
&=(-1)^{i+j}\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}
1 & P^{(i)}_{(1,\ldots,\hat j,\ldots,m)}\\
O_{(m-1)\times1} & P^{(1,\ldots,\hat i,\ldots,m)}_{(1,\ldots,\hat j,\ldots,m)}
\end{bmatrix}\right)\\
&=(-1)^{i+j}\det\left(P^{(1,\ldots,\hat i,\ldots,m)}_{(1,\ldots,\hat j,\ldots,m)}\right)=c_j^i,
\end{aligned}
\]

where the second equality is obtained by switching \(i-1\) adjacent rows, and then switching \(j-1\) adjacent columns, and where the fourth equality follows from (2.4.1). Combining the above identities gives the result.

### Theorem 2.4.7

If \(P\) is a matrix in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\), then

\[
P\operatorname{adj}(P)=\det(P)I_m.
\]

**Proof.** Let \(P=[p_j^i]\). For given integers \(1\le k,l\le m\), let \(Q=[q_j^i]\) be the matrix in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\) obtained by replacing the \(k\)-th column of \(P\) with the \(l\)-th column of \(P\); that is,

\[
Q=[P_{(1)}\ \cdots\ P_{(k-1)}\ P_{(l)}\ P_{(k+1)}\ \cdots\ P_{(m)}].
\]

Let \(\operatorname{adj}(P)=[c_j^i]^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\operatorname{adj}(Q)=[d_j^i]^{\mathsf T}\). By Theorem 2.4.6,

\[
\sum_i c_k^i p_k^i=\det(P).
\]

If \(k\ne l\), then two columns of \(Q\) are equal and

\[
Q^{(1,\ldots,\hat i,\ldots,m)}_{(1,\ldots,\hat k,\ldots,m)}=P^{(1,\ldots,\hat i,\ldots,m)}_{(1,\ldots,\hat k,\ldots,m)}.
\]

It follows from Theorems 2.4.2 and 2.4.6 that

\[
\sum_i c_k^i p_l^i=\sum_i d_k^i q_l^i=\det(Q)=0.
\]

Thus,

\[
\sum_i c_k^i p_l^i=\det(P)\,\delta_{kl}
\]

for \(k,l=1,\ldots,m\), where \(\delta_{kl}\) is Kronecker’s delta. Then \([c_j^i][p_j^i]^{\mathsf T}=\det(P)I_m\). Taking transposes gives the result.

### Theorem 2.4.8

If \(P\) is a matrix in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\), then the following are equivalent:

- (a) \(P\) is invertible.
- (b) \(\det(P)\ne 0\).
- (c) \(\operatorname{rank}(P)=m\).

If any of the above equivalent conditions is satisfied, then

\[
P^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det(P)}\operatorname{adj}(P).
\]

**Proof.** (a) \(\Rightarrow\) (b): By Theorem 2.4.5(b), \(\det(P)\det(P^{-1})=1\), hence \(\det(P)\ne 0\).

(b) \(\Rightarrow\) (c): Since \(\det([P_{(1)}\ \cdots\ P_{(m)}])=\det(P)\ne0\), it follows from Theorem 2.4.2 that \(P_{(1)},\ldots,P_{(m)}\) are linearly independent, so

\[
\operatorname{rank}(P)=\operatorname{rank}([P_{(1)}\ \cdots\ P_{(m)}])=m.
\]

(c) \(\Rightarrow\) (a): Since \(\operatorname{rank}(P)=m\), \(\mathscr P=(P_{(1)},\ldots,P_{(m)})\) is a basis for \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times1}\). Let \(Q=[[E_1]_{\mathscr P}\ \cdots\ [E_m]_{\mathscr P}]\). By definition, \(E_j=P[E_j]_{\mathscr P}\) for \(j=1,\ldots,m\), hence

\[
I_m=[E_1\ \cdots\ E_m]=PQ,
\]

so \(Q=P^{-1}\). The final assertion follows from Theorem 2.4.7.

### Theorem 2.4.9

The matrices \(P_1,\ldots,P_m\) in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times1}\) are linearly independent if and only if \(\det([P_1\ \cdots\ P_m])\ne0\).

**Proof.** We have

\[
\begin{aligned}
P_1,\ldots,P_m\text{ are linearly independent}
&\Longleftrightarrow \operatorname{rank}([P_1\ \cdots\ P_m])=m\\
&\Longleftrightarrow \det([P_1\ \cdots\ P_m])\ne0. \qquad [\text{Th 2.4.8}]
\end{aligned}
\]

The next result is not usually included in an overview of determinants, but it will prove invaluable later on.

### Theorem 2.4.10 (Cauchy–Binet Identity)

Let \(1\le k\le m\) be integers.

- (a) If \(P\) and \(Q\) are matrices in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times k}\), then

\[
\det(P^{\mathsf T}Q)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal I_{k,m}}\det(P^I)\det(Q^I).
\]

- (b) If \(R\) and \(S\) are matrices in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times m}\), then

\[
\det(RS^{\mathsf T})=\sum_{J\in\mathcal I_{k,m}}\det(R_J)\det(S_J).
\]

**Proof.** (a): Let \(Q=[q_j^i]\), so that

\[
Q=[Q_{(1)}\ \cdots\ Q_{(k)}],\qquad Q_{(j)}=[q_j^1\ \cdots\ q_j^m]^{\mathsf T},
\]

and

\[
P^{\mathsf T}Q_{(j)}=\sum_i q_j^i(P^{\mathsf T})_{(i)}
\]

for \(j=1,\ldots,k\). We have

\[
\begin{aligned}
\det(P^{\mathsf T}Q)
&=\det\left([P^{\mathsf T}Q_{(1)}\ \cdots\ P^{\mathsf T}Q_{(k)}]\right)\\
&=\det\left(\left[\sum_{i_1}q_1^{i_1}(P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_1)}\ \cdots\ \sum_{i_k}q_k^{i_k}(P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_k)}\right]\right)\\
&=\sum_{1\le i_1,\ldots,i_k\le m}q_1^{i_1}\cdots q_k^{i_k}\det\left([(P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_1)}\ \cdots\ (P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_k)}]\right)\\
&=\sum_{\substack{1\le i_1,\ldots,i_k\le m\\ i_1,\ldots,i_k\text{ distinct}}}q_1^{i_1}\cdots q_k^{i_k}\det\left([(P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_1)}\ \cdots\ (P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_k)}]\right)\\
&=\sum_{1\le i_1<\cdots<i_k\le m}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in S_k}q_1^{i_{\sigma(1)}}\cdots q_k^{i_{\sigma(k)}}\det\left([(P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_{\sigma(1)})}\ \cdots\ (P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_{\sigma(k)})}]\right)\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

where the third equality follows from Theorem 2.4.2. The term in large parentheses in the last row of the preceding display can be expressed as

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{\sigma\in S_k}q_1^{i_{\sigma(1)}}\cdots q_k^{i_{\sigma(k)}}\det\left([(P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_{\sigma(1)})}\ \cdots\ (P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_{\sigma(k)})}]\right)\\
&=\sum_{\sigma\in S_k}q_1^{i_{\sigma(1)}}\cdots q_k^{i_{\sigma(k)}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\det\left([(P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_1)}\ \cdots\ (P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_k)}]\right)\\
&=\det\left([(P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_1)}\ \cdots\ (P^{\mathsf T})_{(i_k)}]\right)\left(\sum_{\sigma\in S_k}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)q_1^{i_{\sigma(1)}}\cdots q_k^{i_{\sigma(k)}}\right)\\
&=\det\left(P^{\mathsf T}_{(i_1,\ldots,i_k)}\right)\det\left(Q^{(i_1,\ldots,i_k)}\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus,

\[
\det(P^{\mathsf T}Q)=\sum_{1\le i_1<\cdots<i_k\le m}\det\left(P^{\mathsf T}_{(i_1,\ldots,i_k)}\right)\det\left(Q^{(i_1,\ldots,i_k)}\right),
\]

which is the classical version of the Cauchy–Binet identity.

### Theorem 2.4.12

If \(V\) is a vector space, and \(\mathcal H\) and \(\mathcal F\) are bases for \(V\), then

\[
\det([\operatorname{id}_V]_{\mathcal H}^{\mathcal F})\ne 0.
\]

**Proof.** We have

\[
1=\det([\operatorname{id}_V]_{\mathcal H}^{\mathcal H}) \qquad [(2.4.2)]
\]

\[
=\det([\operatorname{id}_V]_{\mathcal F}^{\mathcal H})\det([\operatorname{id}_V]_{\mathcal H}^{\mathcal F}) \qquad [\text{Th 2.2.2(c), Th 2.4.5(a)}],
\]

from which the result follows.

It was remarked in connection with Theorem 2.2.3 that \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\) is a ring under the usual operations of matrix addition and matrix multiplication, hence it is a group under matrix addition. But \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\) is clearly not a group under matrix multiplication because not all matrices have an inverse. However, \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\) contains a number of matrix groups. The largest of these is the general linear group, consisting of all invertible matrices:

\[
GL(m)=\{P\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}:\det(P)\ne0\},
\]

where the characterization using determinants follows from Theorem 2.4.8.

We say that a matrix \(P\) in \(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\) is orthogonal if \(P^{\mathsf T}P=I_m\). If so, then \(P^{\mathsf T}=P^{-1}\), hence \(PP^{\mathsf T}=PP^{-1}=I_m\). Thus, \(P^{\mathsf T}P=I_m\) if and only if \(PP^{\mathsf T}=I_m\). The orthogonal group is the subgroup of \(GL(m)\) consisting of all orthogonal matrices:

\[
O(m)=\{P\in GL(m):P^{\mathsf T}P=I_m\}.
\]

For a matrix \(P\) in \(O(m)\), we have

\[
1=\det(I_m)=\det(P^{\mathsf T}P)=\det(P^{\mathsf T})\det(P)=\det(P)^2,
\]

hence \(\det(P)=\pm1\). The special orthogonal group is the subgroup of \(O(m)\) consisting of all matrices with determinant equal to \(1\):

\[
SO(m)=\{P\in O(m):\det(P)=1\}.
\]

## 2.5 Trace and Determinant of Linear Maps

The trace and determinant of a matrix were defined in Section 2.1 and Section 2.4, respectively. We now extend these concepts to linear maps.

Let \(V\) be a vector space, let \(\mathcal H\) be a basis for \(V\), and let \(A:V\longrightarrow V\) be a linear map. The trace of \(A\) is defined by

\[
\operatorname{tr}(A)=\operatorname{tr}([A]_{\mathcal H}^{\mathcal H}), \qquad (2.5.1)
\]

and the determinant of \(A\) by

\[
\det(A)=\det([A]_{\mathcal H}^{\mathcal H}). \qquad (2.5.2)
\]
