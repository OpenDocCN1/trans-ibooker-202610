$$\delta(i,j)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }i=j\\0&\text{if }i\ne j.\end{cases}$$

We henceforth denote $\delta(i,j)$ by either of the equivalent expressions $\delta_{ij}$ or $\delta^i_j$, the choice depending on the context.

For an integer $1\le k\le n$, we say that a $k$-tuple $(i_1,\ldots,i_k)$ of distinct elements of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ is a **multi-index** if $i_1<\cdots<i_k$. The set of multi-indices on $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ with $k$ elements is denoted by $\mathcal I_{k,n}$, and a typical multi-index $I$ in $\mathcal I_{k,n}$ is denoted by $I=(i_1,\ldots,i_k)$. For example,

$$\mathcal I_{3,4}=\{(1,2,3),(1,2,4),(2,3,4)\}.$$

Evidently, $\mathcal I_{k,n}$ has $\binom{n}{k}$ elements, where $\binom{n}{k}$ is a binomial coefficient. The **complement** of the multi-index $I=(i_1,\ldots,i_k)$ in $\mathcal I_{k,n}$ is the multi-index $I^c=(i_{k+1},\ldots,i_n)$ in $\mathcal I_{n-k,n}$ where

$$\{i_{k+1},\ldots,i_n\}=\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{i_1,\ldots,i_k\}.$$

In particular, the complement of the multi-index $(i)$ in $\mathcal I_{1,n}$ is the multi-index

$$(i)^c=(1,\ldots,\widehat{i},\ldots,n)=(1,\ldots,i-1,i+1,\ldots,n)$$

in $\mathcal I_{n-1,n}$, for $i=1,\ldots,n$, where $\widehat{\ }$ indicates that an expression is omitted. To illustrate, for $(2)$ in $\mathcal I_{1,6}$ and $(1,2,3,6)$ in $\mathcal I_{4,6}$, we have the complements $(2)^c=(1,3,4,5,6)$ in $\mathcal I_{5,6}$ and $(1,2,3,6)^c=(4,5)$ in $\mathcal I_{2,6}$.

### B.1  Groups

A group is a pair $(G, \circ)$, where $G$ is a set and $\circ: G \times G \to G$ is a map, called **multiplication**, such that for all $f,g,h$ in $G$.

- [G1] Multiplication is associative:
  
  $$f \circ (g \circ h) = (f \circ g) \circ h.$$

- [G2] There is an element $\operatorname{id}_G$ in $G$, called the **identity**, such that
  
  $$\operatorname{id}_G \circ g = g \circ \operatorname{id}_G = g.$$

- [G3] There is an element $g^{-1}$ in $G$, called the **inverse** of $g$, such that
  
  $$g \circ g^{-1} = g^{-1} \circ g = \operatorname{id}_G.$$

Usually $g \circ h$ is abbreviated to $gh$. It is easily shown that the identity element and the inverse of an element are unique. It is standard practice to refer to $G$ as a group, leaving the multiplication map understood. We say that $G$ is **commutative** if multiplication is commutative; that is, $gh = hg$ for all $g,h$ in $G$. In that situation, it is usual to employ additive notation, with the group denoted by $(G,+)$, the identity by $0_G$ or $0$, and the inverse of $g$ by $-g$.

A subset $G'$ of $G$ is said to be a **subgroup** of $G$ if: (i) $G'$ is a group in its own right (when multiplication on $G$ is restricted to $G'$), and (ii) $G'$ has the same identity element as $G$. We say that a group is **finite** if it is finite as a set.

> **Theorem B.1.1.** If $\{G_\alpha : \alpha \in A\}$ is a collection of subgroups of $G$, then $\bigcap_{\alpha\in A} G_\alpha$ is a subgroup of $G$.

*Proof.* Straightforward.

Let G and H be groups. A map F : G ⟶ H is said to be a group homomorphism if F(gh) = F(g)F(h) for all g, h in G. We say that F is a group isomorphism, and that G and H are isomorphic, if F is bijective.

### Theorem B.1.2. If G and H are groups and F : G ⟶ H is a group isomorphism, then F⁻¹ : H ⟶ G is a group isomorphism.

Proof. The proof is analogous to that of Theorem 1.1.9.

### B.2 Permutation Groups

Let m be a positive integer, and let Sₘ be the set of bijective functions on {1,2,…,m}. In the present context, each element of Sₘ is referred to as a permutation. We make Sₘ into a group, called the symmetric group on {1,2,…,m}, by defining group multiplication to be composition of functions. The identity element of Sₘ is denoted by id. ```

This makes it clear that card(Sₘ) = m!

(a₁ a₂ a₃ a₄ … aₖ₋₁ aₖ)⁻¹ = (a₁ aₖ aₖ₋₁ … a₄ a₃ a₂).  (B.2.4)

It needs to be emphasized that since composition of functions proceeds from right to left, so does multiplication of permutations, including multiplication of transpositions. For example, (1 2)(2 3) sends 1 to 2, and 2 to 3, and 3 to 2 to 1, so that (1 2)(2 3) = (1 2 3).

### **Example B.2.1.** Consider the permutation

$\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 4 & 5 & 1 & 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$  (B.2.5)

Although there are no cycles of length 1 in this example, it is conventional to suppress such cycles in a cycle decomposition. Since the cycles in a cycle decomposition are disjoint, they commute, so we could equally write $\sigma = (2\ 5)(1\ 4\ 3)$. The inverse of $\sigma$ is found simply by interchanging the rows of (B.2.5) and reordering columns to obtain

$\sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 4 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$

$\sigma^{-1} = (1\ 4\ 3)^{-1}(2\ 5)^{-1} = (1\ 3\ 4)(2\ 5)$.

$\sigma^{-1} = (1\ 3)(3\ 4)(2\ 5) = (1\ 4)(1\ 3)(2\ 5)$.

Let $x_1,\ldots,x_m$ be indeterminates, and consider the multinomial

```
$$\Delta=\prod_{1\le i<j\le m}(x_i-x_j).$$
```

For each $\sigma\in S_m$, let

```
$$\sigma(\Delta)=\prod_{1\le i<j\le m}(x_{\sigma(i)}-x_{\sigma(j)}).$$
```

The **sign** of $\sigma$ is defined by

```
$$\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=\frac{\sigma(\Delta)}{\Delta}=\prod_{1\le i<j\le m}\frac{x_{\sigma(i)}-x_{\sigma(j)}}{x_i-x_j}.$$
```

Evidently, $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)$ equals $1$ or $-1$.

### **Theorem B.2.2.** If $\sigma,\rho$ are permutations in $S_m$, then:

- (a) $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\rho)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{sgn}(\rho)$.  
- (b) $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma^{-1})$.  
- (c) $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\rho\sigma^{-1})=\operatorname{sgn}(\rho)$.

*Proof.* (a): Since $\rho(\Delta)=\pm\Delta$, we have

```
$$\frac{(\sigma\rho)(\Delta)}{\rho(\Delta)}=\frac{\sigma(\rho(\Delta))}{\rho(\Delta)}=\frac{\sigma(\pm\Delta)}{\pm\Delta}=\frac{\pm\sigma(\Delta)}{\pm\Delta}=\frac{\sigma(\Delta)}{\Delta}=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma),$$
```

hence

```
$$\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\rho)=\frac{(\sigma\rho)(\Delta)}{\Delta}=\frac{(\sigma\rho)(\Delta)}{\rho(\Delta)}\frac{\rho(\Delta)}{\Delta}=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{sgn}(\rho).$$
```
