In most of the computations that follow, the indices are sequential, thereby avoiding this issue.

**Theorem 7.1.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space, let $\sigma,\rho$ be permutations in $S_s$, and let $\mathcal A$ be a tensor in $T_s^0(V)$. Then

$$(\sigma\rho)(\mathcal A)=\sigma(\rho(\mathcal A)).$$

*Proof.* Let $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ be vectors in $V$, and let $(w_1,\ldots,w_s)=(v_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(s)})$. Then

$$(\sigma\rho)(\mathcal A)(v_1,\ldots,v_s)=\mathcal A(v_{(\sigma\rho)(1)},\ldots,v_{(\sigma\rho)(s)})=\mathcal A(v_{\sigma(\rho(1))},\ldots,v_{\sigma(\rho(s))})$$
$$=\mathcal A(w_{\rho(1)},\ldots,w_{\rho(s)})=\rho(\mathcal A)(w_1,\ldots,w_s)$$
$$=\rho(\mathcal A)(v_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(s)})=\sigma(\rho(\mathcal A))(v_1,\ldots,v_s).$$

Since $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ were arbitrary, the result follows.

We say that a tensor $\mathcal A$ in $T_s^0(V)$ is **symmetric** if $\sigma(\mathcal A)=\mathcal A$ for all permutations $\sigma$ in $S_s$; that is,

$$\mathcal A(v_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(s)})=\mathcal A(v_1,\ldots,v_s)$$

for all vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ in $V$. We denote the set of symmetric tensors in $T_s^0(V)$ by $\Sigma_s(V)$. It is easily shown that $\Sigma_s(V)$ is subspace of $T_s^0(V)$.

A tensor $\mathcal B$ in $T_s^0(V)$ is said to be **alternating** if $\tau(\mathcal B)=-\mathcal B$ for all transpositions $\tau$ in $S_s$, or equivalently, if

$$\mathcal B(v_1,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_s)=-\mathcal B(v_1,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_s)$$

for all vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ in $V$ for all $1\le i<j\le s$. The set of alternating tensors in $T_s^0(V)$ is denoted by $\Lambda^s(V)$. It is readily demonstrated that $\Lambda^s(V)$ is a subspace of $T_s^0(V)$. An element of $\Lambda^s(V)$ is called an **s-covector** or **multicovector**. When $s=1$, the alternating criterion is vacuous, and a 1-covector is simply a covector. Thus,

$$\Lambda^1(V)=V^*.$$ \tag{7.1.2}

Since $\Lambda^s(V)$ is a subspace of $T_s^0(V)$, the **zero multivector** in $\Lambda^s(V)$ is precisely the zero tensor in $T_s^0(V)$. A multivector in $\Lambda^s(V)$ is **nonzero** when it is nonzero as a tensor in $T_s^0(V)$. To be consistent with (5.1.2), we define

$$\Lambda^0(V)=\mathbb R.$$ \tag{7.1.3}

With these definitions, the determinant function $\det:(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times1})^m\to\mathbb R$ is seen to be a multivector in $\Lambda^m(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times1})$.
