
## Step 3. Compute \(\nabla^2_{X,Y}(Z)\).

By definition,

\[
\nabla^2_{X,Y}(Z)=\nabla_X(\nabla_Y(Z))-\nabla_{\nabla_X(Y)}(Z).
\]

We have from (12.4.16) and (12.4.19) that the \(l\)th component of \(\nabla^2_{X,Y}(Z)\circ\varphi\) is

\[
\begin{aligned}
(\nabla^2_{X,Y}(Z)\circ\varphi)^l
&=\left([\nabla_X(\nabla_Y(Z))-\nabla_{\nabla_X(Y)}(Z)]\circ\varphi\right)^l\\
&=\left(\sum^{(1)}+\sum^{(2)}+\sum^{(3)}+\sum^{(4)}+\sum^{(5)}+\sum^{(6)}+\sum^{(7)}\right)
-\left(\sum^{(1)}+\sum^{(2)}+\sum^{(8)}+\sum^{(9)}\right)\\
&=\sum_{ij}^{(3)}\alpha^i\beta^j\frac{\partial^2\gamma^l}{\partial r^i\partial r^j}+\sum_{ijk}^{(4)}\alpha^i\beta^j\frac{\partial\gamma^k}{\partial r^i}\Gamma^l_{jk}+\sum_{ijk}^{(5)}\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\frac{\partial\Gamma^l_{jk}}{\partial r^i}\\
&\quad+\sum_{ijk}^{(6)}\alpha^i\beta^j\frac{\partial\gamma^k}{\partial r^j}\Gamma^l_{ik}+\sum_{ijkn}^{(7)}\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\Gamma^n_{jk}\Gamma^l_{in}\\
&\quad-\sum_{ijk}^{(8)}\alpha^i\beta^j\frac{\partial\gamma^l}{\partial r^k}\Gamma^k_{ij}-\sum_{ijkn}^{(9)}\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\Gamma^n_{ij}\Gamma^l_{nk}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since

\[
\sum_{ijk}^{(4)}\alpha^i\beta^j\frac{\partial\gamma^k}{\partial r^i}\Gamma^l_{jk}+\sum_{ijk}^{(6)}\alpha^j\beta^i\frac{\partial\gamma^k}{\partial r^i}\Gamma^l_{jk}=\sum_{ijk}^{(4)+(6)}(\alpha^i\beta^j+\alpha^j\beta^i)\frac{\partial\gamma^k}{\partial r^i}\Gamma^l_{jk}
\tag{12.4.21}
\]

and

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{ijk}^{(5)}\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\frac{\partial\Gamma^l_{jk}}{\partial r^i}+\sum_{ijkn}^{(7)}\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\Gamma^n_{jk}\Gamma^l_{in}-\sum_{ijkn}^{(9)}\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\Gamma^n_{ij}\Gamma^l_{kn}\\
&=\sum_{ijk}^{(5)}\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\frac{\partial\Gamma^l_{jk}}{\partial r^i}+\sum_{ijk}^{(7)}\left(\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\sum_n\Gamma^n_{jk}\Gamma^l_{in}\right)-\sum_{ijk}^{(9)}\left(\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\sum_n\Gamma^n_{ij}\Gamma^l_{kn}\right)\\
&=\sum_{ijk}^{(5)+(7)-(9)}\left[\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\left(\frac{\partial\Gamma^l_{jk}}{\partial r^i}+\sum_n(\Gamma^l_{in}\Gamma^n_{jk}-\Gamma^l_{kn}\Gamma^n_{ij})\right)\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{12.4.22}
\]

we have from (12.4.20)–(12.4.22) that

\[
(\nabla^2_{X,Y}(Z)\circ\varphi)^l
= \sum_{ij}^{(3)}\alpha^i\beta^j\frac{\partial^2\gamma^l}{\partial r^i\partial r^j}
+\sum_{ijk}^{(4)+(6)}(\alpha^i\beta^j+\alpha^j\beta^i)\frac{\partial\gamma^k}{\partial r^i}\Gamma^l_{jk}
-\sum_{ijk}^{(8)}\alpha^i\beta^j\frac{\partial\gamma^l}{\partial r^k}\Gamma^k_{ij}\\
+\sum_{ijk}^{(5)+(7)-(9)}\alpha^i\beta^j\gamma^k\left(\frac{\partial\Gamma^l_{jk}}{\partial r^i}+\sum_n(\Gamma^l_{in}\Gamma^n_{jk}-\Gamma^l_{kn}\Gamma^n_{ij})\right).
\]

(b): This follows from Theorem 10.1.6 and part (a).

It was remarked following Theorem 12.3.3 that the Christoffel symbols corresponding to \(\mathbb{R}^n\) have constant value 0 and this is related to \(\mathbb{R}^n\) being “flat”. We see from Theorem 12.4.3(b) that in the context of \(\mathbb{R}^n\), the order of vector fields is immaterial when computing the second order covariant derivative. This is reminiscent of the Euclidean situation in \(\mathbb{R}^m\) [see (Theorem 10.3.4(b))]. The following example shows that for the sphere \(S^2\), order is important.

### Example 12.4.4 (\(S^2\)).

In what follows, we use the Christoffel symbol results of Section 13.4. Setting \(X=H_1\circ\varphi^{-1}\) and \(Y=H_2\circ\varphi^{-1}\) in Theorem 12.4.3(a) (so that \(\alpha^1=\beta^2=1\) and \(\alpha^2=\beta^1=0\)), and also setting \(r^1=\theta\) and \(r^2=\phi\), a lengthy but straightforward computation yields

\[
(\nabla^2_{H_1\circ\varphi^{-1},H_2\circ\varphi^{-1}}(Z)-\nabla^2_{H_2\circ\varphi^{-1},H_1\circ\varphi^{-1}}(Z))\circ\varphi
=\sin^2(\theta)\gamma^2H_1-\gamma^1H_2.
\]

Due to the symmetry of the sphere, it is not surprising that the above expression is independent of \(\phi\).

## 12.5 Covariant Derivative on Curves in \(\mathbb{R}^3_\nu\)

Let \((M,\alpha)\) be a regular surface in \(\mathbb{R}^3_\nu\), let \(\lambda(t):(a,b)\to M\) be a smooth curve, and let \(J(t):(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}^3_\nu\) be a map. In the present context, we refer to \(J\) as a **vector field along** \(\lambda\). The set of smooth vector fields along \(\lambda\) is denoted by \(\mathfrak{X}_{\mathbb{R}^3_\nu}(\lambda)\). As an example, if \(V\) is a vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}_{\mathbb{R}^3_\nu}(M)\), then \(V\circ\lambda\) is a vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}_{\mathbb{R}^3_\nu}(\lambda)\). We say that \(J\) is a **(tangent) vector field on \(\lambda\)** if \(J(t)\) is in \(T_{\lambda(t)}(M)\) for all \(t\) in \((a,b)\). Let us denote the set of smooth vector fields on \(\lambda\) by \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)\). For example, \(d\lambda/dt\), the velocity of \(\lambda\), is in \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)\). As another example, if \(X\) is a vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}(M)\), then \(X\circ\lambda\) is a vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)\).

For a vector field \(J\) in \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)\), we have by definition that \(J(t)\) is a vector in \(T_{\lambda(t)}(M)\) for all \(t\) in \((a,b)\). But this is not necessarily so for \(dJ/dt\). In particular, although the velocity of \(\lambda\) is in \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)\), its (Euclidean) acceleration may not be. We need a definition of “derivative” that avoids this problem. Our response is similar to the approach taken in Section 12.4.

The **covariant derivative on \(\lambda\)** consists of two maps, both denoted by \(\nabla/dt\). The first is

\[
\frac{\nabla}{dt}:C^\infty((a,b))\longrightarrow C^\infty((a,b)).
\]

defined by

\[
\frac{\nabla f}{dt}(t)=\frac{df}{dt}(t)
\]

for all functions \(f\) in \(C^\infty((a,b))\) and all \(t\) in \((a,b)\). The second is

\[
\frac{\nabla}{dt}:\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)
\]

defined by

\[
\frac{\nabla J}{dt}(t)=\tan_{\lambda(t)}\left(\frac{dJ}{dt}(t)\right)
\]

for all vector fields \(J\) in \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)\) and all \(t\) in \((a,b)\), where, following Section 12.4, \(\tan_{\lambda(t)}\) denotes the projection map \(\mathcal{P}_{T_{\lambda(t)}(M)}\).

The **(covariant) acceleration** of \(\lambda\) is defined to be the smooth curve

\[
\frac{\nabla}{dt}\left(\frac{d\lambda}{dt}\right)(t):(a,b)\longrightarrow M.
\]

For vector fields \(J,K\) in \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)\), we define a function

\[
\langle J,K\rangle:(a,b)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
\]

in \(C^\infty((a,b))\) by the assignment

\[
t\longmapsto\langle J(t),K(t)\rangle
\]

for all \(t\) in \((a,b)\).

The definition of covariant derivative on a curve has an appealing physical interpretation. Imagine a “bug” that is confined to the 2-dimensional world of a given regular surface in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). For this creature, there is no “up” or “down”, only movements “on” the surface. Suppose the bug is scurrying along, tracing a smooth curve as it goes. From our vantage point in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and knowing something about Newtonian physics, we determine that the bug has a certain velocity and nonzero (Euclidean) acceleration. For both us and the bug, velocity is entirely a tangential phenomenon. On the other hand, we observe the acceleration to have both tangential and normal components. But not so for the bug, which is oblivious to any such normal phenomena. This suggests that in order to quantify what we presume to be the acceleration felt by the bug, we should confine our attention to the tangential component. This is accomplished by taking the projection onto the tangent plane.

### Theorem 12.5.1.

Let \((M,g)\) be a regular surface in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and let \(\lambda(t):(a,b)\longrightarrow M\) be a smooth curve. Let \(J,K\) be vector fields in \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)\), let \(f\) be a function in \(C^\infty((a,b))\), and let \(g(u):(c,d)\longrightarrow(a,b)\) be a diffeomorphism. Then, for all \(t\) in \((a,b)\) and all \(u\) in \((c,d)\):

- (a) \(\displaystyle \frac{\nabla(J+K)}{dt}(t)=\frac{\nabla J}{dt}(t)+\frac{\nabla K}{dt}(t).\)
- (b) \(\displaystyle \frac{\nabla(fJ)}{dt}(t)=\frac{df}{dt}(t)J(t)+f(t)\frac{\nabla J}{dt}(t).\)
- (c) \(\displaystyle \frac{d\langle J,K\rangle}{dt}(t)=\left\langle\frac{\nabla J}{dt}(t),K(t)\right\rangle+\left\langle J(t),\frac{\nabla K}{dt}(t)\right\rangle.\)
- (d) \(\displaystyle \frac{\nabla(J\circ g)}{du}(u)=\frac{dg}{du}(u)\frac{\nabla J}{dt}(g(u)).\)

Let \((M,\mathfrak{g})\) be a regular surface in \(\mathbb{R}^3_{\nu}\), let \((U,\varphi)\) be a chart on \(M\), and let \((H_1,H_2)\) and \(G\) be the corresponding coordinate frame and coordinate unit normal vector field. Let \(\lambda:(a,b)\longrightarrow M\) be a smooth curve such that \(\lambda((a,b))\subset U\), and let \(J\) be a vector field in \(\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)\). By Theorem 10.1.17 and Theorem 11.2.8, the map

\[
\mu = \varphi^{-1}\circ\lambda = (\mu^1,\mu^2):(a,b)\longrightarrow U
\tag{12.5.2}
\]

is smooth. Then \(J(t)\) can be expressed as

\[
J(t)=\sum_i \alpha^i(t) H_i|_{\mu(t)},
\tag{12.5.3}
\]

where the \(\alpha^i\) are uniquely determined functions in \(C^\infty((a,b))\), called the components of \(J\) with respect to \((U,\varphi)\). The right-hand side of (12.5.3) is said to express \(J\) in local coordinates with respect to \((U,\varphi)\).

### Theorem 12.5.2.

With the above setup, for all \(t\) in \((a,b)\):

- (a) \(\displaystyle \frac{dJ}{dt}(t)=\sum_k\left(\frac{d\alpha^k}{dt}(t)+\sum_{ij}\alpha^i(t)\frac{d\mu^j}{dt}(t)\Gamma^k_{ij}(\mu(t))\right)H_k|_{\mu(t)}+\left(\sum_{ij}\alpha^i(t)\frac{d\mu^j}{dt}(t)\vartheta_{ij}(\mu(t))\right)G_{\mu(t)}.\)
- (b) \(\displaystyle \frac{\nabla J}{dt}(t)=\sum_k\left(\frac{d\alpha^k}{dt}(t)+\sum_{ij}\alpha^i(t)\frac{d\mu^j}{dt}(t)\Gamma^k_{ij}(\mu(t))\right)H_k|_{\mu(t)}.\)
- (c) \(\displaystyle \frac{d^2\lambda}{dt^2}(t)=\sum_k\left(\frac{d^2\mu^k}{dt^2}(t)+\sum_{ij}\frac{d\mu^i}{dt}(t)\frac{d\mu^j}{dt}(t)\Gamma^k_{ij}(\mu(t))\right)H_k|_{\mu(t)}+\left(\sum_{ij}\frac{d\mu^i}{dt}(t)\frac{d\mu^j}{dt}(t)\vartheta_{ij}(\mu(t))\right)G_{\mu(t)}.\)
- (d) \(\displaystyle \left\langle\frac{d\lambda}{dt}(t),\frac{d\lambda}{dt}(t)\right\rangle=\sum_{ij}\frac{d\mu^i}{dt}(t)\frac{d\mu^j}{dt}(t)\mathfrak{g}_{ij}(\mu(t)).\)
