
Thus, $d \circ i_X + i_X \circ d$ is a form derivation on $M$. By Theorem 16.4.3, the same is true of $\mathcal{L}_X$.

Let $f$ be a function in $C^\infty(M)$. Then

```
(d \circ i_X + i_X \circ d)(f) = d(i_X(f)) + i_X(d(f)) = X(f) [(16.5.2), (16.5.3)] = \mathcal{L}_X(f) [(16.4.1)]
```

and

```
(d \circ i_X + i_X \circ d)(d(f)) = d(i_X(d(f))) + i_X(d^2(f)) = d(X(f)) [Th 16.1.1(c), (16.5.2)] = d(\mathcal{L}_X(f)) [(16.4.1)] = \mathcal{L}_X(d(f)). [Th 16.4.6]
```

Since $f$ was arbitrary, $d \circ i_X + i_X \circ d$ and $\mathcal{L}_X$ agree on $C^\infty(M)$ and $\{d(f) : f \in C^\infty(M)\}$. The result now follows from Theorem 16.3.2.

The next result is a generalization of Theorem 16.4.6.

## Theorem 16.5.4 (Commutativity of Lie Derivative and Exterior Derivative of Forms). If $M$ is a smooth manifold and $X$ is a vector field in $\mathfrak{X}(M)$, then

```
\mathcal{L}_X \circ d = d \circ \mathcal{L}_X;
```

that is,

```
\mathcal{L}_X(d(\omega)) = d(\mathcal{L}_X(\omega))
```

for all forms $\omega$ in $\Lambda^s(M)$.

Proof. It follows from Theorem 16.1.1(c) and Theorem 16.5.3 that

```
\mathcal{L}_X \circ d = (d \circ i_X + i_X \circ d) \circ d = d \circ i_X \circ d + i_X \circ d^2 = d \circ i_X \circ d
```

and

```
d \circ \mathcal{L}_X = d \circ (d \circ i_X + i_X \circ d) = d^2 \circ i_X + d \circ i_X \circ d = d \circ i_X \circ d.
```

The result follows.

The material in this section borrows heavily from Section 8.2 and Section 12.7.

Let $M$ be a smooth manifold, let $(U, (x^i))$ and $(\widetilde{U}, (\widetilde{x}^i))$ be overlapping charts on $M$, and let $\mathcal{X}$ and $\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}$ be the corresponding coordinate frames. Recall

We say that the orientation -𝔒 is the opposite of 𝔒.

A vector space always has precisely two orientations, but the situation is different for smooth manifolds. As we saw in Example 12.7.9, the Möbius band does not have a smooth unit normal vector field, and therefore, by Theorem 12.7.6, it fails to have an orientation. On the other hand, if a smooth manifold has an orientation, then it has at least two of them, namely, the given orientation and its opposite—but there could be more.

Defining the orientation of a smooth manifold in terms of an atlas ultimately rests on the way the bases for tangent spaces are oriented, which is geometrically appealing. However, this approach is not computationally convenient. We now present an algebraic alternative that eases this problem, but at the expense of greater abstraction.

Let M be a smooth m-manifold. We say that a form ω in Λᵐ(M) is an orientation form (on M) if it is nowhere-vanishing; or equivalently, if ω_p is a nonzero multicovector in Λᵐ(T_p(M)) for all p in M; or equivalently, if ω_p is an orientation multicovector on T_p(M) for all p in M. In the literature, such a form is often referred to as a volume form, but we reserve this terminology for a particular type of orientation form that makes it possible (in certain settings) to measure “volume”. As we will soon see, there is no guarantee that a given smooth manifold has an orientation form.

## Theorem 16.6.1. Let M be a smooth manifold, and let ω, ϑ be orientation forms on M. Then:

- (a) There is a uniquely determined nowhere-vanishing function f in C^∞(M) such that ω = f ϑ.
- (b) If M is connected as a topological space, then f is either strictly positive or strictly negative.

assignment $p \longmapsto f(p)$ defines a nowhere-vanishing function $f : M \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ such that $\varpi = f\vartheta$. It follows from Theorem 14.3.3(b) that $\varpi|_U$ and $\vartheta|_U$ are orientation forms on $U$, and then from Theorem 15.8.5 that $\varpi|_U = g\,d(x^1)\wedge\cdots\wedge d(x^m)$ and $\vartheta|_U = h\,d(x^1)\wedge\cdots\wedge d(x^m)$, where $g$ and $h$ are uniquely determined functions in $C^\infty(U)$. Since $\varpi|_U$ and $\vartheta|_U$ are nowhere-vanishing, so are $g$ and $h$. It follows that $\varpi|_U = (g/h)\vartheta|_U$, hence $f|_U = g/h$ is a function in $C^\infty(U)$. Since $p$ was arbitrary, $f$ is in $C^\infty(M)$. The uniqueness of $f$ follows from the preceding arguments.

(b): This follows from Theorem 9.1.20 and Theorem 10.1.1.

Let $M$ be a smooth manifold, and let $\varpi$ and $\vartheta$ be orientation forms on $M$. By Theorem 16.6.1(a), there is a uniquely determined nowhere-vanishing function $f$ in $C^\infty(M)$ such that $\varpi = f\vartheta$. We say that $\varpi$ and $\vartheta$ are **consistent (on $M$)**, and write $\varpi \sim \vartheta$, if $f$ is strictly positive on $M$. It is easily shown that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation on the set of orientation forms on $M$. The equivalence class containing $\varpi$ is denoted by $[\varpi]$. Since $\varpi$ and $-\varpi$ are not consistent, $[\varpi]$ and $[-\varpi]$ are distinct equivalence classes. We emphasize again that there is no guarantee that a given smooth manifold has even one orientation form, so the preceding equivalence relation may be vacuous. On the other hand, if the equivalence relation does exist, there could be more than two equivalence classes.

## Theorem 16.6.2.
