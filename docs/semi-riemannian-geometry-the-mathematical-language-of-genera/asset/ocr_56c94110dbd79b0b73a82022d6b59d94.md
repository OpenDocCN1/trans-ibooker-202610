A glance at Figure 14.2.2(b) explains why a function such as β is called a bump function. Bump functions are often called upon to extend the domain of a smooth map, as in the proof of the next result.

# Theorem 14.2.7 (Extension of Function). Let M be a smooth manifold, let p be a point in M, let U be a neighborhood of p in M, and let f be a function in C^∞(U). Then there is a function \tilde{f} in C^∞(M) and a neighborhood \tilde{U} ⊆ U of p in M such that f and \tilde{f} agree on \tilde{U}; that is, f|_{\tilde{U}} = \tilde{f}|_{\tilde{U}}.

Proof. Let β be a bump function in C^∞(M) that has support in U and constant value 1 on a neighborhood \tilde{U} ⊆ U of p in M. We define a function \tilde{f} : M ⟶ ℝ by

```
\tilde{f}(q)=\begin{cases} \beta(q)f(q) & \text{if } q\in U \\ 0 & \text{if } q\in M\setminus U. \end{cases}
```

Because β and f are smooth on U, so is \tilde{f}. Let q be a point in M\setminus U. Since β has support in U, q is in M\setminus supp(β), and because supp(β) is closed in M, M\setminus supp(β) is open in M. Thus, \tilde{f}=0 on the neighborhood M\setminus supp(β) of q in M. Since q was arbitrary, \tilde{f} is smooth on M\setminus U. This shows that \tilde{f} is smooth on M. Lastly, because β=1 on \tilde{U}, f and \tilde{f} agree on \tilde{U}.
