This shows that parallel translation of a vector from one point to another in $\mathrm{PIn}$ is independent of the choice of smooth curve joining the two points. In particular, parallel translation of a vector along the entire trajectory of a closed smooth curve in $\mathrm{PIn}$, such as a circle, takes the vector to itself.

**Example 18.5.4 ($S^2$).** Let us view the regular surface $S^2$, discussed in Section 13.4, as a smooth 2-manifold with the connection defined in Example 18.1.1. Let

$\theta(t):(a,b)\longrightarrow(0,\pi)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\phi(t):(a,b)\longrightarrow(-\pi,\pi)$

be diffeomorphisms, and consider the smooth curve $\lambda(t):(a,b)\longrightarrow S^2$ given by

$$\lambda(t)=\varphi(\theta(t),\phi(t))=\bigl(\sin(\theta(t))\cos(\phi(t)),\sin(\theta(t))\sin(\phi(t)),\cos(\theta(t))\bigr).$$

The coordinate basis at $\lambda(t)$ is $(H_1|_{(\theta(t),\phi(t))},H_2|_{(\theta(t),\phi(t))})$, where

$$H_1|_{(\theta(t),\phi(t))}=\bigl(\cos(\theta(t))\cos(\phi(t)),\cos(\theta(t))\sin(\phi(t)),-\sin(\theta(t))\bigr)$$

$$H_2|_{(\theta(t),\phi(t))}=\bigl(-\sin(\theta(t))\sin(\phi(t)),\sin(\theta(t))\cos(\phi(t)),0\bigr).$$

Setting

$$v=\frac{d\lambda}{dt}(t_0) \tag{18.5.2}$$

in Theorem 18.5.1 and recalling the results on Christoffel symbols in Section 13.4, the differential equations in Example 18.5.2 become

$$\frac{d\alpha^1}{dt}(t)-\cos(\theta(t))\sin(\theta(t))\frac{d\phi}{dt}(t)\alpha^2(t)=0$$

$$\frac{d\alpha^2}{dt}(t)+\cot(\theta(t))\left(\frac{d\phi}{dt}(t)\alpha^1(t)+\frac{d\theta}{dt}(t)\alpha^2(t)\right)=0, \tag{18.5.3}$$

where we have used

$(x\circ\lambda(t),x^2\circ\lambda(t))=(x^1,x^2)\circ\lambda(t)=\varphi^{-1}\circ\lambda(t)=(\theta(t),\phi(t)).$

For a given point $\theta_0$ in $(0,\pi)$, let $\theta$ be the function that has constant value $\theta_0$, and let $\phi$ be the identity function. As well, let us denote $t$ by $\phi$, so that $\phi_0=t_0$ and $\phi$ is in $(-\pi,\pi)$. Setting $c_0=\cos(\theta_0)$ and $s_0=\sin(\theta_0)$, we have

$$\lambda(\phi)=(s_0\cos(\phi),s_0\sin(\phi),c_0) \tag{18.5.4}$$

$$\frac{d\lambda}{d\phi}(\phi)=(-s_0\sin(\phi),s_0\cos(\phi),0) \tag{18.5.5}$$

$$H_1|_{(\theta_0,\phi)}=(c_0\cos(\phi),c_0\sin(\phi),-s_0) \tag{18.5.6}$$

$$H_2|_{(\theta_0,\phi)}=(-s_0\sin(\phi),s_0\cos(\phi),0).$$

Now set φ₀ = 0. Then (18.5.2) and (18.5.5) give v = (0, s₀, 0). We seek an explicit expression for

Π_{λ(0)}^{λ(φ)} ((0, s₀, 0)) = α¹(φ) H₁|_{(θ₀,φ)} + α²(φ) H₂|_{(θ₀,φ)},  (18.5.8)

(0, s₀, 0) = Π_{λ(0)}^{λ(0)} ((0, s₀, 0)) = α¹(0) H₁|_{(θ₀,0)} + α²(0) H₂|_{(θ₀,0)}.  (18.5.9)

α¹(0) = A      α²(0) = −B/s₀.  (18.5.12)

hence A = 0 and B = −s₀. We then have from (18.5.10) that

α¹(φ) = s₀ sin(c₀ φ)      α²(φ) = cos(c₀ φ).  (18.5.13)

Finally, substituting (18.5.6) and (18.5.13) into (18.5.8) yields

Π_{λ(0)}^{λ(φ)} ((0, s₀, 0)) = s₀ sin(c₀ φ)(c₀ cos(φ), c₀ sin(φ), −s₀) + cos(c₀ φ)(−s₀ sin(φ), s₀ cos(φ), 0).

For purposes of investigating the properties of parallel translation of v = (0, s₀, 0), there is no harm in extending the domain of φ to all of ℝ. In particular, we have for φ = 2π that

Π_{λ(0)}^{λ(2π)} ((0, s₀, 0)) = s₀ sin(2π c₀)(c₀, 0, −s₀) + cos(2π c₀)(0, s₀, 0).

It is easily shown that Π_{λ(0)}^{λ(2π)}((0, s₀, 0)) = (0, s₀, 0) if and only if θ₀ = π/2; that is, if and only if the image of λ lies on the “equator”. We therefore have the surprising finding that parallel translation of v along the entire trajectory of a latitude curve other than the equator does not send v to itself. This is a manifestation of the “curvature” of the sphere.

The intersection of S² and a plane through the origin is called a great circle of S², the equator being the obvious example. The parametrization we selected at the outset features the equator, but this was a matter of convenience. Due to the symmetry of S², any great circle would do.

# Theorem 18.5.5 (Diffeomorphism Invariance of Parallelism of a Curve).

Let (M, ∇) be a smooth manifold with a connection, let λ(t) : (a, b) ⟶ M be a smooth curve, and let g(u) : (c, d) ⟶ (a, b) be a diffeomorphism. Then the vector field J in 𝔛_M(λ) is parallel if and only if the vector field J ∘ g in 𝔛_M(λ ∘ g) is parallel.

Proof. This follows from Theorem 10.2.4(a) and Theorem 18.3.3(a).

# Theorem 18.5.6.

Let (M, ∇) be a smooth manifold with a connection, let λ : (a, b) ⟶ M be a smooth curve, and let t₀ and t be points in (a, b). Then Π_{λ(t₀)}^{λ(t)} : T_{λ(t₀)}(M) ⟶ T_{λ(t)}(M) is a linear isomorphism, and its inverse

(Π_{λ(t₀)}^{λ(t)})⁻¹ : T_{λ(t)}(M) ⟶ T_{λ(t₀)}(M)

is given by

(Π_{λ(t₀)}^{λ(t)})⁻¹(v) = Π_{λ(t)}^{λ(t₀)}(v)

for all vectors v in T_{λ(t)}(M).

Proof. Let v, w be vectors in T_{λ(t₀)}(M). By Theorem 18.5.1, there are parallel vector fields J, K in 𝔛_M(λ) such that J(t₀) = v and K(t₀) = w. For a real number c, we have

∇(cJ + K)/dt (t) = c ∇J/dt (t) + ∇K/dt (t) = 0

for all t in (a, b). Thus, cJ + K is a parallel vector field in 𝔛_M(λ) such that (cJ + K)(t₀) = cJ(t₀) + K(t₀) = cv + w.

It follows from the uniqueness property in Theorem 18.5.1 that Π_{λ(t₀)}^{λ(t)}(cv + w) = (cJ + K)(t) = cJ(t) + K(t) = c Π_{λ(t₀)}^{λ(t)}(v) + Π_{λ(t₀)}^{λ(t)}(w).

Thus, Π_{λ(t₀)}^{λ(t)} is linear.

Let v and w be vectors in T_{λ(t₀)}(M) and T_{λ(t)}(M), respectively. It follows from the properties of ordinary differential equations that w is the parallel translate of v if and only if v is the parallel translate of w. Thus, Π_{λ(t₀)}^{λ(t)} is a bijective map, and therefore a linear isomorphism, with inverse (Π_{λ(t₀)}^{λ(t)})⁻¹ = Π_{λ(t)}^{λ(t₀)}.

# Theorem 18.5.7 (Existence of Parallel Frame on a Curve). Let $(M, \nabla)$ be a smooth $m$-manifold with a connection, let $\lambda(t): (a,b) \longrightarrow M$ be a smooth curve, let $t_0$ be a point in $(a,b)$, and let $(v_1,\dots,v_m)$ be a basis for $T_{\lambda(t_0)}(M)$. Then there is a unique frame $\mathcal{J}$ on $\lambda$ consisting of parallel vector fields in $\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)$ such that $\mathcal{J}(t_0) = (v_1,\dots,v_m)$ and $\mathcal{J}(t)$ is a basis for $T_{\lambda(t)}(M)$ for all $t$ in $(a,b)$.

Proof. According to Theorem 18.5.1, there is a unique parallel vector field $J_i$ in $\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)$ such that $J_i(t_0)=v_i$ for $i=1,\dots,m$. By definition, $J_i(t)=\Pi_{\lambda(t_0)}^{\lambda(t)}(v_i)$ for all $t$ in $(a,b)$. Suppose there is a point $t$ in $(a,b)$ such that $\sum_i a^i J_i(t)=0$ for some real numbers $a^1,\dots,a^m$. Using Theorem 18.5.6 to apply $(\Pi_{\lambda(t_0)}^{\lambda(t)})^{-1}$ to both sides of the preceding identity yields $\sum_i a^i v_i=0$, hence $a^i=0$ for $i=1,\dots,m$. Thus, $J_1(t),\dots,J_m(t)$ are linearly independent vectors in $T_{\lambda(t)}(M)$. By Theorem 14.3.4(c), $T_{\lambda(t)}(M)$ has dimension $m$, so $\mathcal{J}(t)$ is a basis for $T_{\lambda(t)}(M)$ for all $t$ in $(a,b)$.

At the beginning of this section, covariant differentiation along a curve was used to define parallel translation. The next result shows that parallel translation can be used to define covariant differentiation along a curve. In this sense, covariant differentiation and parallel translation are equivalent concepts.

# Theorem 18.5.8. Let $(M,\nabla)$ be a smooth manifold with a connection, let $\lambda(t): (a,b) \longrightarrow M$ be a smooth curve, let $t_0$ be a point in $(a,b)$, and let $K$ be a vector field in $\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)$. Then

$$\frac{\nabla K}{dt}(t_0)=\lim_{t\to t_0}\frac{\Pi_{\lambda(t)}^{\lambda(t_0)}(K(t))-K(t_0)}{t}$$

Proof. Let $\mathcal{J}=(J_1,\dots,J_m)$ be the frame given by Theorem 18.5.7. Then $K(t)$ can be expressed as

$$K(t)=\sum_i \alpha^i(t)J_i(t),\qquad(18.5.14)$$

where the $\alpha^i$ are uniquely determined functions in $C^\infty((a,b))$. In particular

$$K(t_0)=\sum_i \alpha^i(t_0)v_i.$$

Since each $J_i$ is a parallel vector field in $\mathfrak{X}_M(\lambda)$, we have $(\nabla J_i/dt)(t)=0$, hence

$$\frac{\nabla K}{dt}(t)=\sum_i\left(\frac{d\alpha^i}{dt}(t)J_i(t)+\alpha^i(t)\frac{\nabla J_i}{dt}(t)\right)=\sum_i\frac{d\alpha^i}{dt}(t)J_i(t),$$

so

$$\frac{\nabla K}{dt}(t_0)=\sum_i\frac{d\alpha^i}{dt}(t_0)J_i(t_0)=\sum_i\frac{d\alpha^i}{dt}(t_0)v_i.\qquad(18.5.15)$$
