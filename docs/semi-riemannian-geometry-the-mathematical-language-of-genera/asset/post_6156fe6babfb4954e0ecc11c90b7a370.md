Clearly, 𝔰 is bilinear and symmetric. Let (e₁,...,eₘ) be the standard basis for ℝᵐ, and suppose v = (a¹,...,aᵐ) is a vector in ℝᵐ such that ⟨v,w⟩ = 0 for all vectors w in ℝᵐ. Then, in particular, 0 = ⟨v,eᵢ⟩ = ±aⁱ for each i, hence v = 0. Thus, 𝔰 is nondegenerate. This shows that 𝔰 is a scalar product on ℝᵐ. The scalar product space (ℝᵐ,𝔰) is called semi-Euclidean (m,ν)-space. We usually denote (ℝᵐ,𝔰) by ℝᵥᵐ, and write ℝᵥᵐ = (ℝᵐ,𝔰).

Let us observe that the presence or absence of a scalar product on ℝᵐ has nothing to do with the existence of a basis. Accordingly, the standard basis for ℝᵥᵐ is defined to be the standard basis for ℝᵐ.

## Theorem 4.1.2. If (V,g) is a scalar product space and U is a subspace of V, then:
- (a) dim(U⊥) = dim(U⁰).
- (b) dim(V) = dim(U) + dim(U⊥).
- (c) U⊥⊥ = U.

Proof. (a): We have U⊥ = {v ∈ V : ⟨v,u⟩ = 0 for all u ∈ U} = {v ∈ V : F(v) ∈ U⁰} = F⁻¹(U⁰) = S(U⁰) = im(S|U⁰), hence dim(U⊥) = rank(S|U⁰).

Since S : V* ⟶ V is a linear isomorphism, so is S|U⁰ : U⁰ ⟶ S(U⁰). Then Theorem 1.1.14 gives rank(S|U⁰) = dim(U⁰). The result follows.

(b): This follows from Theorem 1.4.1 and part (a).

(c): If u is a vector in U, then ⟨u,v⟩ = 0 for all vectors v in U⊥, hence u is in U⊥⊥. Thus, U ⊆ U⊥⊥. Using part (b) twice yields dim(U) + dim(U⊥) = dim(V) = dim(U⊥) + dim(U⊥⊥), so dim(U) = dim(U⊥⊥). The result now follows from Theorem 1.1.7(b).

## Theorem 4.1.3.

If $(V,\mathfrak{g})$ is a scalar product space and $U$ is a subspace of $V$, then the following are equivalent:
- (a) $\mathfrak{g}$ is nondegenerate on $U$.
- (b) $U\cap U^\perp=\{0\}$.
- (c) $V=U\oplus U^\perp$.
- (d) $\mathfrak{g}$ is nondegenerate on $U^\perp$.

**Proof.** (a) $\Leftrightarrow$ (b): We have

$\mathfrak{g}$ is nondegenerate on $U$
$\Leftrightarrow\quad v$ is in $U$, and $\langle v,w\rangle=0$ for all $w$ in $U$ implies $v=0$
$\Leftrightarrow\quad v$ is in $U$, and $v$ is in $U^\perp$ implies $v=0$
$\Leftrightarrow\quad U\cap U^\perp=\{0\}$.

(b) $\Rightarrow$ (c): It follows from

$\dim(V)=\dim(U)+\dim(U^\perp) \qquad\text{[Th 4.1.2(b)]}$
$\qquad=\dim(U+U^\perp)+\dim(U\cap U^\perp) \qquad\text{[Th 1.1.15]}$

and $U\cap U^\perp=\{0\}$ that $\dim(V)=\dim(U+U^\perp)$, and then from Theorem 1.1.7(b) that $V=U+U^\perp$. By Theorem 1.1.7, $U+U^\perp=U\oplus U^\perp$, hence $V=U\oplus U^\perp$.

(c) $\Rightarrow$ (b): This follows from Theorem 1.1.7.
(c) $\Leftrightarrow$ (d): This follows from (a) $\Leftrightarrow$ (c) and Theorem 4.1.2(c).

Continuing with the notation of Theorem 4.1.3, suppose $\mathfrak{g}$ is nondegenerate on $U$, in which case $V=U\oplus U^\perp$. In this setting only, we refer to $U^\perp$ (previously called the perp of $U$ in $V$ with respect to $\mathfrak{g}$) as the **orthogonal complement of $U$ (in $V$ with respect to $\mathfrak{g}$)**. It follows from Theorem 4.1.2(c) that $U$ is the orthogonal complement of $U^\perp$ in $V$ with respect to $\mathfrak{g}$. Thus, $U$ and $U^\perp$ are orthogonal complements of each other in $V$ with respect to $\mathfrak{g}$. To illustrate, suppose $v\ne0$. It is easily shown that the subspaces $\mathbb{R}^{m-\nu}\times\{0\}^{\nu}$ and $\{0\}^{m-\nu}\times\mathbb{R}^{\nu}$ of $\mathbb{R}^m$ are orthogonal complements of each other in $\mathbb{R}^m$ with respect to $\mathfrak{g}$. Then Theorem 4.1.3 gives

$\mathbb{R}^m_\nu=(\mathbb{R}^{m-\nu}\times\{0\}^{\nu})\oplus(\{0\}^{m-\nu}\times\mathbb{R}^{\nu}).$ \tag{4.1.2}

Making the obvious identifications and abusing notation slightly, this can be expressed succinctly as $\mathbb{R}^m_\nu=\mathbb{R}^{m-\nu}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{\nu}$.

## Theorem 4.1.4.

If $(V,\mathfrak{g})$ is a scalar product space and $U$ is a subspace of $V$ on which $\mathfrak{g}$ is nondegenerate, then each vector $v$ in $V$ can be expressed uniquely in the form

$v=P_U(v)+P_{U^\perp}(v)$,

where $P_U(v)$ is a vector in $U$ and $P_{U^\perp}(v)$ is a vector in $U^\perp$.

**Proof.** This follows from Theorem 4.1.3.

In the notation of Theorem 4.1.4, the **orthogonal projection** on $U$ is the linear map

$$\mathcal{P}_U: V \longrightarrow U$$

defined by the assignment

$$v \longmapsto \mathcal{P}_U(v).$$

More generally, for a subset $S$ of $V$, let us define

$$\mathcal{P}_U(S)=\{\mathcal{P}_U(s):s\in S\}.$$

When $S$ consists of a single vector, say, $v$, we denote

$$\mathcal{P}_U(\{v\}) \quad \text{by} \quad \mathcal{P}_U(v).$$

## Theorem 4.1.5.

If $(V,\mathfrak{g})$ is a scalar product space and $v,w$ are vectors in $V$ such that $\langle v,v\rangle\neq 0$, then

$$\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}v}(w)=\frac{\langle v,w\rangle}{\langle v,v\rangle}v.$$

*Proof.* Since $\langle v,v\rangle\neq 0$, $\mathfrak{g}$ is nondegenerate on $\mathbb{R}v$, so Theorem 4.1.4 applies. Thus, $\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}v}(w)=cv$ and $w=cv+\mathcal{P}_{(\mathbb{R}v)^\perp}(w)$ for some real number $c$. Then

$$\langle v,w\rangle=\langle v,cv+\mathcal{P}_{(\mathbb{R}v)^\perp}(w)\rangle=c\langle v,v\rangle,$$

so $c=\langle v,w\rangle/\langle v,v\rangle$.

An observation related to (4.1.2) is that $\mathfrak{g}$ is positive definite on $\mathbb{R}^{m-\nu}\times\{0\}^{\nu}$ and negative definite on $\{0\}^{m-\nu}\times\mathbb{R}^{\nu}$. The next result shows that this is a specific instance of a more general phenomenon.

## Theorem 4.1.6 (Sylvester’s Law of Inertia).

If $(V,\mathfrak{g})$ is a scalar product space, then:
- (a) There are subspaces $V^+$ and $V^-$ of $V$ such $V=V^+\oplus V^-$, with $\mathfrak{g}$ positive definite on $V^+$ and negative definite on $V^-$.
- (b) The dimensions $\dim(V^+)$ and $\dim(V^-)$ are uniquely determined by $\mathfrak{g}$ in the following sense: if $W^+$ and $W^-$ are subspaces of $V$ such that $V=W^+\oplus W^-$, with $\mathfrak{g}$ positive definite on $W^+$ and negative definite on $W^-$, then $\dim(V^+)=\dim(W^+)$ and $\dim(V^-)=\dim(W^-)$.

*Proof.* (a): If $\mathfrak{g}=0$, the result is trivial, so assume otherwise. By Theorem 3.2.4(b), there is a nonzero vector $v$ in $V$ such that either $\langle v,v\rangle>0$ or $\langle v,v\rangle<0$. Suppose $\langle v,v\rangle>0$; the argument when $\langle v,v\rangle<0$ is similar. Then $\mathbb{R}v$ is a nonzero subspace of $V$ on which $\mathfrak{g}$ is positive definite. Let $V^+$ be a (not necessarily unique) subspace of $V$ that has maximal dimension among subspaces on which $\mathfrak{g}$ is positive definite, and let $V^-=(V^+)^\perp$. Then $\mathfrak{g}$ is nondegenerate on $V^+$, so Theorem 4.1.3 gives

$$V=V^+\oplus V^- \tag{4.1.3}$$

and

$$V^+ \cap V^- = \{0\}. \tag{4.1.4}$$ 

We claim that g is negative semidefinite on $V^-$. Suppose, for a contradiction, that there is a nonzero vector $v^-$ in $V^-$ such that $\langle v^-, v^-\rangle > 0$, and consider the subspace $V^+ + \mathbb{R}v^-$ of $V$. Since $\mathbb{R}v^- \subseteq V^-$, it follows from (4.1.3) that

$$V^+ + \mathbb{R}v^- = V^+ \oplus \mathbb{R}v^- . \tag{4.1.5}$$ 

Let $v^+ + a v^-$ be a vector in $V^+ + \mathbb{R}v^-$. Then

$$\langle v^+ + a v^-, v^+ + a v^-\rangle = \langle v^+, v^+\rangle + a^2\langle v^-, v^-\rangle \ge 0,$$

with

$$\begin{aligned}
\langle v^+ + a v^-, v^+ + a v^-\rangle &= 0 \\
\Leftrightarrow \quad \langle v^+, v^+\rangle &= 0 \text{ and } a^2\langle v^-, v^-\rangle = 0 \\
\Leftrightarrow \quad v^+ &= 0 \text{ and } a = 0 \\
\Leftrightarrow \quad v^+ + a v^- &= 0.
\end{aligned}\tag{[4.1.5]}$$ 

This shows that g is positive definite on $V^+ + \mathbb{R}v^-$. It follows from the maximality property of $V^+$ that $V^+ + \mathbb{R}v^- = V^+$, so $v^-$ is in $V^+$, which contradicts (4.1.4). This proves the claim.

We now claim that g is negative definite on $V^-$. Let $w^-$ be a vector in $V^-$ such that $\langle w^-, w^-\rangle = 0$. Since g is negative semidefinite on $V^-$, Theorem 3.2.6 applies. Thus, $\langle w^-, v^-\rangle^2 \le \langle w^-, w^-\rangle\langle v^-, v^-\rangle = 0$ for all vectors $v^-$ in $V^-$, hence $\langle w^-, v^-\rangle = 0$ for all vectors $v^-$ in $V^-$. Since $w^-$ is in $V^- = (V^+)^\perp$, $\langle w^-, v^+\rangle = 0$ for all vectors $v^+$ in $V^+$. We have from (4.1.3) that $V = V^+ + V^-$, so $\langle w^-, v\rangle = 0$ for all vectors $v$ in $V$. Since g is nondegenerate, $w^- = 0$. Thus, g is negative definite on $V^-$. This proves the claim.

(b): Suppose there is a nonzero vector $v$ in $V^+ \cap W^-$. By assumption, g is positive definite on $V^+$, so $\langle v, v\rangle > 0$. Also by assumption, g is negative definite on $W^-$, hence $\langle v, v\rangle < 0$, which is a contradiction. Thus, $V^+ \cap W^- = \{0\}$. We have from Theorem 1.1.17 that $V^+ + W^- = V^+ \oplus W^-$, and then from Theorem 1.1.18 that

$$\dim(V^+) + \dim(W^-) = \dim(V^+ + W^-) \le \dim(V).$$

By Theorem 1.1.18 and (4.1.3),

$$\dim(V^+) + \dim(V^-) = \dim(V).$$

It follows that $\dim(W^-) \le \dim(V^-)$. Similarly, since g is positive definite on $W^+$ and negative definite on $V^-$, we have $\dim(V^-) \le \dim(W^-)$. Thus, $\dim(V^-) = \dim(W^-)$. A corresponding argument shows that $\dim(V^+) = \dim(W^+)$.
