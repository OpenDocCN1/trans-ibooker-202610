
where we note that, by definition, $\gamma$ and $\beta$ have the same sign. Then

$$-1<\beta<1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \gamma^2(1-\beta^2)=1. \tag{22.1.3}$$

When $\gamma\ge 1$, which is the case of greatest interest,

$$0\le\beta<1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}.$$

Given a map $A$ in $L_m$, we have from Theorem 4.4.3 that $\det(A)=\pm1$, and from (22.1.1) that $\gamma\ge1$ or $\gamma\le-1$. Consider the sets

$$L_m^+ = \{A\in L_m:\det(A)=1\},$$

$$L_m^{\uparrow}=\{A\in L_m:\gamma\ge1\},$$

and

$$L_m^{+\uparrow}=L_m^+\cap L_m^{\uparrow}.$$

In the present context, an orientation-preserving (orientation-reversing) map is said to be **proper (improper)**.

## Theorem 22.1.3. *Let $A$ be a map in $L_m$. Then $A$ is in $L_m^{+\uparrow}$ if and only if $A$ is proper.*

*Proof.* Since $\det(A)=+1$, the result follows from Theorem 8.2.8.

Let us recall the convention adopted in Section 4.9 that for a given orthonormal basis $(e_1,\ldots,e_m)$ for $V$, the future time cone $\mathcal T^+$ is defined to be the one containing the timelike unit vector $e_m$.

## Theorem 22.1.4. *If $A$ is a map in $L_m$, then the following are equivalent:*  
(a) $A$ is in $L_m^{\uparrow}$.  
(b) $A(e_m)$ is in $\mathcal T^+$.  
(c) $A$ is orthochronous.

*Proof.* (a) $\Longleftrightarrow$ (b): According to the above convention on $e_m$, we have from (4.9.8) that $A(e_m)$ is in $\mathcal T^+$ if and only if $\langle A(e_m),e_m\rangle<0$. Since $[e_m]=[0\ \cdots\ 0\ 1]^{\mathsf T}$, by Theorem 2.2.4,

$$[A(e_m)]=\begin{bmatrix}[A^1_m]_2\\ \gamma\end{bmatrix},$$

and because $g_{\mathcal E}=\operatorname{diag}(1,\ldots,1,-1)$, by Theorem 3.1.2(a),

$$\langle A(e_m),e_m\rangle=[A(e_m)]^{\mathsf T}g_{\mathcal E}[e_m]=-\gamma.$$

It follows that $\langle A(e_m),e_m\rangle<0$ if and only if $\gamma>0$. Since $\gamma^2\ge1$, we have $\gamma>0$ if and only if $\gamma\ge1$. Thus, $A(e_m)$ is in $\mathcal T^+$ if and only if $\gamma\ge1$.

(b) $\Longleftrightarrow$ (c): Given the above convention on $e_m$, this follows from Theorem 4.9.6.

In view of the preceding two results, we can characterize $L_m^+$, $L_m^{\uparrow}$, and $L_m^{+\uparrow}$ as follows:

$$L_m^+ = \{A \in L_m : A\text{ is proper}\},$$

$$L_m^{\uparrow} = \{A \in L_m : A\text{ is orthochronous}\},$$

and

$$L_m^{+\uparrow} = \{A \in L_m : A\text{ is proper and orthochronous}\}.$$

## Theorem 22.1.5. $L_m^+$, $L_m^{\uparrow}$, and $L_m^{+\uparrow}$ are subgroups of $L_m$.

*Proof.* It is easily shown that $L_m^+$ is a subgroup of $L_m$. Using Theorem 4.9.6, we see that $L_m^{\uparrow}$ is a subgroup of $L_m$. Since the intersection of subgroups is a subgroup, the result for $L_m^{+\uparrow}$ is immediate.

In the physics literature, $L_m^{+\uparrow}$ is called the **proper orthochronous Lorentz group** on $V$.

Recall from Section 2.4 that the orthogonal group $O(m)$ consists of all matrices $P$ in $\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}$ such that $P^\mathsf{T}P=I_m$, and the special orthogonal group $SO(m)$ is the subgroup of $O(m)$ consisting of those matrices $P$ such that $\det(P)=1$.

We say that a map $A$ in $\operatorname{Lin}(V,V)$ is a **rotation** (with respect to $\vartheta$) if $[A]$ is of the form

$$[A]=\begin{bmatrix}[A]^1_1 & O_{(m-1)\times 1}\\ O_{1\times(m-1)} & 1\end{bmatrix},$$

where $[A]^1_1$ is in $SO(m-1)$. By definition, $\det([A]^1_1)=1$, so $\det(A)=1$. The set of rotations is

$$R_m=\{A\in\operatorname{Lin}(V,V): A\text{ a rotation}\}.$$

## Theorem 22.1.6. $R_m$ is a subgroup of $L_m^{+\uparrow}$.

*Proof.* Since $SO(m-1)$ is a group, it is easily shown that so is $R_m$. Let $A$ be a map in $R_m$. We have from $\mathfrak g=\operatorname{diag}(1,\ldots,1,-1)$ that

$$[A]^\mathsf{T}\mathfrak g[A]=\begin{bmatrix}([A]^1_1)^\mathsf{T}[A]^1_1 & O_{(m-1)\times1}\\ O_{1\times(m-1)} & -1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{m-1} & O_{(m-1)\times1}\\ O_{1\times(m-1)} & -1\end{bmatrix}=\mathfrak g.$$

By Theorem 4.4.2, $A$ is a linear isometry, and is therefore in $L_m$. Since $\det(A)=1$, $A$ is in $L_m^+$. By definition, $\gamma=1$, so $A$ is in $L_m^{\uparrow}$. This shows that $A$ is in $L_m^{+\uparrow}$. Thus, $R_m\subseteq L_m^{+\uparrow}$.

## Theorem 22.1.7. If $A$ is a map in $L_m^{+\uparrow}$, then the following are equivalent:

- (a) $A$ is in $R_m$.  
- (b) $\gamma=1$.  
- (c) $[A]^1_2=O_{(m-1)\times1}$.  
- (d) $[A]^2_1=O_{1\times(m-1)}$.

Proof. (b) ⇒ (c): We have from Theorem 22.1.2(d) that

$$(a_m^1)^2 + \cdots + (a_m^{m-1})^2 = 0,$$

from which the result follows.

(c) ⇒ (b): By Theorem 22.1.2(d), $\gamma^2 = 1$, hence $\gamma = \pm 1$. Since $A$ is in $\mathrm{L}_m^\uparrow$, we have $\gamma = 1$.

(c) ⇔ (d): We have from Theorem 22.1.2(d) that

$$(a_m^1)^2 + \cdots + (a_m^{m-1})^2 = (a_1^m)^2 + \cdots + (a_{m-1}^m)^2,$$

from which the result follows.

(a) ⇒ (b): By definition.

(b) ⇒ (a): We have just shown that (b), (c), and (d) are equivalent, so $[A]$ is of the form

$$[A] = \begin{bmatrix} [A]_1^1 & O_{(m-1)\times 1} \\ O_{1\times (m-1)} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

for some matrix $[A]_1^1$ in $\mathrm{Mat}_{(m-1)\times (m-1)}$. By Theorem 22.1.2(a), $[A]_1^1 ([A]_1^1)^\mathrm{T} = I_{m-1}$, hence $[A]_1^1$ is in $\mathrm{O}(m-1)$. Since $A$ is in $\mathrm{L}_m^\uparrow$, we have $\det([A]_1^1) = \det([A]) = 1$, so $[A]_1^1$ is in $\mathrm{SO}(m-1)$. Thus, $A$ is in $\mathrm{R}_m$.

We say that a map $A$ in $\mathrm{Lin}(V,V)$ is a boost (with respect to $\mathcal{E}$ in the $(m-1)$st spacelike direction) if $[A]$ is of the form

$$[A] = \begin{bmatrix} I_{m-2} & O_{(m-2)\times 2} \\ O_{2\times (m-2)} & \begin{bmatrix} \gamma & -\beta\gamma \\ -\beta\gamma & \gamma \end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix},$$

where $\gamma \ge 1$ and $\beta$ is computed using (22.1.2). For convenience of notation, we have defined boosts in the $(m-1)$st spacelike direction, but any other spacelike direction would do. For instance, a boost in the first spacelike direction takes the form

$$\begin{bmatrix} \gamma & O_{1\times (m-2)} & -\beta\gamma \\ O_{(m-2)\times 1} & I_{m-2} & O_{(m-2)\times 1} \\ -\beta\gamma & O_{1\times (m-2)} & \gamma \end{bmatrix}.$$

The set of boosts is

$$\mathrm{B}_m = \{A \in \mathrm{Lin}(V,V) : A \text{ a boost}\}.$$

It is clear that $\mathrm{B}_m$ contains the identity map. Let $A'$ be another boost, with

$$[A'] = \begin{bmatrix} I_{m-2} & O_{(m-2)\times 2} \\ O_{2\times (m-2)} & \begin{bmatrix} \gamma' & -\beta'\gamma' \\ -\beta'\gamma' & \gamma' \end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix}.$$

Then

$$[A][A'] = \begin{bmatrix} I_{m-2} & O_{(m-2)\times 2} \\ O_{2\times (m-2)} & \begin{bmatrix} \gamma'' & -\beta''\gamma'' \\ -\beta''\gamma'' & \gamma'' \end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix},$$

where
\[\gamma'' = \gamma\gamma'(1 + \beta\beta')\quad\text{and}\quad\beta'' = \frac{\beta + \beta'}{1 + \beta\beta'}.\]

It is easily shown that $\gamma'' \ge 1$, and that $\gamma''$ and $\beta''$ satisfy (22.1.2). Thus, $A \circ A'$ is a boost. This demonstrates that $\mathrm{B}_m$ is closed under multiplication. However,

\[\begin{bmatrix} I_{m-2} & O_{(m-2)\times 2} \\ O_{2\times(m-2)} & \begin{bmatrix} \gamma & -\beta\gamma \\ -\beta\gamma & \gamma \end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} I_{m-2} & O_{(m-2)\times 2} \\ O_{2\times(m-2)} & \begin{bmatrix} \gamma & \beta\gamma \\ \beta\gamma & \gamma \end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix}.\]

Since the matrix on the right-hand side is missing the necessary minus signs to make it a boost, $\mathrm{B}_m$ does not contain inverses (except in the case of the identity map). Thus, $\mathrm{B}_m$ is not a group.

## Theorem 22.1.8. $\mathrm{B}_m$ is a subset of $\mathrm{L}_m$.

*Proof.* Let $A$ be a map in $\mathrm{B}_m$. We have from $\mathfrak{g}_\mathcal{E} = \operatorname{diag}(1,\dots,1,-1)$ and the identity in (22.1.3) that

\[[A]^\mathrm{T}\,\mathfrak{g}_\mathcal{E}\,[A] = \begin{bmatrix} I_{m-2} & O_{(m-2)\times 2} \\ O_{2\times(m-2)} & \begin{bmatrix} \gamma & \beta\gamma \\ -\beta\gamma & -\gamma \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \gamma & -\beta\gamma \\ -\beta\gamma & \gamma \end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} I_{m-2} & O_{(m-2)\times 2} \\ O_{2\times(m-2)} & \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix} = \mathfrak{g}_\mathcal{E}.\]

By Theorem 4.4.2, $A$ is a linear isometry and is therefore in $\mathrm{L}_m$.

## Theorem 22.1.9. $\mathrm{R}_m \cap \mathrm{B}_m = \{\mathrm{id}_V\}$.

*Proof.* If $A$ is in $\mathrm{R}_m \cap \mathrm{B}_m$, then, in the above notation,

\[\begin{bmatrix} [A]^1_1 & O_{(m-1)\times 1} \\ O_{1\times(m-1)} & 1 \end{bmatrix} = [A] = \begin{bmatrix} I_{m-2} & O_{(m-2)\times 2} \\ O_{2\times(m-2)} & \begin{bmatrix} \gamma & -\beta\gamma \\ -\beta\gamma & \gamma \end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix},\]

where, by definition, $[A]^1_1$ is in $\mathrm{SO}(m-1)$, $\gamma \ge 1$, and $\beta$ is computed using (22.1.2). It follows that $\gamma = 1$, and then from (22.1.2) that $\beta = 0$. Thus, $[A] = I_m$.
