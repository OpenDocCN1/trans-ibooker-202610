
implicit in the way (7.1.1) is presented. Setting \((w_1,w_2,w_3)=(v_1,v_3,v_2)\), we have from (7.1.1) that

$$\sigma(\mathcal A)(v_1,v_3,v_2)=\sigma(\mathcal A)(w_1,w_2,w_3)=\mathcal A(w_{\sigma(1)},w_{\sigma(2)},w_{\sigma(3)})$$
$$=\mathcal A(w_2,w_3,w_1)=\mathcal A(v_3,v_2,v_1).$$

In most of the computations that follow, the indices are sequential, thereby avoiding this issue.

## Theorem 7.1.1.
Let $V$ be a vector space, let $\sigma,\rho$ be permutations in $S_s$, and let $\mathcal A$ be a tensor in $T_s^0(V)$. Then

$$(\sigma\rho)(\mathcal A)=\sigma(\rho(\mathcal A)).$$

*Proof.* Let $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ be vectors in $V$, and let $(w_1,\ldots,w_s)=(v_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(s)})$. Then

$$(\sigma\rho)(\mathcal A)(v_1,\ldots,v_s)=\mathcal A(v_{(\sigma\rho)(1)},\ldots,v_{(\sigma\rho)(s)})=\mathcal A(v_{\sigma(\rho(1))},\ldots,v_{\sigma(\rho(s))})$$
$$=\mathcal A(w_{\rho(1)},\ldots,w_{\rho(s)})=\rho(\mathcal A)(w_1,\ldots,w_s)$$
$$=\rho(\mathcal A)(v_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(s)})=\sigma(\rho(\mathcal A))(v_1,\ldots,v_s).$$

Since $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ were arbitrary, the result follows.

We say that a tensor $\mathcal A$ in $T_s^0(V)$ is **symmetric** if $\sigma(\mathcal A)=\mathcal A$ for all permutations $\sigma$ in $S_s$; that is,

$$\mathcal A(v_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(s)})=\mathcal A(v_1,\ldots,v_s)$$

for all vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ in $V$. We denote the set of symmetric tensors in $T_s^0(V)$ by $\Sigma_s(V)$. It is easily shown that $\Sigma_s(V)$ is subspace of $T_s^0(V)$.

A tensor $\mathcal B$ in $T_s^0(V)$ is said to be **alternating** if $\tau(\mathcal B)=-\mathcal B$ for all transpositions $\tau$ in $S_s$, or equivalently, if

$$\mathcal B(v_1,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_s)=-\mathcal B(v_1,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_s)$$

for all vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ in $V$ for all $1\le i<j\le s$. The set of alternating tensors in $T_s^0(V)$ is denoted by $\Lambda^s(V)$. It is readily demonstrated that $\Lambda^s(V)$ is a subspace of $T_s^0(V)$. An element of $\Lambda^s(V)$ is called an **s-covector** or **multicovector**. When $s=1$, the alternating criterion is vacuous, and a 1-covector is simply a covector. Thus,

$$\Lambda^1(V)=V^*.$$ \tag{7.1.2}

Since $\Lambda^s(V)$ is a subspace of $T_s^0(V)$, the **zero multivector** in $\Lambda^s(V)$ is precisely the zero tensor in $T_s^0(V)$. A multivector in $\Lambda^s(V)$ is **nonzero** when it is nonzero as a tensor in $T_s^0(V)$. To be consistent with (5.1.2), we define

$$\Lambda^0(V)=\mathbb R.$$ \tag{7.1.3}

With these definitions, the determinant function $\det:(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times1})^m\to\mathbb R$ is seen to be a multivector in $\Lambda^m(\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times1})$.

There are several equivalent ways to characterize multivectors in $\Lambda^s(V)$.

## Theorem 7.1.2.
Let $V$ be a vector space and let $\eta$ be a tensor in $T^0_s(V)$. Then the following are equivalent:

- (a) $\eta$ is a multivector in $\Lambda^s(V)$.
- (b) $\sigma(\eta)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\eta$ for all permutations $\sigma$ in $S_s$.
- (c) If $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ are vectors in $V$ and two (or more) of them are equal, then $\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_s)=0$.
- (d) If $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ are vectors in $V$ and $\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_s)\ne0$, then $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ are linearly independent.

**Proof.** The proof is similar to that of Theorem 2.4.2.

$(a)\Rightarrow(b)$: Let $\sigma=\tau_1\cdots\tau_k$ be a decomposition of $\sigma$ into transpositions. By Theorem 7.1.1,

$$\sigma(\eta)=(\tau_1\cdots\tau_k)(\eta)=(\tau_1\cdots\tau_{k-1})(\tau_k(\eta))$$
$$\qquad=(\tau_1\cdots\tau_{k-1})(-\eta)=-(\tau_1\cdots\tau_{k-1})(\eta).$$

Repeating the process $k-1$ more times gives $\sigma(\eta)=(-1)^k\eta$. By Theorem B.2.3, $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)=(-1)^k$.

$(b)\Rightarrow(a)$: If $\tau$ is a transposition in $S_s$, then, by Theorem B.2.3, $\tau(\eta)=\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\eta=-\eta$.

$(a)\Rightarrow(c)$: If $v_i=v_j$ for some $1\le i<j\le s$, then

$$\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_s)=\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_s).$$

On the other hand, since $\eta$ is alternating,

$$\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_s)=-\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_s).$$

Thus,

$$\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_s)=-\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_s),$$

from which the result follows.

$(c)\Rightarrow(a)$: For all $1\le i<j\le s$, we have

$$0=\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_i+v_j,\ldots,v_i+v_j,\ldots,v_s)$$
$$\quad=\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_s)+\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_s)$$
$$\quad+\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_s)+\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_s)$$
$$\quad=\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_s)+\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_j,\ldots,v_i,\ldots,v_s),$$

from which the result follows.

To prove $(c)\Leftrightarrow(d)$, we replace the assertion in part (d) with the following logically equivalent assertion:

$(d')$: If $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ are linearly dependent vectors in $V$, then $\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_s)=0$.

$(c)\Rightarrow(d')$: Since $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ are linearly dependent, one of them can be expressed as a linear combination of the others, say, $v_1=\sum_{i=2}^s a^i v_i$. Then

$$\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_s)=\eta\left(\sum_{i=2}^s a^i v_i,v_2,\ldots,v_s\right)=\sum_{i=2}^s a^i\eta(v_i,v_2,\ldots,v_s).$$

Since $\eta(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_s)$ has $v_i$ in (at least) two positions, $\eta(v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_s)=0$ for $i=2,\ldots,s$. Thus, $\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_s)=0$.

$(d')\Rightarrow(c)$: If two (or more) of $v_1,\ldots,v_s$ are equal, then they are linearly dependent, so $\eta(v_1,\ldots,v_s)=0$.

We now introduce a way of associating a multivector to a given tensor. Let $V$ be a vector space. **Alternating map** is the family of linear maps

$$\operatorname{Alt}:\mathcal{T}^0_s(V)\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}^0_s(V)$$

defined for $s\ge 0$ by

$$\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{A})=\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_s}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,\sigma(\mathcal{A}) \tag{7.1.4}$$

for all tensors $\mathcal{A}$ in $\mathcal{T}^0_s(V)$.

## Theorem 7.1.3.
Let $V$ be a vector space, let $\mathcal{A}$ be a tensor in $\mathcal{T}^0_s(V)$, let $\sigma$ be a permutation in $S_s$, and let $\eta$ be a multivector in $\Lambda^s(V)$. Then:

(a) $\sigma(\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{A}))=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{A})=\operatorname{Alt}(\sigma(\mathcal{A}))$.

(b) $\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{A})$ is a multivector in $\Lambda^s(V)$.

(c) $\operatorname{Alt}(\eta)=\eta$.

(d) $\operatorname{Alt}(\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{A}))=\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{A})$.

(e) $\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{T}^0_s(V))=\Lambda^s(V)$.

**Proof.** (a): We have

$$\begin{aligned}
\sigma(\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{A}))
&=\sigma\left(\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{\rho\in S_s}\operatorname{sgn}(\rho)\,\rho(\mathcal{A})\right) &&\text{[(7.1.4)]}\\
&=\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{\rho\in S_s}\operatorname{sgn}(\rho)\,\sigma(\rho(\mathcal{A}))\\
&=\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{\rho\in S_s}\operatorname{sgn}(\rho)\,(\sigma\rho)(\mathcal{A}) &&\text{[Th 7.1.1]}\\
&=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{\rho\in S_s}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\rho)\,(\sigma\rho)(\mathcal{A}) &&\text{[Th B.2.2]}\\
&=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{A}), &&\text{[(7.1.4)]}
\end{aligned}$$

where the last equality follows from the observation that as $\rho$ varies over $S_s$, so does $\sigma\rho$. We also have

$$\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Alt}(\sigma(\mathcal{A}))
&=\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{\rho\in S_s}\operatorname{sgn}(\rho)\,\rho(\sigma(\mathcal{A})) &&\text{[(7.1.4)]}\\
&=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{\rho\in S_s}\operatorname{sgn}(\rho\sigma)\,(\rho\sigma)(\mathcal{A}) &&\text{[Th 7.1.1, Th B.2.2]}\\
&=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{Alt}(\mathcal{A}).
\end{aligned}$$

where the last equality is justified as above.

(b): For all transpositions τ in S_s, it follows from part (a) that τ(Alt(A)) = −Alt(A), so Alt(A) is alternating.

(c): We have

Alt(η) = 1/s! ∑_{σ∈S_s} sgn(σ) σ(η)   [(7.1.4)]
= 1/s! ∑_{σ∈S_s} η   [Th 7.1.2(b)]
= η.   [card(S_s) = s!]

(d): This follows from parts (b) and (c).

(e): We have from part (b) that Alt(T_s^0(V)) ⊆ Λ^s(V). Since Λ^s(V) ⊆ T_s^0(V), by part (c),

Λ^s(V) = Alt(Λ^s(V)) ⊆ Alt(T_s^0(V)).

In view of Theorem 7.1.3(b), we can replace the map in (7.1.4) with
Alt : T_s^0(V) ⟶ Λ^s(V).

## 7.2 Wedge Products

In Section 5.1, we introduced a type of multiplication of tensors called the tensor product. Our next task is to define a corresponding operation for multicovectors.

Let V be a vector space. Wedge product is the family of linear maps
∧ : Λ^s(V) × Λ^{s'}(V) ⟶ Λ^{s+s'}(V)

defined for s, s' ≥ 0 by

η ∧ ζ = (s + s')! / (s! s'!) Alt(η ⊗ ζ)   (7.2.1)

for all multicovectors η in Λ^s(V) and ζ in Λ^{s'}(V). That is,

(η ∧ ζ)(v₁, …, v_{s+s'}) = (s + s')! / (s! s'!) [ 1/(s + s')! ∑_{σ∈S_{s+s'}} sgn(σ) σ(η ⊗ ζ)(v_{σ(1)}, …, v_{σ(s+s')}) ] = 1/(s! s'!) ∑_{σ∈S_{s+s'}} sgn(σ) η(v_{σ(1)}, …, v_{σ(s)}) ζ(v_{σ(s+1)}, …, v_{σ(s+s')})

for all vectors v₁, …, v_{s+s'} in V, where the first equality follows from (7.2.1), and the second equality from (5.1.4) and (7.1.1).
