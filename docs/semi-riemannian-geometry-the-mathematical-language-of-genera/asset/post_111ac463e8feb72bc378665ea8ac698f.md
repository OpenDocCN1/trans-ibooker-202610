
*Proof.* Let \(\mathcal{H}\) be a basis for \(V\). We have from Theorem 4.4.2 that \(\det(g_{\mathcal{H}})=\det(g_{\mathcal{H}})\det(A)^2\), and from Theorem 3.3.3 that \(\det(g_{\mathcal{H}})\ne 0\). Thus, \(\det(A)^2=1\), from which the result follows.

Linear isometries are of special interest because they “preserve norms”, as the next result shows.

**Theorem 4.4.4.**

Let \((V,\mathfrak{q})\) be a scalar product space, and let \(A:V\longrightarrow V\) be a linear map. Then:

- (a) If \(A\) is a linear isometry, then \(\|A(v)\|=\|v\|\) for all vectors \(v\) in \(V\).
- (b) If \(\|A(v)\|=\|v\|\) for all vectors \(v\) in \(V\), and \(A\) maps spacelike (resp., timelike, lightlike) vectors to spacelike (resp., timelike, lightlike) vectors, then \(A\) is a linear isometry.

*Proof.* (a): We have

\[
\|A(v)\|=\sqrt{\langle A(v),A(v)\rangle}=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}=\|v\|.
\]

(b): Since \(\|A(v)\|=\|v\|\) is equivalent to \(\langle A(v),A(v)\rangle=\langle v,v\rangle\), the assumption regarding the way \(A\) maps vectors yields \(\langle A(v),A(v)\rangle=\langle v,v\rangle\).

**Theorem 4.4.5.**

Let \((V,\mathfrak{q})\) be a scalar product space, and let \(A:V\longrightarrow V\) be a linear map. Then \(A\) is a linear isometry if and only if \(A^{\dagger}\) is a linear isometry.

*Proof.* (\(\Rightarrow\)): By Theorem 4.4.2, \(A\) is a linear isomorphism and \(A^{\dagger}=A^{-1}\). For vectors \(v,w\) in \(V\), we then have

\[
\begin{aligned}
\langle A^{\dagger}(v),A^{\dagger}(w)\rangle &= \langle v,A^{\dagger}(A^{\dagger}(w))\rangle && [\mathrm{Th}\ 4.3.1(a)]\\
&= \langle v,A\circ A^{\dagger}(w)\rangle && [\mathrm{Th}\ 4.3.2(c)]\\
&= \langle v,w\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\]

(\(\Leftarrow\)): This follows from Theorem 4.3.2(c) and (\(\Rightarrow\)).

Let \((V,\mathfrak{q})\) be a scalar product space of dimension \(m\) and index \(\nu\), let \(\mathcal{E}\) be an orthonormal basis for \(V\), and let \(A:V\longrightarrow V\) be a linear isometry. To simplify notation, we denote \([A]_{\mathcal{E}}^{\mathcal{E}}\) by \([A]\) and adopt similar abbreviations for other matrices. Consider the partition of \([A]\) given by

- \([A_2^2]\quad \nu\times\nu\).

## 4.5 Dual Scalar Product Spaces

In this section, we show that a scalar product on a vector space induces a corresponding scalar product on its dual space.

Let \((V,\mathfrak{g})\) be a scalar product space, let \(V^*\) be its dual space, and let \(F\) and \(S\) be the flat map and sharp map corresponding to \(\mathfrak{g}\). We define a function

\[
\mathfrak{g}^* = \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^* : V^*\times V^*\to\mathbb{R}
\]

by

\[
\langle\eta,\zeta\rangle^* = \langle\eta^S,\zeta^S\rangle
\tag{4.5.1}
\]

for all covectors \(\eta,\zeta\) in \(V^*\). According to (3.3.1), \(\langle\eta^S,\zeta^S\rangle=(\eta^S)^F(\zeta^S)=\eta(\zeta^S)\), hence

\[
\langle\eta,\zeta\rangle^* = \eta(\zeta^S).
\tag{4.5.2}
\]

**Theorem 4.5.1.** *With the above setup, \((V^*,\mathfrak{g}^*)\) is a scalar product space, called the dual scalar product space corresponding to \((V,\mathfrak{g})\).*

*Proof.* Clearly, \(\mathfrak{g}^*\) is bilinear and symmetric. Suppose \(\eta\) is a covector in \(V^*\) such that \(\langle\eta,\zeta\rangle^*=0\), that is, \(\langle\eta^S,\zeta^S\rangle=0\), for all covectors \(\zeta\) in \(V^*\). Since \(S\) is surjective, as \(\zeta\) varies over \(V^*\), \(\zeta^S\) varies over \(V\). Because \(\mathfrak{g}\) is nondegenerate, \(\eta^S=0\); and since \(S\) is injective, by Theorem 1.1.12, \(\eta=0\). Thus, \(\mathfrak{g}^*\) is nondegenerate.

Let \(\mathcal{F}\) and \(\mathcal{S}\) be the flat map and sharp map corresponding to \(\mathfrak{g}^*\). We have from the identification \(V^{**}=V\) that

\[
\mathcal{F}: V^* \longrightarrow V \quad \text{and} \quad \mathcal{S}: V \longrightarrow V^*.
\]

Perhaps the more obvious choice of notation would be \(\mathrm{F}^*\) instead of \(\mathcal{F}\), and \(\mathrm{S}^*\) instead of \(\mathcal{S}\). However, looking back at Theorem 3.3.5, this would confuse what we now denote by \(\mathcal{F}\) and \(\mathcal{S}\) with the pullback by \(\mathrm{F}\) and the pullback by \(\mathrm{S}\).

**Theorem 4.5.2.** If \((V,\mathfrak{g})\) is a scalar product space, and \(\mathrm{F}\) and \(\mathrm{S}\) are the flat map and sharp map corresponding to \(\mathfrak{g}\), then:

- (a) \(\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{S}\).
- (b) \(\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{F}\).

*Proof.* (a): For covectors \(\eta,\zeta\) in \(V^*\), we have

\[
\begin{aligned}
\eta^{\mathcal{F}}(\zeta)&=\langle\eta,\zeta\rangle^* \quad [(3.3.1)]\\
&=\langle\eta^{\mathrm{S}},\zeta^{\mathrm{S}}\rangle \quad [(4.5.1)]\\
&=\langle\zeta^{\mathrm{S}},\eta^{\mathrm{S}}\rangle\\
&=(\zeta^{\mathrm{S}})^{\mathrm{F}}(\eta^{\mathrm{S}}) \quad [(3.3.1)]\\
&=\zeta(\eta^{\mathrm{S}})\\
&=\eta^{\mathrm{S}}(\zeta). \quad [(1.2.3)]
\end{aligned}
\]

Since \(\eta\) and \(\zeta\) were arbitrary, \(\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{S}\).

(b): We have

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{S}&=\mathcal{F}^{-1} \quad [(3.3.5)]\\
&=\mathrm{S}^{-1} \quad [\text{part (a)}]\\
&=\mathrm{F}. \quad [(3.3.5)]
\end{aligned}
\]

**Theorem 4.5.3.** Let \((V,\mathfrak{g})\) be a scalar product space, let \(\mathcal{H}=(h_1,\dots,h_m)\) be a basis for \(V\), let \(\Theta=(\theta^1,\dots,\theta^m)\) be its dual basis, and let \(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}=[\mathfrak{g}^{ij}]\). Then:

- (a) \(\theta^{j\mathrm{S}}=\sum_i \mathfrak{g}^{ij} h_i\) for \(j=1,\dots,m\).
- (b) \([\mathrm{S}]_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{H}}=\mathfrak{g}_{\Theta}^*=\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}\).

*Proof.* (b): Since a scalar product is symmetric, the same is true of its matrix with respect to a given basis. Accordingly, the transpose in Theorem 3.3.1(b) can be dropped. We then have

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathfrak{g}_{\Theta}^{*} &= [\mathcal{F}]_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{H}} && \text{[Th 3.3.1(b)]}\\
&= [\mathrm{S}]_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{H}} && \text{[Th 4.5.2(a)]}\\
&= [\mathrm{F}^{-1}]_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{H}}\\
&= \left([\mathrm{F}]_{\mathcal{H}}^{\Theta}\right)^{-1} && \text{[Th 2.2.5(b)]}\\
&= \mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}. && \text{[Th 3.3.1(b)]}
\end{aligned}
\]

(a): This follows from (2.2.2), (2.2.3), and part (b).

**Theorem 4.5.4 (Raising an Index).**

Let \((V,g)\) be a scalar product space, let \(\mathcal{H}=(h_1,\ldots,h_m)\) be a basis for \(V\), and let \(\Theta=(\theta^1,\ldots,\theta^m)\) be its dual basis. Let \(g_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}=[g^{ij}]\), and let \(\eta\) be a covector in \(V^*\), with

\[
\eta=\sum_i a_i\theta^i.
\tag{4.5.3}
\]

Then

\[
\eta^{\mathrm{S}}=\sum_i a^i h_i,
\]

where

\[
a^i=\sum_j g^{ij}a_j.
\tag{4.5.4}
\]

for \(i=1,\ldots,m\). Expressed more succinctly:

\[
[\eta^{\mathrm{S}}]_{\mathcal{H}}=g_{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}[\eta]_{\Theta}.
\]

*Proof.* This follows from Theorem 3.3.2 and Theorem 4.5.2(a), but it is instructive to work through the details separately. Let \(\zeta\) be a covector in \(V^*\), and observe that \(g_{\Theta}^{*}=\left[\langle\theta^i,\theta^j\rangle^*\right]\). Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
\eta^{\mathrm{S}}(\zeta) &= \eta^{\mathcal{F}}(\zeta) && \text{[Th 4.5.2(a)]}\\
&=\langle\eta,\zeta\rangle^* && \text{[(3.3.1)]}\\
&=\langle\zeta,\eta\rangle^*\\
&=\left\langle\sum_i\zeta(h_i)\theta^i,\sum_j a_j\theta^j\right\rangle^* && \text{[Th 1.2.1(e)]}\\
&=\sum_{ij}\zeta(h_i)a_j\langle\theta^i,\theta^j\rangle^*\\
&=\sum_{ij}g^{ij}a_jh_i(\zeta) && \text{[(1.2.3), Th 4.5.3(b)]}\\
&=\left(\sum_i\left[\sum_jg^{ij}a_j\right]h_i\right)(\zeta)\\
&=\left(\sum_i a^ih_i\right)(\zeta).
\end{aligned}
\]
