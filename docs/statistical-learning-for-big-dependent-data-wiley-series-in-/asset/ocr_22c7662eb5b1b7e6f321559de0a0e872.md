# 3.2.2 Some Special Cases

The structure of the coefficient matrices \(\phi_i\) of a VAR(\(p\)) model may convey useful information about the underlying time series. We consider some special cases in this section.

**Case 1: TFM.** In this case, without loss of generality, we assume that \(z_t\) can be partitioned into \(z_{1t}\) and \(z_{2t}\) such that the model becomes

\(\begin{bmatrix} \phi_{11}(B) & \mathbf{0} \\ \phi_{21}(B) & \phi_{22}(B) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} z_{1t} \\ z_{2t} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{1t} \\ a_{2t} \end{bmatrix} . \quad (3.22)\)

Here \(z_{1t}\) is the input vector and follows the VAR model \(\phi_{11}(B)z_{1t} = a_{1t}\). The output vector is \(z_{2t}\) and satisfies the equation

\(\phi_{22}(B)z_{2t} = -\phi_{21}(B)z_{1t} + a_{2t} = -\phi_{21}(B)[\phi_{11}(B)]^{-1}a_{1t} + a_{2t}. \quad (3.23)\)

However, Eq. (3.23) is not in a TF formulation, because \(a_{2t}\) and \(a_{1t}\) might be correlated. To put the model in a TF form, we need to perform some orthogonalization. Specifically, consider the multivariate multiple linear regression

\(a_{2t} = \beta a_{1t} + e_t, \quad (3.24)\)

and obtain the least squares estimate of \(\beta\), denoted by \(\hat{\beta}\). The residual series \(\hat{e}_t = a_{2t} - \hat{\beta}a_{1t}\) is orthogonal to \(a_{1t}\). Consequently, we have a TFM

\(z_{2t} = [\phi_{22}(B)]^{-1}[-\phi_{21}(B) + \hat{\beta}\phi_{11}(B)]z_{1t} + [\phi_{22}(B)]^{-1}\hat{e}_t. \quad (3.25)\)

In practice, one might be interested in testing the null hypothesis \(H_0 : \phi_{12}(B) = \mathbf{0}\) versus the alternative hypothesis \(H_a : \phi_{12}(B) \neq \mathbf{0}\). If the distribution of \(a_t\) is known, e.g. multivariate Gaussian, then the likelihood ratio test can be used. This is referred to as the *Granger causality* test in the literature. For the test, the full model is the \(k\)-dimensional VAR(\(p\)) model and the reduced model is in Eq. (3.22). The resulting likelihood ratio statistic would asymptotically follow a \(\chi^2_{df}\), where the degrees of freedom \(d = pk_1k_2\) with \(k_i\) being the dimension of \(z_{it}\). See, for instance, Tsay (2014) for further information.

There are multiple advantages in using VAR models directly in handling TFMs. First, both \(z_{1t}\) and \(z_{2t}\) are random vectors so the approach can treat multiple inputs and multiple outputs without the requirement of pre-whitening. Second, there is no need to identify which variable is input and which one is output in data analysis. Third, one can perform the Granger causality test to confirm the unidirectional relationship from \(z_{1t}\) to \(z_{2t}\). On the other hand, the model in Eq. (3.25) might not be parsimonious for the true underlying TFM.

**Case 2:** Block structure: If \(\phi_{21}(B)\) of Eq. (3.22) is also zero, then \(z_{1t}\) and \(z_{2t}\) follow two separate VAR models, even though the two vector series might be contemporaneously correlated. By contemporaneous correlations, we mean the correlations among elements of \(a_t\). In the special case that \(\phi(B)\) is a diagonal matrix, then \(z_t\) consists of \(k\) scalar AR time series that are not dynamically dependent.
