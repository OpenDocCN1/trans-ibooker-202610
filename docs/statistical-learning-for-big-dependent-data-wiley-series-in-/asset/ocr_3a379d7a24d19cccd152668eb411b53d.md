# ### 1.3.1 Sample Mean

An unbiased estimator of the population mean of the scalar process \(z_{it}\) is the sample mean

```
\(\bar{z}_{\cdot i} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T z_{it}.\) (1.13)
```

Putting them together, the sample mean of the vector process \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\) is

```
\(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}} = (\bar{z}_1, \dots, \bar{z}_k)' = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \boldsymbol{z}_t.\) (1.14)
```

For an independent and identically distributed random sample, it is easy to see that \(E(\bar{z}_i) = E(z_{it}) = \mu_i\); and the variance of \(\bar{z}_i\) is \(\sigma_i^2 / T\), where \(\sigma_i^2 = \text{Var}(z_{it})\). Therefore, the sample mean in this case is a consistent estimator of the population mean \(\mu_i\) as \(T \to \infty\).

Note that for a stationary process, the consistency of the sample mean does not necessarily hold. For example, let \(z_{11}\) be a random sample from a normal distribution with mean zero and variance \(\sigma^2\). Let \(z_{1t} = z_{11}\) for \(t = 2, \dots, T\). This special process \(\{z_{1t}\}\) is stationary because (1) \(E(z_{1t}) = 0\), which is time-invariant, (2) \(\text{Var}(z_{1t}) = \sigma^2\), which is also time-invariant, and \(\text{Cov}(z_{1t}, z_{1t-j}) = \sigma^2\), which is time-invariant for all \(j\). However, in this particular case we have \(\bar{z}_{\cdot 1} = z_{11}\) and \(\text{Var}(\bar{z}_{\cdot 1}) = \sigma_1^2\), no matter what the sample size \(T\) is. Therefore, the sample mean is not a consistent estimator for this special process as \(T \to \infty\).

The condition under which the sample mean \(\bar{z}_{\cdot i}\) is a consistent estimator of the population mean \(\mu_i\) is referred to as the *ergodic* condition of a stochastic process. The ergodic theory is concerned with conditions under which the time average of a long realization (sample mean) converges to the space average (average of many random samples at a given time index) of a dynamic system. For a scalar weakly stationary \(z_t\), the ergodic condition for \(\bar{z}\) to converge to \(\mu = E(z_t)\) is that \(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} |\gamma_i| < \infty\). We assume this condition in the book from now on.

For a weakly stationary vector process \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\), the covariance matrix of the sample mean \(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}\) is given by

```
\(\begin{aligned} \text{Var}(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}}) &= E \left[ (\bar{\boldsymbol{z}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\bar{\boldsymbol{z}} - \boldsymbol{\mu})' \right] = E \left[ \left\{ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T (\boldsymbol{z}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \right\} \left\{ \frac{1}{T} \sum_{j=1}^T (\boldsymbol{z}_j - \boldsymbol{\mu})' \right\} \right] \\ &= \frac{1}{T^2} \sum_{t=1}^T E \left[ (\boldsymbol{z}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \left\{ \sum_{j=1}^T (\boldsymbol{z}_j - \boldsymbol{\mu})' \right\} \right] = \frac{1}{T^2} \left[ \sum_{|j-t| \le T-1} (T - |j|)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(j) \right] . \end{aligned}\) (1.15)
```

This implies that the asymptotic variance of the scalar sample mean \(\bar{z}_{\cdot i}\) is

```
\(T \times \text{Var}(\bar{z}_{\cdot i}) \to \gamma_{ii}(0) + 2 \sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty} \gamma_{ii}(\ell), \quad \text{as } T \to \infty.\) (1.16)
```

where, again, \(\gamma_{ii}(\ell)\) is the lag-\(\ell\) autocovariance of \(z_{it}\). From Equation (1.2), if \(\{z_{it}\}\) is white noise the variance of the sample mean \(\bar{z}_{\cdot i}\) is \(\sigma_i^2 / T\). In general, we have

```
\(\text{Var}(\bar{z}_{\cdot i}) \approx \frac{1}{T} \left[ \gamma_{ii}(0) + 2 \sum_{l=1}^{T} \gamma_{ii}(l) \right] , \quad \text{as } T \to \infty.\)
```
