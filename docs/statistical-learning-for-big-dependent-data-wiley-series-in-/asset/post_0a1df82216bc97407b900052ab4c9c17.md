
AIC(p) = ln |\hat{\Sigma}_a| + \frac{2pk^2}{T},
BIC(p) = ln |\hat{\Sigma}_a| + \frac{ln(T)pk^2}{T},
Hannan and Quinn (HQ)(p) = ln |\hat{\Sigma}_a| + \frac{2ln(ln(T))pk^2}{T},

where \(\hat{\Sigma}_a\) is the ML estimate of \(\Sigma\) in Eq. (3.32), \(T\) is the sample size, and \(k\) is the dimension. These criteria are the multivariate generalizations of those studied in Section 2.8.4. See Akaike (1973), Schwarz (1978), and Hannan and Quinn (1979). In practice, with a prespecified maximum order \(P\), one selects the order that gives rise to the minimum value of a criterion function. For instance, the order selected by AIC is

\(\hat{p} = \arg \min_{0 \le p \le P} \text{AIC}(p),\)

where \(p=0\) corresponding to fitting a model with constant term only. Under the assumption that \(\mathbf{z}_t\) follows a finite-order VAR model, both BIC and HQ criteria are shown to be consistent, but AIC has some small probability to specify an order greater than the true order \(p\). However, AIC is often used because there exists no true VAR model in any real application. For further discussion of properties of AIC, see Shibata (1980).

### Model checking

Let \(\mathbf{R}_j\) be the lag-\(j\) cross-correlation matrix of the innovation series \(\mathbf{a}_t\) of the VAR(\(p\)) model in Eq. (3.13). For model checking, the null hypothesis of interest is \(H_0 : \mathbf{R}_1 = \cdots = \mathbf{R}_m = \mathbf{0}\) versus the alternative hypothesis \(H_a : \mathbf{R}_i \neq \mathbf{0}\) for some \(1 \le i \le m\), where \(m\) is a prespecified positive integer. Let \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_t\) be the residual series of the model, e.g. the least squares residuals. A natural estimate of \(\mathbf{R}_j\) is

\(\hat{\mathbf{R}}_j = \hat{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2} \hat{\mathbf{C}}_j \hat{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2},\)

where \(\hat{\mathbf{C}}_v\) is the lag-\(v\) residual covariance matrix given by

\(\hat{\mathbf{C}}_v = \frac{1}{T-p} \sum_{t=p+v+1}^T \hat{\mathbf{a}}_t \hat{\mathbf{a}}_{t-v}, \quad v = 0, 1, \dots,\)

and \(\hat{\mathbf{D}} = \text{diag}\{\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{0,11}, \dots, \hat{\mathbf{C}}_{0,kk}\}\), consisting of the diagonal elements of \(\hat{\mathbf{C}}_0\). A commonly used test statistic is the multivariate portmanteau statistic defined as

\(Q_k(m) = T^2 \sum_{\ell=1}^m \frac{1}{T-\ell} \text{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{R}}'_\ell \hat{\mathbf{R}}_0^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{R}}_\ell \hat{\mathbf{R}}_0^{-1})\)
\(= T^2 \sum_{\ell=1}^m \frac{1}{T-\ell} \text{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{R}}'_\ell \hat{\mathbf{R}}_0^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{R}}_\ell \hat{\mathbf{R}}_0^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{D}}^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{D}})\)
\(= T^2 \sum_{\ell=1}^m \frac{1}{T-\ell} \text{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{R}}'_\ell \hat{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{D}}^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{R}}_0^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{R}}_0^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{D}} \hat{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{R}}_\ell \hat{\mathbf{D}}\hat{\mathbf{D}}^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{R}}_0^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{D}}^{-1})\)
\(= T^2 \sum_{\ell=1}^m \frac{1}{T-\ell} \text{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{C}}'_\ell \hat{\mathbf{C}}_0^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{C}}_\ell \hat{\mathbf{C}}_0^{-1}).\)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 3.2, one can show that the test statistic $Q_k(m)$ is asymptotically distributed as $\chi_d^2$ distribution with degrees of freedom $d = (m-p)k^2$ provided that \(m > p\). Readers are referred to Li and McLeod (1981) and Hosking (1981) for further information.

### Model simplification

One of the difficulties in multivariate time series modeling is that a fitted model often contains many parameters that are highly correlated, but marginally insignificant at the usual 5% level. Model simplification becomes a necessity. However, it would be too tedious to remove insignificant parameters one by one. There are several ways to overcome this difficulty. One approach is to use regularization such as the \(\ell_1\) penalized estimation. We shall discuss this approach in Chapter 7 in analysis of high-dimensional time series. The second approach is *thresholding* by simultaneously setting all parameters with \(t\)-ratios less than a given threshold to zero. This approach is used by Tsay (2014) and available in the **MTS** package of R. To implement thresholding, care must be exercised in choosing a proper threshold. A large threshold can easily invalidate the adequacy of a fitted model, whereas a small threshold often results in insufficient simplification. Following Tsay (2014), we examine the resulting information criteria after re-fitting the model for a given threshold. If the information criterion, e.g. AIC, increases, then the threshold is too high. On the other hand, if the selected information criterion drops, then one can increase the threshold. Limited experience shows that this thresholding approach works well in many applications.

## 3.2.5 Prediction

Consider the VAR(\(p\)) model in Eq. (3.13). Suppose that one is interested in predicting \(z_{n+h}\) at the forecast origin \(t = n\). That is, given the information available at time \(t = n\), denoted by \(F_t\), one wants to learn \(P(z_{n+h}|F_t)\), where \(h\) is a positive integer referring to as the forecast horizon in the literature. For simplicity, one often focuses on \(E(z_{n+h}|F_t)\) and \(\text{Var}(z_{n+h}|F_t)\) instead of the conditional distribution \(P(z_{n+h}|F_t)\). To this end, we use the criterion of minimizing the mean squares of forecast errors (MSFE) and consider two cases. Other forecasting criteria can be used with some complication of no closed-form solution to produce forecasts.

### Case 1

Assume that the model is known, i.e. estimated parameters of the VAR(\(p\)) model are treated as the true ones. From the model, we have

\(z_{n+h} = \phi_0 + \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i z_{n+h-i} + a_{n+h}.\) (3.35)

For \(h = 1\), Eq. (3.35) becomes

\(z_{n+1} = \phi_0 + \phi_1 z_n + \cdots + \phi_p z_{n+1-p} + a_{n+1}.\)

Under the MSFE criterion, the 1-step ahead point forecast is simply

\(z_n(1) = E(z_{n+1}|F_t) = \phi_0 + \phi_1 z_n + \cdots + \phi_p z_{n+1-p},\) (3.36)

and the associated forecast error and its variance are

\(e_n(1) = z_{n+1} - z_n(1) = a_{n+1}, \quad V[e_n(1)] = \Sigma_a.\) (3.37)

By Eqs. (3.36) and (3.37), we have

\(z_{n+1} = z_n(1) + a_{n+1}.\) (3.38)

For \(h = 2\), using Eqs. (3.35) and (3.38), we have

\(z_{n+2} = \phi_0 + \phi_1 z_{n+1} + \phi_2 z_n + \cdots + \phi_p z_{n+2-p} + a_{n+2}\)
\(= \phi_0 + \phi_1[z_n(1) + a_{n+1}] + \phi_2 z_n + \cdots + \phi_p z_{n+2-p} + a_{n+2}\)
\(= \phi_0 + \phi_1 z_n(1) + \phi_2 z_n + \cdots + \phi_p z_{n+2-p} + a_{n+2} + \phi_1 a_{n+1}.\)

It is then clear that the 2-step ahead prediction and its associated error are

\(z_n(2) = E(z_{n+2}|F_n) = \phi_0 + \phi_1 z_n(1) + \phi_2 z_n + \cdots + \phi_p z_{n+2-p}\) (3.39)
\(e_n(2) = z_{n+2} - z_n(2) = a_{n+2} + \phi_1 a_{n+1}\) (3.40)
\(V[e_n(2)] = \Sigma_a + \phi_1 \Sigma_a \phi_1'.\)

In general, one can repeat the process to obtain that, for the \(h\)-step ahead prediction,

\(z_n(h) = \phi_0 + \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i z_n(h-i)\) (3.41)
\(e_n(h) = a_{n+h} + \sum_{j=1}^{h-1} \psi_j a_{n+h-j}\) (3.42)
\(V[e_n(h)] = \Sigma_a + \sum_{j=1}^{h-1} \psi_j \Sigma_a \psi_j',\) (3.43)

where \(\psi_i\) is the \(i\)th \(\psi\)-weight coefficient matrix of \(z_t\); see Eq. (3.19). In Eq. (3.41), it is understood that \(z_n(\ell) = z_{n+\ell}\) for \(\ell \le 0\), i.e. \(z_n(0) = z_n\) and \(z_n(-1) = z_{n-1}\). Eqs. (3.41) and (3.43) imply that the forecasts and their associated covariance matrices of a VAR(\(p\)) model can be calculated recursively.

Using \(\phi_0 = (\mathbf{I} - \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i)\mu\), Eq. (3.41) can be rewritten as

\(z_n(h) - \mu = \sum_{i=1}^p [z_n(h-i) - \mu].\)

Then, by using the \(kp\)-dimensional model representation in Eq. (3.18), one can easily derive that, if \(z_t\) is a stationary time series, we have \(z_n(h) - \mu \to \mathbf{0}\) as \(h \to \infty\). That is, \(z_n(h) \to \mu\) as \(h \to \infty\). This is referred to as the *mean reverting* of a stationary vector AR process. Furthermore, from Eq. (3.43), we have \(\text{V}[e_n(h)] \to \text{V}(z_t)\) as \(h \to \infty\).

### Case 2: Parameter uncertainty

In practice, the coefficient matrices \(\phi_i\) are unknown and one may consider parameter uncertainty in forecasting. For VAR models, the result is relatively simple. Note that the estimates \(\hat{\phi}_i\) and \(\hat{\Sigma}_a\) are available given \(F_n\) so that the model can be written as

\(z_{n+h} = \hat{\phi}_0 + \sum_{i=1}^p \hat{\phi}_i z_{n+h-i} + [\phi_0 - \hat{\phi}_0] + \sum_{i=1}^p [\phi_i - \hat{\phi}_i] z_{n+h-i} + a_{n+h}.\) (3.44)
