
Thus, \(\boldsymbol{w}_t\) follows a stationary VARMA(1,2) model. However, from Eq. (3.67) and denoting the MA matrix polynomial of \(\boldsymbol{w}_t\) by \(\theta^*(B)\), we can see that

\(|\theta^*(1)| = \begin{vmatrix} 1 - 0.5 - 0.005 & -0.5 + 0.245 \\ 0.495 & 1 - 1 - 0.255 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} 0.495 & -0.255 \\ 0.495 & -0.255 \end{vmatrix} = 0.\)

This implies that one is a solution of the determinant equation \(|\theta^*(B)| = 0\). Consequently, the VARMA(1,2) model for \(\boldsymbol{w}_t\) is non-invertible.

The fact that differencing individual components of a co-integrated vector process with co-integrating rank less than the dimension results in a non-invertible VARMA model is referred to as *over-differencing* in the time series literature. Over-differencing can lead to complications in modeling co-integrated processes. For instance, since \(\boldsymbol{w}_t\) is non-invertible, it does not have a finite-order VAR approximation. Therefore, one often needs a high-order VAR model to achieve good approximation in modeling \(\boldsymbol{w}_t\) in practice. Another complication is that the solutions of the ML function of a non-invertible model are on the boundary of the parameter space and, hence, the conventional asymptotic theory for MLE would not apply. Interested readers are referred to Tanaka (2017, chapter 7) and the references therein.

To avoid over-differencing, the idea of error-correction was proposed in Engle and Granger (1987). Details are given in the next section.

## 3.6 ERROR-CORRECTION MODELS

Consider the VARMA model in Eq. (3.1), but assume that 1 might be a solution of the determinant equation \(|\phi(B)| = 0\). In other words, we assume \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\) can be an integrated process of order 1. Let \(\nabla \boldsymbol{z}_t = \boldsymbol{z}_t - \boldsymbol{z}_{t-1} = (I - B)\boldsymbol{z}_t\) be the first-differenced series of \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\). By straightforward algebraic calculation, e.g. Tsay (2014, chapter 5), we have

\(\nabla \boldsymbol{z}_t = \Pi \boldsymbol{z}_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p-1} \Phi_i^* \nabla \boldsymbol{z}_{t-i} + \boldsymbol{c}_0 + \theta(B)\boldsymbol{a}_t, \quad (3.68)\)

where \(\Pi = \sum_{i=1}^p \Phi_i - I = -\phi(1)\) and \(\Phi_i^* = -(\phi_{i+1} + \cdots + \phi_p)\) for \(i = 1, \dots, p-1\). Eq. (3.68) is called an *error-correction model* (ECM) for the integrated process \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\). Note that this model is invertible because we do not alter the MA matrix polynomial. However, if \(|\phi(1)| = 0\), then the coefficient matrix \(\Pi\) is singular. In general, we have the following cases:

- If rank(\(\Pi\)) = 0, then \(\Pi = \mathbf{0}\) and \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\) has \(k\) unit roots. It is not co-integrated.
- If rank(\(\Pi\)) = \(k\), then \(\Pi\) is nonsingular and \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\) is a stationary process.
- If rank(\(\Pi\)) = \(m\) with \(1 \le m < k\), then \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\) has \(\nu = k - m\) unit roots and there exist \(m\) co-integrating vectors. In this case, one can write \(\Pi = \boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}'\), where both \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) are \(k \times m\) real-valued matrices of rank \(m\). Let \(\boldsymbol{w}_t = \boldsymbol{\beta}'\boldsymbol{z}_t\), which is an \(m\)-dimensional stationary process. In other words, columns of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) are the co-integrating vectors of \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\). Note that \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}' = (\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{P})(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\beta})'\) for any \(m \times m\) orthogonal matrix \(\boldsymbol{P}\). Therefore, co-integrating vectors are not uniquely defined if \(m > 1\). The column space of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is unique, however.

The stationarity of \(\boldsymbol{w}_t\) of a co-integrated process \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\) can easily be seen by re-writing the model as
\(\nabla z_t = \alpha w_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p-1} \phi_i^* \nabla z_{t-i} + c_0 + \theta(B) a_t. \tag{3.69}\)

Here \(\alpha\) is of full rank and \(\nabla z_t\) is stationary, \(\boldsymbol{w}_t\) must be stationary, because a stationary process cannot depend on any unit-root process.

The error-correction model of Eq. (3.68) is referred to as ECM in the *transitory form* because the coefficient matrices \(\phi_i^*\) show the transitory effects. An alternative form of ECM is given by
\(\nabla z_t = \Pi z_{t-p} + \sum_{i=1}^{p-1} \phi_i^* \nabla z_{t-i} + c_0 + \theta(B) a_t, \tag{3.70}\)

where \(\Pi\) remains unchanged, i.e. \(\Pi = -\phi(1)\), but \(\phi_i^* = -(\mathbf{I} - \phi_1 - \cdots - \phi_i)\) for \(i=1, \dots, p-1\). These new \(\phi_i^*\) matrices contain the long-run cumulative effects, and the ECM in Eq. (3.70) is referred to as ECM in the *long-run form*.

### 3.6.1 Co-integration Test

Detecting the existence (and the number) of co-integrating vectors of a multivariate process is referred to as *co-integration* test in the econometric literature. There are several approaches available in the literature to co-integration test. For VAR models, the most commonly used co-integration test is the Johansen test (Johansen, 1991; Johansen and Juselius, 1990). The test can be carried out using either ECM of (3.68) or (3.70). We use the transitory form in our discussion and expand the ECM of Eq. (3.68) to include possible time trend. That is,
\(\nabla z_t = \Pi z_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^{p-1} \phi_i^* \nabla z_{t-i} + d_t + a_t, \tag{3.71}\)

where \(d_t = c_0 + c_1 t\) with \(c_0\) and \(c_1\) are \(k\)-dimensional real vectors and other coefficients as the same as those of Eq. (3.68). The co-integration test is then concerned with the rank of the matrix \(\Pi\), which is related to the correlation matrix between \(z_{t-1}\) and \(\nabla z_t\). Assuming multivariate normality, Johansen (1991, 1995) uses the ML test to detect the rank of \(\Pi\). This is essentially based on canonical correlation analysis. Specifically, to mitigate the effect of the stationary part on co-integration test, one considers two preliminary multivariate linear regressions

\(\nabla z_t = \sum_{i=1}^{p-1} \gamma_i \nabla z_{t-i} + d_t + u_t \tag{3.72}\)
\(z_{t-1} = \sum_{i=1}^{p-1} \gamma_i^* \nabla z_{t-i} + d_t^* + v_t \tag{3.73}\)

where a superscript “*” is used for the coefficient matrix of Eq. (3.73), the deterministic trend is pre-specified, and \(u_t\) and \(v_t\) denote the error term, respectively. Let \(\hat{u}_t\) and \(\hat{v}_t\) be the residual of the OLS estimation of Eqs. (3.72) and (3.73), respectively. Then, one considers the multivariate linear regression

\(\hat{u}_t = \Pi \hat{v}_t + \epsilon_t, \quad (3.74)\)

where \(\epsilon_t\) is the error term. The least squares estimates of \(\Pi\) are equivalent for Eqs. (3.71) and (3.74). Therefore, under multivariate normality, one can check the rank of \(\Pi\) by testing the canonical correlation coefficients between \(\hat{u}_t\) and \(\hat{v}_t\). Let \(\lambda_1^2 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_k^2\) be the ordered squared canonical correlations between \(\hat{u}_t\) and \(\hat{v}_t\). Johansen (1991) considered two test statistics for identifying rank(\(\Pi\)).

For \(m = 0, \dots, k-1\), consider the null hypothesis \(H(m): \text{Rank}(\Pi) = m\) versus the alternative hypothesis \(H_a : \text{rank}(\Pi) > m\). The test statistic is

\(L_{tr}(m) = -(T - kp) \sum_{i=m+1}^k \ln(1 - \lambda_i^2), \quad (3.75)\)

where \(T\) is the sample size, \(p\) is the VAR order, \(k\) is the dimension. The idea of the test is that, under the null hypothesis \(H(m)\), \(\text{rank}(\Pi) = m\) so that the largest \(m\) canonical correlations between \(\hat{u}_t\) and \(\hat{v}_t\) are positive, but the remaining \(k - m\) canonical correlations are zero. This test statistic is referred to as the trace test. Unlike the stationary case, the limiting distribution of \(L_{tr}(m)\) under \(H(m)\) is not chi-square. It is a function of standard Brownian motion. For details, see Johansen (1991) and Tsay (2014, chapter 5) and the references therein. In practice, the asymptotic critical values of \(L_{tr}(m)\) are obtained by simulation.

The second test statistic considered by Johansen (1991) is concerned with the test

\(H_0 : \text{Rank}(\Pi) = m \quad \text{vs} \quad \text{Rank}(\Pi) = m + 1.\)

In this case, the test statistic is

\(L_{\max}(m) = -(T - kp) \ln(1 - \lambda_{m+1}^2). \quad (3.76)\)

This test statistic is referred to as the maximum test. Similarly to \(L_{tr}(m)\), the limiting distribution of \(L_{\max}(m)\) is also not chi-square, but a function of the standard Brownian motion.

Once the number of co-integrating vectors \(m\) is determined, the eigenvectors associated with the largest \(m\) eigenvalues \(\{\lambda_i^2 | i = 1, \dots, m\}\) of the canonical correlation analysis between \(\hat{u}_t\) and \(\hat{v}_t\) can be used to estimate the co-integrating vectors.
