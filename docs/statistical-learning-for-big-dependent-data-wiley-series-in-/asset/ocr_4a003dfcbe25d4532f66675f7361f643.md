In applications, both \(\mu_t\) and \(\Phi\) may account for seasonality and \(\mu_t\) may also include the effect of covariates.

The spatial variation of \(Y_t\) is modeled via the columns of the loading matrix \(L\). Write the \(j\)th column of \(L\) as \(L_j = (L_{1j}, \dots, L_{mj})'\) for \(j=1, \dots, r\). The GSDF model assumes that \(L_j\) is a conditionally independent, distance-based Gaussian process (or Gaussian random field). That is,

\(L_j \sim N(\kappa_j, \tau_j^2 R_j), \quad j=1, \dots, r,\quad (9.105)\)

where \(\kappa_t\) is an \(m\)-dimensional mean vector, \(\tau_j\) is a scaling parameter, and \(R_j = [r_{uv}(\phi_j)]\) with \(r_{uv}(\phi_j) = \rho_{\phi_j}(||s_u - s_v||), u, v = 1, \dots, m\) for suitably defined correlation functions \(\rho_{\phi}(.)\) with \(j=1, \dots, r\). For simplicity, the parameters \(\phi_j\) are typically a scalar or a low-dimensional vector. For example, \(\phi_j\) is a scalar if \(\rho_{\phi}\) is the exponential function \(\rho_{\phi_j}(h) = \exp(-h/\phi_j)\) or the spherical function \(\rho_{\phi_j}(h) = [1 - 1.5(h/\phi_j) + 0.5(h/\phi_j)^3]I_{h/\phi_j \le 1}\) with \(h\) denoting distance.

Estimation of the GSDF model in Eqs. (9.103)-(9.105) can be carried out by Markov chain Monte Carlo methods in conjunction with techniques of extended Kalman filter and Metropolis-Hasting algorithm. Of course, important issues to consider in using the GSDF model include the selection of the number of common factors \(r\) and the estimation of factor process \(f_t\). These issues can be addressed with the use of information criteria or under a Bayesian framework. See Lopes et al. (2011) for further details and applications.

# ## 9.8.4 S-T HMs

Assume that the locations \(\{s_1, \dots, s_m\}\) are fixed, where \(s_i \in D \subset R^d\), and that the time points are discrete and equally spaced, say \(t=1, \dots, T\). A general framework for analyzing the S-T data is the hierarchical dynamic S-T model (DSTM) discussed in Cressie and Wikle (2011) and Wikle et al. (2019). See also the review article Harvill (2010).

Under this framework, the data model is

\(Z_t = H_t(Y_t, \theta_{h,t}, \epsilon_t), \quad t=1, \dots, T,\quad (9.106)\)

where \(Z_t\) denotes the observed data at time \(t\), \(Y_t\) is the latent S-T process of interest, \(H_t\) is a linear or nonlinear mapping that connects the data to the latent process, and \(\epsilon_t\) is the data error, which represents measurement error and/or certain small-scale temporal variability. The model parameters are denoted by \(\theta_{h,t}\), which may vary with space (signified by the distance \(h\)) and/or time.

Under the model, one assumes that the data \(\{Z_t\}\) are temporally independent conditioned on the latent process \(Y_t\) and the parameters \(\theta_{h,t}\). Therefore, the joint distribution of the data can be written in product form

\(p[\{Z_t\}_{t=1}^T | \{Y_t\}_{t=1}^T, \{\theta_{h,t}\}_{t=1}^T] = \prod_{t=1}^T p[Z_t | Y_t, \theta_{h,t}].\quad (9.107)\)
