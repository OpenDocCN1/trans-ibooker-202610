
matrix of the common factors, \(\epsilon_t\) is an \(r \times r\) iid Gaussian process with mean zero and covariance \(\Sigma_e > 0\), and \(f_0 \sim N(m_0, C_0)\) with known hyperparameters \(m_0\) and \(C_0\). In applications, both \(\mu_t\) and \(\Phi\) may account for seasonality and \(\mu_t\) may also include the effect of covariates.

The spatial variation of \(Y_t\) is modeled via the columns of the loading matrix \(L\). Write the \(j\)th column of \(L\) as \(L_j = (L_{1j}, \dots, L_{mj})'\) for \(j=1, \dots, r\). The GSDF model assumes that \(L_j\) is a conditionally independent, distance-based Gaussian process (or Gaussian random field). That is,

\(L_j \sim N(\kappa_j, \tau_j^2 R_j), \quad j=1, \dots, r,\quad (9.105)\)

where \(\kappa_j\) is an \(m\)-dimensional mean vector, \(\tau_j\) is a scaling parameter, and \(R_j = [r_{uv}(\phi_j)]\) with \(r_{uv}(\phi_j) = \rho_{\phi_j}(||s_u - s_v||), u, v = 1, \dots, m\) for suitably defined correlation functions \(\rho_{\phi}(.)\) with \(j=1, \dots, r\). For simplicity, the parameters \(\phi_j\) are typically a scalar or a low-dimensional vector. For example, \(\phi_j\) is a scalar if \(\rho_{\phi}\) is the exponential function \(\rho_{\phi_j}(h) = \exp(-h/\phi_j)\) or the spherical function \(\rho_{\phi_j}(h) = [1 - 1.5(h/\phi_j) + 0.5(h/\phi_j)^3]I_{h/\phi_j \le 1}\) with \(h\) denoting distance.

Estimation of the GSDF model in Eqs. (9.103)-(9.105) can be carried out by Markov chain Monte Carlo methods in conjunction with techniques of extended Kalman filter and Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. Of course, important issues to consider in using the GSDF model include the selection of the number of common factors \(r\) and the estimation of factor process \(f_t\). These issues can be addressed with the use of information criteria or under a Bayesian framework. See Lopes et al. (2011) for further details and applications.

## 9.8.4 S-T HMs

Assume that the locations \(\{s_1, \dots, s_m\}\) are fixed, where \(s_i \in D \subset R^d\), and that the time points are discrete and equally spaced, say \(t=1, \dots, T\). A general framework for analyzing the S-T data is the hierarchical dynamic S-T model (DSTM) discussed in Cressie and Wikle (2011) and Wikle et al. (2019). See also the review article Harvill (2010).

Under this framework, the data model is

\(Z_t = H_t(Y_t, \theta_{h,t}, \epsilon_t), \quad t=1, \dots, T,\quad (9.106)\)

where \(Z_t\) denotes the observed data at time \(t\), \(Y_t\) is the latent S-T process of interest, \(H_t\) is a linear or nonlinear mapping that connects the data to the latent process, and \(\epsilon_t\) is the data error, which represents measurement error and/or certain small-scale temporal variability. The model parameters are denoted by \(\theta_{h,t}\), which may vary with space (signified by the distance \(h\)) and/or time.

Under the model, one assumes that the data \(\{Z_t\}\) are temporally independent conditioned on the latent process \(Y_t\) and the parameters \(\theta_{h,t}\). Therefore, the joint distribution of the data can be written in product form

\(p[\{Z_t\}_{t=1}^T | \{Y_t\}_{t=1}^T, \{\theta_{h,t}\}_{t=1}^T] = \prod_{t=1}^T p[Z_t | Y_t, \theta_{h,t}].\quad (9.107)\)

Typically, the conditional distribution of \( Z_t \) is assumed to be normal, but other members of the exponential family can also be used. See the previous section.

Another important issue of applying the DSTM is the decomposition of the joint distribution of the latent S-T process \(\{Y_t\}\). To this end, the technique of time series analysis can be used and we have

\( p[Y_1, ..., Y_T] = \prod_{t=2}^T p[Y_t|Y_{t-1}, ..., Y_1] \times p(Y_1),\) (9.108)

where, for simplicity, the parameters are omitted from the distribution functions. Equation (9.108) provides a natural way to introduce assumptions under which the joint distribution of \(\{Y_t\}\) can easily be calculated. For instance, if one assumes that the first-order Markov property holds, then

\( p[Y_t|Y_{t-1}, ..., Y_1, \{\theta_{h,t}\}_{t=1}^T] = p[Y_t|Y_{t-1}, \theta_{h,t}],\) (9.109)

where, again, for simplicity, we put all parameters (including those for the time evolution of \( Y_t \)) into the parameter vector \( \theta_{h,t} \). Consequently, assume that \( Y_t \) is a first-order Markov process, we have

\( p[Y_T, ..., Y_1|\{\theta_{h,t}\}] = \left( \prod_{t=2}^T p[Y_t|Y_{t-1}, \theta_{h,t}] \right) p[Y_1|\theta_{h,1}].\) (9.110)

Under the first-order Markov property, we can express the model of \( Y_t \) as

\( Y_t = M(Y_{t-1}, \theta_{h,t}, \eta_t),\) (9.111)

where \( M \) denotes the temporal transition and \( \eta_t \) is a spatial noise process that is temporally uncorrelated. The transition \( M \) of Eq. (9.111) can be linear or nonlinear. For instance, \( Y_t \) may follow a VAR(1) model or a threshold VAR(1) model with multiple regimes. Interested readers are referred to Tsay (2014) and Tsay and Chen (2019), for properties of VAR(1) and threshold VAR models, respectively, among other multivariate time series books. The above model can be generalized to any finite-order Markov process so long as the order is small so that the computation remains manageable.

There are two general approaches to estimate DSTM. The first approach is to use the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm of Dempster et al. (1977) and the second approach is the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method. In some non-linear cases, sequential Monte Carlo methods or particle filtering can also be used, especially when the dimension \( m \) is small. Some details can be found in Cressie and Wikle (2011).
