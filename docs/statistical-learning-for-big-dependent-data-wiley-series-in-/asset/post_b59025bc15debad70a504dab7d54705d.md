## 7.5 PARTIAL LEAST SQUARES

Partial least squares (PLS) regression was proposed by H. Wold (1966) and has been widely used in many scientific fields, especially in chemometrics (i.e. computational chemistry). Geladi and Kowalski (1986) provide a tutorial and Abdi (2003) gives a nice introduction of PLS. Ng (2013) considers PLS from an algorithmic point of view. Like PCR, PLS also addresses the singularity difficulty of the \(X'X\) matrix, but it can handle multivariate dependent variables. Therefore, we treat \(Y\) as a \(n \times m\) data matrix in this section. The goal of PLS regression is to predict \(Y\) from \(X\) and to describe their common structure. For readers familiar with multivariate statistical analysis, PLS can be regarded as a method between canonical correlation analysis and PCR of \(Y\) and \(X\). In contrast with PCR, which seeks orthogonal transformations to explain as much as possible the variance of \(X\), PLS finds linear combinations of \(X\) that are also relevant to \(Y\). Specifically, PLS seeks a set of linear transformations that performs a simultaneous decomposition of \(X\) and \(Y\) such that those transformed components explain as much as possible the covariance between \(X\) and \(Y\). Mathematically, PLS can be formulated as follows:

\[
X = OP' + \eta, \tag{7.38}
\]
\[
Y = UQ' + E, \tag{7.39}
\]

where \(\eta\) and \(E\) denote the error terms and it is understood that they can be zero. Similarly to the PCR case, \(O\) and \(U\) are the score matrices and \(P\) and \(Q\) are the loading matrices. PLS then considers the linear regression between \(U\) and \(O\). The idea of PLS is to perform the decompositions in Eqs. (7.38) and (7.39) so that the covariance between \(X\) and \(Y\) is maximized. Intuitively, one must take information from each other into account in performing the decompositions. To gain further insight, it pays to consider the spectral decomposition of PCR in Eq. (7.34). The decomposition can be achieved by an iterative method called the *Nonlinear Iterative Partial Least Squares* (NIPALS) algorithm, which is similar to the power method for obtaining eigenvalue and eigenvector of a positive-definite matrix. Let **_p_** be a column of the loading matrix **_P_** of Eq. (7.34) and **_o_** be its corresponding score vector. From the eigenvalue-eigenvector analysis, we have

\[
X'Xp = \lambda p
\]

Letting

\[
o = Xp
\]

we have

\[
p = \frac{1}{\lambda} X'o
\]

### NIPALS Algorithm for _X_:

- I. Initialization: Let **_o_** be an arbitrary _n_-dimensional vector, e.g. an arbitrary column of _X_.
- II. Let $p = X'o / ||X'o||$,
- III. Update $o = Xp$,

where $\|x\|$ denotes the Euclidean norm of _x_. Iterate step II and step III until **_o_** converges. In other words, the algorithm starts with an initial value of **_o_**, then applies Eq. (7.42) to update **_p_** followed by updating **_o_** using Eq. (7.41). When convergence occurs, one obtains the first principal component **_p_**<sub>1</sub> and the associated scores **_o_**<sub>1</sub>. To find the subsequent components, one set $Y = X - o_1p'_1$ and repeat the algorithm.

The algorithm also applies to PCA of _Y_ to obtain the score matrix **_U_** and loading matrix **_Q_** of Eq. (7.39). The question then is how to take information from each other between _X_ and _Y_ to achieve the goal of PLS. A simple idea is to swap **_o_** and **_u_** in the updating. The resulting NIPALS algorithm is as follows:

### NIPALS Algorithm:

- 1. Initialization: Let **_u_** be an arbitrary starting vector, e.g. **_u_** being an arbitrary column of _Y_.
- 2. Loop:
  - * $p = X'u / ||X'u||$,
  - * $o = Xp$,
  - * $q = Y'o / ||Y'o||$,
  - * $u = Yq$.
- 3. Until **_o_** converges.

If _Y_ is a vector, one can set $q = 1$ and omits the last two steps of the loop. The algorithm gives the first set of PLS components and loadings. For subsequent components and loadings, we define

\[
X = X - op', \quad Y = Y - uq',
\]

and repeat the algorithm.

After $r$ iterations, we obtain two $n \times r$ matrices $\mathbf{O}$ and $\mathbf{U}$ and two loading matrices $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$. We then relate $\mathbf{Y}$ to $\mathbf{X}$ by the linear regression

\[
\mathbf{U} = \mathbf{O}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon},
\]

where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ denotes the error term. The fitted value of $\mathbf{Y}$ is then

\[
\widehat{\mathbf{Y}} = \widehat{\mathbf{U}}\mathbf{Q}' = \mathbf{O}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathbf{Q}' = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{P}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\mathbf{Q}', \quad (7.43)
\]

where $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{XP}$ of PCA is used.

To gain insight into the NIPALS algorithm, consider

\[
\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{u}/\|\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{u}\|
\]
\[
= \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{q}/\|\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{q}\|
\]
\[
= \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}'\mathbf{o}/\|\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}'\mathbf{o}\|
\]
\[
= \mathbf{X}'\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}'\mathbf{X}\mathbf{p}/\|\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}'\mathbf{X}\mathbf{p}\|
\]
\[
= \frac{1}{\lambda}(\mathbf{Y}'\mathbf{X})'(\mathbf{Y}'\mathbf{X})\mathbf{p}. \quad (7.44)
\]

Clearly, $\mathbf{p}$ is an eigenvector of the sample covariance matrix of $\mathbf{Y}'\mathbf{X}$. The algorithm in Eq. (7.44) is exactly the updating rule in the Power method for computing the largest eigenvalue-eigenvector of the symmetric matrix $\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}'\mathbf{X}$.

Besides the NIPALS algorithm, there are alternative methods available to compute the PLS regression. See, for instance, de Jong (1993). Helland (1990) provides an explanation of PLS from a prediction point of view, which is easy to follow when $\mathbf{Y}$ is a scalar dependent variable.

### PLS algorithm 1: (PLS1)

- 1. Define the starting values of the $\mathbf{X}$ residuals, $\boldsymbol{\eta}_0$, and $y$ residuals $\mathbf{f}_0$:

\[
\boldsymbol{\eta}_0 = \mathbf{X} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_x,
\]
\[
\mathbf{f}_0 = \mathbf{Y} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_y,
\]

where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_X$ denotes the matrix consisting of mean vector of $\mathbf{x}_t$ and $\mu_y$ is the vector consisting of mean of dependent variable $y_{t+h}$. In other words, $\boldsymbol{\eta}_0$ and $\mathbf{f}_0$ are the mean-adjusted $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{Y}$.

For $i = 1, 2, \dots$, do the steps 2 to 4 below:

- 2. Introduce the $\mathbf{X}$-scores, $\mathbf{o}_i$, as linear combinations of the $\mathbf{X}$ residuals $\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i-1}$ using the covariances between $\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i-1}$ and $\mathbf{f}_{i-1}$ as weights:

\[
\mathbf{o}_i = \boldsymbol{\eta}'_{i-1}\mathbf{w}_i, \quad \text{with } \mathbf{w}_i = \text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i-1}, \mathbf{f}_{i-1}).
\]

- 3. Determine $\mathbf{X}$ loading $\mathbf{p}_i$ and $\mathbf{Y}$ loading $\mathbf{q}_i$ by least squares:

\[
\mathbf{p}_i = \text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i-1}, \mathbf{o}_i)/\text{Var}(\mathbf{o}_i),
\]
\[
\mathbf{q}_i = \text{Cov}(\mathbf{f}_{i-1}, \mathbf{o}_i)/\text{Var}(\mathbf{o}_i).
\]

That is, regress $\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i-1}$ and $\mathbf{f}_{i-1}$ on $\mathbf{o}_i$ to obtain the LS estimates.
