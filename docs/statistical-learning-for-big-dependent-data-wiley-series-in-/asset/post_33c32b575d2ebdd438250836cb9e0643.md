
## 3.2.2 Some Special Cases

The structure of the coefficient matrices \(\phi_i\) of a VAR(\(p\)) model may convey useful information about the underlying time series. We consider some special cases in this section.

**Case 1: TFM.** In this case, without loss of generality, we assume that \(z_t\) can be partitioned into \(z_{1t}\) and \(z_{2t}\) such that the model becomes

\(\begin{bmatrix} \phi_{11}(B) & \mathbf{0} \\ \phi_{21}(B) & \phi_{22}(B) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} z_{1t} \\ z_{2t} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{1t} \\ a_{2t} \end{bmatrix} . \quad (3.22)\)

Here \(z_{1t}\) is the input vector and follows the VAR model \(\phi_{11}(B)z_{1t} = a_{1t}\). The output vector is \(z_{2t}\) and satisfies the equation

\(\phi_{22}(B)z_{2t} = -\phi_{21}(B)z_{1t} + a_{2t} = -\phi_{21}(B)[\phi_{11}(B)]^{-1}a_{1t} + a_{2t}. \quad (3.23)\)

However, Eq. (3.23) is not in a TF formulation, because \(a_{2t}\) and \(a_{1t}\) might be correlated. To put the model in a TF form, we need to perform some orthogonalization. Specifically, consider the multivariate multiple linear regression

\(a_{2t} = \beta a_{1t} + e_t, \quad (3.24)\)

and obtain the least squares estimate of \(\beta\), denoted by \(\hat{\beta}\). The residual series \(\hat{e}_t = a_{2t} - \hat{\beta}a_{1t}\) is orthogonal to \(a_{1t}\). Consequently, we have a TFM

\(z_{2t} = [\phi_{22}(B)]^{-1}[-\phi_{21}(B) + \hat{\beta}\phi_{11}(B)]z_{1t} + [\phi_{22}(B)]^{-1}\hat{e}_t. \quad (3.25)\)

In practice, one might be interested in testing the null hypothesis \(H_0 : \phi_{12}(B) = \mathbf{0}\) versus the alternative hypothesis \(H_a : \phi_{12}(B) \neq \mathbf{0}\). If the distribution of \(a_t\) is known, e.g. multivariate Gaussian, then the likelihood ratio test can be used. This is referred to as the *Granger causality* test in the literature. For the test, the full model is the \(k\)-dimensional VAR(\(p\)) model and the reduced model is in Eq. (3.22). The resulting likelihood ratio statistic would asymptotically follow a \(\chi^2_{df}\), where the degrees of freedom \(d = pk_1k_2\) with \(k_i\) being the dimension of \(z_{it}\). See, for instance, Tsay (2014) for further information.

There are multiple advantages in using VAR models directly in handling TFMs. First, both \(z_{1t}\) and \(z_{2t}\) are random vectors so the approach can treat multiple inputs and multiple outputs without the requirement of pre-whitening. Second, there is no need to identify which variable is input and which one is output in data analysis. Third, one can perform the Granger causality test to confirm the unidirectional relationship from \(z_{1t}\) to \(z_{2t}\). On the other hand, the model in Eq. (3.25) might not be parsimonious for the true underlying TFM.

**Case 2:** Block structure: If \(\phi_{21}(B)\) of Eq. (3.22) is also zero, then \(z_{1t}\) and \(z_{2t}\) follow two separate VAR models, even though the two vector series might be contemporaneously correlated. By contemporaneous correlations, we mean the correlations among elements of \(a_t\). In the special case that \(\phi(B)\) is a diagonal matrix, then \(z_t\) consists of \(k\) scalar AR time series that are not dynamically dependent.

### Case 3: Hierarchical TF: If $z_t = (z'_{1t}, z'_{2t}, z'_{3t})'$ and satisfies the model

\(\begin{bmatrix} \phi_{11}(B) & 0 & 0 \\ \phi_{21}(B) & \phi_{22}(B) & 0 \\ \phi_{31}(B) & \phi_{32}(B) & \phi_{33}(B) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} z_{1t} \\ z_{2t} \\ z_{3t} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{1t} \\ a_{2t} \\ a_{3t} \end{bmatrix} . \quad (3.26)\)

Then, $z_{1t}$ is an input vector to $z_{2t}$ and both $z_{1t}$ and $z_{2t}$ are input vectors to $z_{3t}$. The implied TFMs can be obtained by the same technique mentioned in Case 1.

### Case 4: Suppose that the VAR model can be decomposed as:

\(z_t = \Phi_*(B)z_{t-1} + \mathbf{a}_t = A(B)C(B)z_{t-1} + \mathbf{a}_t \quad (3.27)\)

where $\Phi_*(B) = \sum_{i=1}^p \Phi_i B^{i-1}$, and $A(B) = \sum_{i=0}^{p_1} A_i B^i$ and $C(B) = \sum_{i=0}^{p_2} C_i B^i$ are matrix polynomial operators of dimensions $k \times r$ and $r \times k$ with degrees $p_1$ and $p_2$, respectively, and $p = p_1 + p_2 + 1$. Letting $y_t = C(B)z_t$ be a $r$-dimensional time series, we can write the model for $z_t$ as
\(z_t = A(B)y_{t-1} + \mathbf{a}_t\).

This is the reduced-rank AR model proposed by Velu et al. (1986) and is useful when $r$ is much smaller than $k$. See also Ahn and Reinsel (1988). It is related to the factor models studied in Chapter 6.

## 3.2.3 Estimation

Given the realizations \(\{z_1, \dots, z_T\}\), one can estimate the VAR(\(p\)) model by the ordinary least squares (OLS) method, the generalized least squares (GLS) method, or the maximum likelihood (ML) method. It turns out that the OLS estimators are the same as the GLS estimators for VAR models and, under the normality assumption, OLS estimators are asymptotically equivalent to the ML estimators. See, for instance, Tsay (2014, chapter 2) and the references therein. Therefore, we shall only consider the OLS estimation here.

The VAR(\(p\)) model in Eq. (3.13) can be written as

\(z'_t = x'_t \beta + a'_t, \quad (3.28)\)

where $x_t = (1, z'_{t-1}, \dots, z'_{t-p})'$ and $\beta' = [\phi_0, \phi_1, \dots, \phi_p]$ is a \((kp + 1) \times p\) coefficient matrix. Using Eq. (3.28) for \(t = p + 1, \dots, T\), we have the following matrix equation of data

\(Z = X\beta + A, \quad (3.29)\)

where the \(i\)th rows of \(Z, X\), and \(A\) are \(z'_{p+i}, x'_{p+i}\), and \(a'_{p+i}\), respectively. The dimensions of \(Z\) and \(A\) are \((T - p) \times k\), whereas that of \(X\) is \((T - p) \times (kp + 1)\). The objective function of the OLS estimation of the model is then
\(S(\beta) = \text{tr}[(Z - X\beta)(Z - X\beta)'],\)

where tr(\(C\)) is the trace of the square-matrix \(C\). Taking partial derivatives of \(S(\beta)\) with respect to \(\beta\), we can obtain a linear system of equations of the first-order conditions from which the OLS estimate is

\(\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}(X'Z) = \left( \sum_{t=p+1}^T x_t x'_t \right)^{-1} \left( \sum_{t=p+1}^T x_t z'_t \right). \quad (3.30)\)

The OLS estimate of \(\Sigma_a\) is

$$\hat{\Sigma}_a = \frac{1}{T-(k+1)p-1} \sum_{t=p+1}^T \hat{a}_t \hat{a}'_t \quad (3.31)$$

where \(\hat{a}_t = z_t - \hat{\phi}_0 - \sum_{i=1}^p \hat{\Phi}_i z_{t-i}\) is the residual series. If ML estimation is used, then the ML estimate of \(\Sigma_a\) is

$$\hat{\Sigma}_a = \frac{1}{T-p} \sum_{t=p+1}^T \hat{a}_t \hat{a}'_t. \quad (3.32)$$

**Theorem 3.1** Assume that \(a_t\) of the VAR(\(p\)) model is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random vectors with mean zero and positive-definite covariance matrix \(\Sigma_a\). Then, the OLS estimation gives (a) \(E(\hat{\beta}) = \beta\), (b) \(E(\hat{\Sigma}_a) = \Sigma_a\), (c) the residual \(\hat{A}\) is uncorrelated with \(\hat{\beta}\), and (d) the covariance matrix of the parameter estimates is

$$Cov[vec(\hat{\beta})] = \tilde{\Sigma}_a \otimes (X'X)^{-1}.$$

To establish the limiting normal distribution of the estimate \(\hat{\beta}\), we further assume that

$$E|a_{it}a_{ju}a_{ru}a_{vt}| < \infty, \quad \text{for all } 1 \le i, j, u, v \le k \text{ and } t, \quad (3.33)$$

which implies that all fourth moments of \(a_t\) are finite.

**Theorem 3.2** Assume that the condition of Theorem 3.1 and Eq. (3.33) hold. Then, as \(T \to \infty\),

- (i) \(\hat{\beta} \to_p \beta,\)
- (ii) \(\sqrt{T-p}[vec(\hat{\beta}) - vec(\beta)] \to_d N(0, \Sigma_a \otimes G^{-1}),\)

where \(\to_p\) and \(\to_d\) denote convergence in probability and in distribution, respectively, and

$$G = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0' \\ 0 & \Gamma_0^* \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ u \end{bmatrix} [0, u']$$

where 0 is a \(kp\)-dimensional zero vector, \(u = 1_p \otimes \mu\) with \(1_p\) being the \(p\)-dimensional vector of ones, and

$$\Gamma_0^* = \begin{bmatrix} \Gamma_z(0) & \Gamma_z(1) & \cdots & \Gamma_z(p-1) \\ \Gamma_z(-1) & \Gamma_z(0) & \cdots & \Gamma_z(p-2) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \Gamma_z(1-p) & \Gamma_z(2-p) & \cdots & \Gamma_z(0) \end{bmatrix}.$$
