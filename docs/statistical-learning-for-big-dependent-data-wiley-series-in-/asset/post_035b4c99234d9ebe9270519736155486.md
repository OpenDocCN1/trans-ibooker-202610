## 9.3.6 Kriging

Kriging is a spatial prediction method; see Matheron (1963). Following Cressie and Wikle (2011), we introduce kriging using a HM framework. The spatial process of interest is \( \{Y(s)|s \in D\} \) with \( 0 < \text{Var}[Y(s)] < \infty \) for all \( s \in D \). Note that \( Y(s) \) may not be second-order stationary. The observed data \( Z(s) \) are noisy and follow the model in Eq. (9.8). Assume, for now, that the first two moment equations of \( Y(s) \) are known and given by

\(\mu_y(s) = E[Y(s)], \quad s \in D,\) (9.24)

\(C_y(s_1, s_2) = \text{Cov}[Y(s_1), Y(s_2)], \quad s_1, s_2 \in D.\) (9.25)

In addition, the variance of the measurement error \(\sigma^2_e\) is also known. In Eq. (9.25), we do not assume \( C_y(s_1, s_2) \) is a function of \( s_1 - s_2 \). The variogram of \( Y(s) \) is then given by

\(2\gamma_y(s_1, s_2) = \text{Var}[Y(s_1) - Y(s_2)] = C_y(s_1, s_1) + C_y(s_2, s_2) - 2C_y(s_1, s_2).\)

Kriging is to obtain an optimal spatial prediction of \( Y(s_0) \) conditional on \( \boldsymbol{Z} = \{Z(s_1), \dots, Z(s_m)\}\), where \( s_0 \in D \) is the location of interest. Three types of kriging are considered in the literature, depending on the assumption imposed on the mean equation of \( Y(s) \). The first kriging is simple kriging, in which \( E[Y(s)] \) is known. The second type of kriging is ordinary kriging, in which \( E[Y(s)] = \mu \) is an unknown constant, and the final type of kriging is universal kriging, in which \( E[Y(s)] \) depends on some covariates with unknown parameters.

### 9.3.6.1 Simple Kriging

The method known as *simple kriging* is to search over all heterogeneously linear predictors, \( \{\boldsymbol{a}'\boldsymbol{Z} + u | \boldsymbol{a} \in R^m, u \in R\} \), to obtain an optimal spatial predictor of \( Y(s_0) \). Like minimizing the mean squared prediction error (MSPE) in time series analysis, one seeks \( \boldsymbol{a} \) and \( u \) by taking partial derivatives of

\(\text{MSPE}(\boldsymbol{a}, u) = E[Y(s_0) - \boldsymbol{a}'\boldsymbol{Z} - u]^2,\) (9.26)

with respect to \( \boldsymbol{a} \) and \( u \). Equation (9.26) can be written as

\(\text{MSPE}(\boldsymbol{a}, u) = \text{Var}[Y(s_0) - \boldsymbol{a}'\boldsymbol{Z} - u] + \{E[Y(s_0) - \boldsymbol{a}'\boldsymbol{Z} - u]\}^2.\)

The second term is \([\mu_y(s_0) - \boldsymbol{a}'\mu_y - u]^2\), where \( \mu_y = [\mu_y(s_1), \dots, \mu_y(s_m)]'\), which is zero if \( u \) satisfies

\(u = \mu_y(s_0) - \boldsymbol{a}'\mu_y.\) (9.27)

With \( u \) given in Eq. (9.27), the first term of MSPE is

\(C_y(s_0, s_0) - 2\boldsymbol{a}' \text{Cov}[Y(s_0), \boldsymbol{Z}] + \boldsymbol{a}'\Sigma_z \boldsymbol{a},\) (9.28)

where the \( m \times m \) covariance matrix \( \Sigma_z = [C_z(s_i, s_j)] \) is given by

\(\begin{array}{ c }
C_z(u, v) = \left\{ \begin{array}{ c c }
C_y(v, v) + \sigma_\epsilon^2, & \text{if } u = v, v \in D, \\
C_y(u, v), & \text{if } u \neq v, 
\end{array} \right. \\
\end{array}\)

and

\(\text{Cov}[Y(s_0), Z] = [C_y(s_0, s_1), \dots, C_y(s_0, s_m)]' \equiv c_y(s_0). \quad (9.29)\)

Taking partial derivatives of Eq. (9.28) with respect to \( a \) and letting the result equal to \( \mathbf{0} \), we obtain

\( -2c_y(s_0) + 2\Sigma_z a = \mathbf{0}. \)

Consequently, the optimal coefficient vector is \( a_* = \Sigma_z^{-1}c_y(s_0) \) and, hence, \( u_* = \mu_y(s_0) - c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_z^{-1}\mu_y \). Based on the prior derivation, the simple-kriging predictor is

\( Y^*(s_0) = \mu_y(s_0) + c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_z^{-1}(Z - \mu_y), \quad (9.30) \)

where \( c_y(s_0) \) is given in Eq. (9.29) and \( \mu_y \) is defined in Eq. (9.27). Plugging \( u_* \) and \( a_* \) into Eq. (9.26), the minimized MSPE is

\( \sigma_{y,sk}^2(s_0) = \text{MSPE}(a_*, u_*) = C_y(s_0, s_0) - c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_z^{-1}c_y(s_0), \quad (9.31) \)

where the subscript \( sk \) is used to denote simple kriging. The quantity \( \sigma_{y,sk}^2(s_0) \) of Eq. (9.31) is often referred to as simple-kriging variance.

When covariates are available and the model follows Eqs. (9.6) to (9.8), we have \( \mu_y(s) = x(s)'\beta \) and the simple-kriging predictor becomes

\( Y^*(s_0) = x(s_0)'\beta + c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_z^{-1}(Z - X\beta). \quad (9.32) \)

The simple-kriging variance of Eq. (9.31) remains unchanged.

Note that the simple-kriging solution and variance can also be obtained under the following HM:

- Data model: Conditional on \( \sigma_\epsilon^2 \),

\( Z(s_i)|Y(s_i), \sigma_\epsilon^2 \sim_{ind} N[Y(s_i), \sigma_\epsilon^2], \quad s_i \in D, \quad i = 1, \dots, m. \)

- Process model: Conditional on \( \beta \) and \( C_y(\cdot, \cdot) \), \( Y(\cdot) \) is a Gaussian process satisfying

\( E[Y(s)] = x(s)'\beta, \quad \text{Cov}[Y(s_i, s_j)] = C_y(s_i, s_j), \quad s_1, s_2 \in D. \)

From the data model, we have \( Z|Y \sim N(Y, \sigma_\epsilon^2 I) \), where \( Y \) is given in Eq. (9.7) and \( I \) is the \( m \times m \) identity matrix. From the process model, we have

\(\begin{bmatrix} Y(s_0) \\ Y \end{bmatrix} \sim N \left( \begin{bmatrix} x(s_0)' \\ X \end{bmatrix} \beta, \begin{bmatrix} C_y(s_0, s_0) & c_y(s_0)' \\ c_y(s_0) & \Sigma_y \end{bmatrix} \right), \quad (9.33)\)

where \(\Sigma_y = \text{Var}(Y)\). Therefore, by properties of multivariate normal distribution,

\(Y(s_0)|Y \sim N[x(s_0)'\beta + c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_y^{-1}(Y - X\beta), C_y(s_0, s_0) - c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_y^{-1}c_y(s_0)].\) (9.34)

Next, by Bayes' Theorem, \([Y|Z] \propto [Z|Y][Y]\), where \([A|C]\) denotes the conditional distribution of \(A\) given \(C\), and \([Y]\) denotes the marginal distribution of \(Y\). By the conjugate property of Gaussian distribution, we can obtain the posterior distribution \([Y|Z]\) as

\(Y|Z \sim N[D^{-1}(\sigma_\epsilon^{-2}Z + \Sigma_y^{-1}X\beta), D^{-1}],\) (9.35)

where \(D = \sigma_\epsilon^{-2}I + \Sigma_y^{-1}\) is the sum of two precision matrices. Using well-known Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury matrix identities, we can rewrite the conditional distribution as

\(Y|Z \sim N(X\beta + K(Z - X\beta), (I - K)\Sigma_y),\) (9.36)

where \(K = \Sigma_y\Sigma_z^{-1}\) with \(\Sigma_z = \Sigma_y + \sigma_\epsilon^2 I\). Now, using iterated expectation and the fact that \([Y(s_0)|Y, Z] = [Y(s_0)|Y]\), we have

\(E[Y(s_0)|Z] = E[\{E[Y(s_0)|Y, Z]\}|Z]\)
\(= E[x(s_0)'\beta + c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_y^{-1}(Y - X\beta)|Z]\)
\(= x(s_0)'\beta + c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_y^{-1}K(Z - X\beta) \quad \text{[Equation (9.36)]}\)
\(= x(s_0)'\beta + c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_z^{-1}(Z - X\beta)\)
\(= Y^*(s_0).\) (9.37)

For the posterior variance, we have

\(\text{Var}[Y(s_0)|Z] = E\{\text{Var}[Y(s_0)|Y]|Z\} + \text{Var}\{E[Y(s_0)|Y]|Z\}\)
\(= C_y(s_0, s_0) - c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_y^{-1}c_y(s_0) + \text{Var}[c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_y^{-1}Y|Z]\)
\(= C_y(s_0, s_0) - c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_y^{-1}Kc_y(s_0) \quad \text{[Equation (9.36)]}\)
\(= C_y(s_0, s_0) - c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_z^{-1}c_y(s_0)\)
\(= \sigma^2_{y.sk}(s_0).\) (9.38)

This completes the derivation under the HM framework.

### 9.3.6.2 Ordinary Kriging

The derivation of simple kriging assumes the mean and covariance function are known. Here we relax the assumption by allowing the mean function to be unknown, but impose certain unbiasedness, leading to the use of homogeneously linear combination of the data in spatial prediction. Ordinary kriging is referred to the case that the mean of \(Y(s)\) is constant, but unknown.

The ordinary-kriging predictor is then a linear combination of the data $Z$, say $b'Z$, that minimizes the MSPE

\( MSPE(b) = E[Y(s_0) - b'Z]^2, \quad (9.39) \)

subject to the unbiasedness condition \( E(b'Z) = E[Y(s_0)] = \mu \) for all \( \mu \in R \). Since \( E[Z(s)] = E[Y(s)] = \mu \), we have \( E(Z) = \mu 1 \), where \( 1 \) is the \( m \)-dimensional vector of 1s. The unbiasedness condition then implies

\( b'1 = 1, \quad (9.40) \)

that is, the sum of the ordinary-kriging coefficients is 1. The method of Lagrange multipliers is then used to minimize the objective function

\( \ell(b) = MSPE(b) - 2\lambda(b'1 - 1). \)

More specifically, the objective function is

\( \ell(b, \lambda) = C_y(s_0, s_0) - 2b'c_y(s_0) + b'\Sigma_z b - 2\lambda(b'1 - 1), \quad (9.41) \)

where, again, \( \Sigma_z = Var(Z) \). Taking partial derivatives with respect to \( b \) and \( \lambda \), and setting them to zero, we have

\( -c_y(s_0) + \Sigma_z b = \lambda 1, \\ b'1 = 1. \quad (9.42) \)

Solving the above equations, we obtain

\( b_* = \Sigma_z^{-1}[c_y(s_0) + k_* 1], \)

and \( k_* = [1 - 1'\Sigma_z^{-1}c_y(s_0)] / (1'\Sigma_z^{-1}1) \). Consequently, the ordinary-kriging predictor is

\( \widehat{Y}(s_0) = b_*'Z = \{c_y(s_0) + 1(1 - 1'\Sigma_z^{-1}1) / (1'\Sigma_z^{-1}1)\}'\Sigma_z^{-1}Z, \quad (9.43) \)

and the minimized MSPE is

\( \sigma_{y,ok}^2 = C_y(s_0, s_0) - c_y(s_0)'\Sigma_z^{-1}c_y(s_0) + [1 - 1'\Sigma_z^{-1}c_y(s_0)]^2 / (1'\Sigma_z^{-1}1), \quad (9.44) \)

where the subscript \( ok \) denotes ordinary kriging. \( \sigma_{y,ok}^2 \) is referred to as ordinary-kriging variance.
