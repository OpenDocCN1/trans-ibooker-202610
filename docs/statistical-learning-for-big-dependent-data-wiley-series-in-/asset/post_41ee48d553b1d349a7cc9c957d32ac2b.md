Note that \(E[Y(\mathbf{s}_0)|\mathbf{Z}] = E[Z(\mathbf{s}_0)|\mathbf{Z}]\), but these two conditional expectations have different properties and they serve different purposes.

Before discussing data analysis, we start with some basic concepts of the spatial process \(Y(\mathbf{s})\) with \(\mathbf{s} \in D \subset R^d\), where \(D\) is assumed to have positive volume in \(R^d\). The process \(Y(\mathbf{s})\) is second-order stationary if

\(E[Y(\mathbf{s})] = \mu, \quad \text{for all } \mathbf{s} \in D,\)
(9.2)

\(\text{Cov}[Y(\mathbf{s}_1), Y(\mathbf{s}_2)] = C(\mathbf{s}_1 - \mathbf{s}_2), \quad \text{for all } \mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{s}_2 \in D.\)
(9.3)

Eq. (9.2) states that the expectation of \(Y(\mathbf{s})\) is invariant in \(D\), and the function \(C(\cdot)\) of Eq. (9.3) is called a *covariogram* or a stationary covariance function. Furthermore, if \(C(\mathbf{s}_1 - \mathbf{s}_2)\) is a function only of \(\|\mathbf{s}_1 - \mathbf{s}_2\|\), then \(C(\cdot)\) is called *isotropic*. From the definition, readers immediately know that the concept of second-order (or weak) stationarity of a spatial process is similar to that of a time series. The only difference is that for spatial processes the location \(\mathbf{s}\) has no well-defined direction if \(d > 1\). The concept of *ergodicity* of \(Y(\mathbf{s})\) is also defined in a similar way as that of a time series. Roughly speaking, for an ergodic process \(Y(\mathbf{s})\), one can estimate \(E[Y(\mathbf{s})]\) by spatial averages.

For a second-order stationary spatial process \(Y(\mathbf{s})\), define

\(C_y(\mathbf{h}) = \text{Cov}[Y(\mathbf{s}), Y(\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{h})], \quad \text{for all } \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{h} \in D.\)
(9.4)

For simplicity, we further assume that \(Y(\mathbf{s})\) and \(\varepsilon(\mathbf{s})\) are independent Gaussian processes so that the first two moments are of interest. Define \(\sigma_y^2 = C_y(\mathbf{0})\) and

\(\sigma_0^2 = \lim_{\mathbf{h} \to \mathbf{0}} [C_y(\mathbf{0}) - C_y(\mathbf{h})],\)
(9.5)

which is nonnegative. Note that \(\sigma_0^2\) denotes the jump in the covariogram of \(Y(\mathbf{s})\) process at \(\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}\). Then, we have

\(\text{Cov}[Z(\mathbf{s}), Z(\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{h})] \equiv C_z(\mathbf{h}) = \begin{cases} \sigma_y^2 + \sigma_e^2, & \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0} \\ C_y(\mathbf{h}), & \mathbf{h} \neq \mathbf{0}. \end{cases}\)

The quantity

\(\lim_{\mathbf{h} \to \mathbf{0}} [C_z(\mathbf{0}) - C_z(\mathbf{h})] = \sigma_0^2 + \sigma_e^2\)

is referred to the *nugget effect* in the geostatistics literature. See, for instance, Journel and Huijbregts (1978). The name *nugget* is used to signify the jump in \(C_z(\mathbf{h})\) at \(\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}\). From its definition, the nugget is the sum of the jump in covariogram of \(Y(\mathbf{s})\) at \(\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}\) and the variance of observed noise. In time series analysis, if there exist measurement errors, then an observed stationary time series becomes \(z_t = y_t + \epsilon_t\), where \(y_t\) is the underlying time series and \(\epsilon_t\) is the measurement error, which is assumed to be independent of \(y_t\). In this case, it is easy to see that (a) \(\text{Var}(z_t) = \gamma_z(0) = \gamma_y(0) + \sigma_e^2\), where \(\sigma_e^2\) is the variance of \(\epsilon_t\); and (b) \(\gamma_z(\ell) = \gamma_y(\ell)\), for \(\ell \neq 0\) is an integer. If \(t\) is continuous, then \(\gamma_z(0) = \gamma_y(0) + \sigma_e^2\) and \(\gamma_z(h) = \gamma_y(h)\) for \(h > 0\). Therefore, nugget effect also occurs in time series analysis in the presence of measurement error. In the spatial statistics, the nugget effect further includes any intrinsic jump $\sigma_0^2$ of the covariance function of the spatial process $Y(s)$ at $h = 0$.

With nugget effect, a simple geostatistical model on the domain $D \subset R^d$ can be written as a HM below:

- Data model: Conditional on $\sigma_e^2$, for $i = 1, \dots, m$,
  $$Z(s_i)|Y(s_i), \sigma_e^2 \sim_{\text{ind}} N[Y(s_i), \sigma_e^2].$$

- Process model: Conditional on $\mu$ and $C_y(.)$, $Y(.)$ is a stationary Gaussian process with mean $\mu$ and covariance function $C_y(h)$ with $h \in D$.

Based on the nature of $D$, spatial processes $Y(s)$ are often classified into geostatistical processes, lattice processes, spatial point processes, and random-set processes. For instance, when $D$ consists of two-dimensional grid points, $Y(s)$ is a lattice process, and when $D$ consists of random points in $R^2$, $Y(s)$ is a point process. See Cressie and Wikle (2011, chapter 4) for further details. In what follows, we provide some further details concerning statistical inference of the first three types of spatial process. The random-set processes are less employed in applications.

## 9.3 GEOSTATISTICAL PROCESSES

In applications, some covariates are often available and we postulate that the spatial process $Y(s)$ follows the linear model

$$Y(s) = x(s)'\beta + \eta(s), \quad s \in D, \qquad (9.6)$$

where $\eta(s)$ is a mean-zero Gaussian process with stationary covariance function $C_y(.)$, $x(.)$ is a $k$-dimensional vector of covariates, which are available at every location in $D$, and $\beta$ is a $k$-dimensional vector of parameters. At the observation locations $s_1, \dots, s_m$, we then have a linear mixed model

$$\boldsymbol{Y} = \boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\eta}, \qquad (9.7)$$

where $\boldsymbol{Y} = [Y(s_1), \dots, Y(s_m)]'$, the $j$th row of $\boldsymbol{X}$ is $x(s_j)'$, $\boldsymbol{\eta} = [\eta(s_1), \dots, \eta(s_m)]'$, which denotes a spatial random effect and $\text{Var}(\boldsymbol{\eta}) = [C_y(s_i - s_j)]$ is an $m \times m$ positive-definite matrix. In Eq. (9.7), $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ is the fixed effect of the covariates and we assume that $k < m$. The data model becomes

$$Z(s_i) = Y(s_i) + \epsilon(s_i), \quad i = 1, \dots, m, \qquad (9.8)$$

where $\{\epsilon(s_i)\}$ are iid $N(0, \sigma_e^2)$. Then, for $s \in D$, we have $\text{Var}[Z(s)] = \sigma_y^2 + \sigma_e^2$, which is referred to as the sill in the geostatistics literature. The nugget effect of the model is

$$c_0 = \sigma_0^2 + \sigma_e^2,$$

where $\sigma_0^2 = \lim_{h \to 0} [C_y(0) - C_y(h)]$. See Eq. (9.5). The quantity $\sigma_z^2 - c_0 = \sigma_y^2 - \sigma_0^2$ is called the partial sill.

### 9.3.1 Stationary Variogram

For a spatial process $Y(\mathbf{s})$ in $D \subset R^d$, assume that the mean is constant and differences of variables that are $\mathbf{h}$-apart vary in a way that depends only on $\mathbf{h}$. Then, the function
$$
2\gamma_y(\mathbf{h}) = \text{Var}[Y(\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{h}) - Y(\mathbf{s})], \quad \text{for all } \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{h} \in D, \quad (9.9)
$$

is called the stationary variogram. If $\text{Var}[Y(\mathbf{s})]$ exists, then we have
$$
2\gamma_y(\mathbf{h}) = 2C_y(\mathbf{0})(1 - C_y(\mathbf{h})/C_y(\mathbf{0})) = 2C_y(\mathbf{0})[1 - \rho_y(\mathbf{h})], \quad (9.10)
$$

where $\rho_y(\mathbf{h})$ serves as the autocorrelation of the spatial process if the variogram is isotropic, which is defined below.

The variogram must satisfy the conditional nonpositive-definiteness condition,
$$
\sum_{i=1}^\ell \sum_{j=1}^\ell \alpha_i \bar{\alpha}_j 2\gamma_y(\mathbf{s}_i - \mathbf{s}_j) \le 0, \quad (9.11)
$$

for any positive integer $\ell$, any set of spatial locations $\{\mathbf{s}_1, \dots, \mathbf{s}_\ell\}$, and any set of complex numbers $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_\ell\}$ satisfying $\sum_{i=1}^\ell \alpha_i = 0$, where $\bar{\alpha}_i$ is the complex conjugate of $\alpha_i$. For a second-order stationary process $Y(\mathbf{s})$, it is easy to show that the condition (9.11) holds. Basically, because $\sum_{i=1}^\ell \alpha_i = 0$, the left side of Eq. (9.11) becomes $-2\text{Var}[\sum_{i=1}^\ell \alpha_i Y(\mathbf{s}_i)]$. This condition is important, because it guarantees that all model-based variances are nonnegative.

If the variogram can be written as a function of $\|\mathbf{h}\| = (h_1^2 + \dots + h_d^2)^{1/2}$, the variogram is said to be *isotropic*; otherwise it is said to be *anisotropic*. Sometimes, we write $\|\mathbf{h}\|$ as $\|\mathbf{h}\|_2$ to emphasize the $\ell_2$ norm.

In the previous chapters, we use the autocovariance function to describe the temporal dependence of a second-order stationary time series. In a similar vein, one often uses variogram to capture the spatial dependence of a second-order stationary spatial process. However, one should note that variogram is more general than the covariance function. For instance, consider the scalar case. The variogram is well defined for a random walk process, for which the covariance function is not defined. In practice, variogram models that depend on only a few parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are often used to summarize the spatial dependence in the literature. Some examples are given next.

### 9.3.2 Examples of Semivariogram

The function $\gamma_y(\mathbf{h})$ of Eq. (9.9) is referred as the semivariogram of $Y(\mathbf{s})$, which can be written as $\gamma_y(\mathbf{h}) = C_y(\mathbf{0}) - C_y(\mathbf{h})$. If it depends on a finite-dimensional parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, then we have
$$
\gamma_y(\mathbf{h}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = C_y(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) - C_y(\mathbf{h}, \boldsymbol{\theta}). \quad (9.12)
$$

In this section, we consider some specific examples of semivariogram. Parametric models can be useful in spatial prediction such as kriging. The first example of semivariogram is the Matérn semivariogram with

C_y(\mathbf{h}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sigma_0^2(\|\mathbf{h}\| = 0) + \sigma_1^2 \{ 2^{\theta_2-1} \Gamma(\theta_2) \}^{-1} \{ \|\mathbf{h}\|/\theta_1 \}^{\theta_2} K_{\theta_2}(\|\mathbf{h}\|/\theta_1), \quad (9.13)
