For time series analysis, the approach has been studied for ARMA mixtures by Xiong and Yeung (2004) and from a Bayesian point of view by Frühwirth-Schnatter and Kaufmann (2008). These procedures start with some initial estimation of the allocation of the series to the groups. Then, compute the parameters of the mixture and with those parameters make a new allocation of the series to the groups, and iterate these two steps until convergence. The estimation is carried out group by group and the best solution is selected by the BIC criterion or the posterior probability.

# 5.3.3.1 Maximum Likelihood (ML) Estimation of the AR Mixture Model

Suppose that each series is generated by a Gaussian AR(p) model, where \(p < k\), of one of \(G\) groups with different AR parameters and residual variances. The series are independent and each one \(\boldsymbol{z}_i = (z_{i1}, \dots, z_{iT})'\), \(i=1, \dots, k\), can be considered as generated by a mixture of distributions:

\(f(\boldsymbol{z}_i) = \sum_{g=1}^G \pi_g f_g(\boldsymbol{z}_i | \boldsymbol{\phi}_g, \sigma_g^2),\) (5.29)

where \(\pi_g\) is the unknown probability of the \(g\) group, \(\boldsymbol{\phi}_g = (\phi_{1g}, \dots, \phi_{pg})'\) is the vector of AR parameters and \(\sigma_g^2\) is the variance of the noise in group \(g\), and \(f_g\) is the joint density of the univariate observations generated by a Gaussian AR(p), see section 2.8.3. Calling \(\boldsymbol{\omega}_g = (\boldsymbol{\phi}'_g, \sigma_g^2)\), and \(\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_G, \boldsymbol{\phi}'_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{\phi}'_G, \sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_G^2)'\) to all the parameter vector of the mixture model in Eq. (5.29), the joint conditional log-likelihood for \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i = (z_{i,p+1}, \dots, z_{i,T})'\), for \(i=1, \dots, k\), is

\(L_C(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^k \log \left( \sum_{g=1}^G \pi_g f_g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i | \boldsymbol{\psi}_g) \right),\) (5.30)

where \(f_g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i | \boldsymbol{\psi}_g)\) is the joint density function of \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i\) conditioned on the first \(p\) observations. This function has many maximums, linked to solutions where each density is determined precisely by an observation. In order to avoid these singularities, we assume that there are at least \(p+1\) observations from each group and try to find a local maximum of this function. An additional problem with this likelihood function is that the normal distributions are not identified, since the ordering \(1, \dots, G\) is arbitrary. To solve this problem, we can assume that the distributions \(1, \dots, G\) correspond to \(\pi_1 \ge \pi_2 \ge \dots \ge \pi_G\).

To maximize the function in Eq. (5.30) with respect to the probabilities \(\pi_g\), the restriction \(\sum_{g=1}^G \pi_g = 1\) is introduced with a Lagrange multiplier and the function to be maximized is

\(L_C(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^k \log \sum_{g=1}^G \pi_g f_g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i | \boldsymbol{\psi}_g) - \lambda \left( \sum_{g=1}^G \pi_g - 1 \right).\) (5.31)

Taking the derivatives of the function with respect to the group probabilities \(\pi_g\):

\(\frac{\partial L_C(\boldsymbol{\beta})}{\partial \pi_g} = \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{f_g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i | \boldsymbol{\psi}_g)}{\sum_{g=1}^G \pi_g f_g(\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_i | \boldsymbol{\psi}_g)} - \lambda = 0\)
