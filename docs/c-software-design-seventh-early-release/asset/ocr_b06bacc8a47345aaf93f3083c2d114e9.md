*Polymorphism* did not strike you as a very modern way of solving things. Instead of following the advice to prefer *value semantics*, the implementation was firmly build on *reference semantics*: many pointers, many manual allocations, and manual lifetime management.¹ Hence the missing detail you are waiting for is a *value semantics* based implementation of the *External Polymorphism* design pattern. And I will not keep you waiting anymore: the resulting solution is commonly called *Type Erasure.*²

# The History of Type Erasure

Before I give you a detailed introduction, let us quickly talk about the history of *Type Erasure*. “Come on”, you complain, “Is this really necessary? I’m dying here to finally see how this stuff works.” Well, I promise to keep it short. But yes, I feel this is a necessary detail of this discussion for two reasons. First, to demonstrate that we as a community, aside from the circle of the most experienced C++ experts, may have overlooked and ignored that technique for too long. And second, to give some well-deserved credit to the inventor of the technique.

The *Type Erasure* design pattern is very often attributed to one of the first and therefore most famous presentation of this technique. At the GoingNative 2013 conference, Sean Parent talked about “Inheritance Is The Base Class of Evil”.³ While the title most certainly has its funny touch, the content was rather serious: Sean recaps his experiences from the development of Photoshop and talks about the dangers and disadvantages of inheritance-based implementations. However, he also presents a solution to the inheritance problem, which later came to be known as *Type Erasure.*

For instance, *Type Erasure* was used in several places in the Boost libraries, for instance by Douglas Gregor for `boost::function`.
