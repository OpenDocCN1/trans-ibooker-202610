## 2.3 Data-Driven Prognostics

Data-driven (DD) prognostics (Figure 2.12) comprises two major approaches, statistical and machine learning (ML), that use acquired data to statistically and probabilistically produce prognostic information such as decisions, estimates, and predictions. Statistical approaches include parametric and nonparametric methods; ML approaches include supervised and unsupervised classification and clustering, and regression and ranking (Pecht 2008). This book will not discuss regression and ranking.

#### Maximum Likelihood Method

The maximum likelihood method (Ross 1987) is a common procedure to estimate unknown parameters of probability distributions. As in Section 1.4, let \( f(t \mid \theta) \) denote the PDF where \( \theta \) is unknown. Assume we have a random sample \( t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N \) from this distribution. The likelihood function is defined as

$$
L(\theta) = f(t_1 \mid \theta) f(t_2 \mid \theta) \cdots f(t_N \mid \theta)
$$
(2.16)

which represents the probability of the sampling event that actually occurred. Since the logarithmic function is strictly increasing, instead of \( L(\theta) \), its logarithm is maximized:

$$
\text{Maximize } \ln(L(\theta))
$$
(2.17)

and the optimal \( \theta \) value is accepted as the estimate of the unknown parameter.

**Example 2.7**  
Assume that the distribution is Weibull with a known value of \( \beta \) and an unknown value of \( \eta \). We discussed in Chapter 1 that

$$
f(t \mid \beta, \eta) = \frac{\beta}{\eta} \left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^{\beta-1} e^{-(t/\eta)^\beta}
$$

and therefore

$$
L(\eta) = \frac{\beta^N}{\eta^{N\beta}} \left(\prod_{i=1}^N t_i\right)^{\beta-1} e^{-\eta^{-\beta} \sum_{i=1}^N t_i^\beta}
$$

so

$$
l(\eta) = \ln(L(\eta)) = N \ln(\beta) - N\beta \ln(\eta) + (\beta-1) \ln\left(\prod_{i=1}^N t_i\right) - \eta^{-\beta} \sum_{i=1}^N t_i^\beta
$$

By differentiation,

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \eta} l(\eta) = -\frac{N\beta}{\eta} + \beta \eta^{-\beta-1} \sum_{i=1}^N t_i^\beta
$$

So, the first-order condition can be written as

$$
-\frac{N\beta}{\eta} + \frac{\beta}{\eta^{\beta+1}} \sum_{i=1}^N t_i^\beta = 0
$$

showing that

$$
\eta = \left(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N t_i^\beta\right)^{1/\beta}
$$
(2.18)

#### Likelihood Ratio Test

The likelihood ratio test (Casella and Berger 2002) is usually used to determine the validity of an estimate. For example, let \( \theta_0 \) be an estimate of an unknown parameter of a PDF \( f(t \mid \theta) \). The likelihood ratio test is based on the likelihood ratio

$$
r = \frac{L(\theta_0)}{\max_{\theta} L(\theta)}
$$
(2.19)

where the likelihood function is denoted by \( L(\theta) \) and the denominator is the maximal value of \( L(\theta) \). In other cases, two estimates are compared. Let \( \theta_1 \) and \( \theta_2 \) be two estimates for \( \theta \); then

$$
r = \frac{L(\theta_1)}{L(\theta_2)}
$$
(2.20)

If the value of \( r \) is small, then in the first case \( \theta_0 \) is unacceptable, and in the second case \( \theta_2 \) is a much better estimate.

**Example 2.8**  
Assume an exponential distribution with a parameter \( \lambda \) and sample elements \( t_1, t_2, \dots, t_N \). Then

$$
f(t \mid \lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}
$$

so

$$
L(\lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t_1} \lambda e^{-\lambda t_2} \cdots \lambda e^{-\lambda t_N} = \lambda^N e^{-\lambda \sum_{i=1}^N t_i}
$$

Therefore, the likelihood ratio test in comparing two estimates, \( \lambda_1 \) and \( \lambda_2 \), can be written as

$$
r = \frac{\lambda_1^N e^{-\lambda_1 \sum_{i=1}^N t_i}}{\lambda_2^N e^{-\lambda_2 \sum_{i=1}^N t_i}} = \left(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2}\right)^N e^{(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1) \sum_{i=1}^N t_i}
$$
(2.21)

by Eq. (2.20).

#### Minimum Mean Square Error

Minimum mean square error is mainly used in fitting function forms with unknown parameters. For example, a density histogram is obtained from a sample with points \( (t_1, f_1), (t_2, f_2), \dots, (t_N, f_N) \), and it is known that the corresponding PDF is \( f(t \mid \theta) \). The least-squares estimate of \( \theta \) is obtained by minimizing the overall squared error:

$$
Q = \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ f(t_i \mid \theta) - f_i \right]^2
$$
(2.22)

**Example 2.9**  
Assume that \( f(t \mid \theta) = \theta e^{-\theta t} \); then

$$
Q = \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \theta e^{-\theta t_i} - f_i \right)^2
$$
(2.23)

The first-order condition can be written as

$$
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial \theta} = 2 \sum_{i=1}^N \left( \theta e^{-\theta t_i} - f_i \right)\left( e^{-\theta t_i} - \theta t_i e^{-\theta t_i} \right) = 0
$$

which is a nonlinear equation and can be solved by standard methods.

#### Maximum a Posteriori Estimation

The maximum a posteriori estimation (Stein et al. 2002) is based on Bayesian principles. Consider again a PDF \( f(t \mid \theta) \) depending on the unknown parameter \( \theta \). The likelihood function is given as \( L(\theta) \), which is maximized in order to get the best estimate for \( \theta \). In practical cases, usually its logarithm is maximized. Assume now that a prior distribution is known for \( \theta \), with PDF \( g(\theta) \). By the Theorem of Bayes,

$$
L(\theta \mid t_1, \dots, t_N) = \frac{L(\theta) g(\theta)}{\int L(\bar{\theta}) g(\bar{\theta})\, d\bar{\theta}}
$$
(2.24)

where the integration domain is the domain of all possible values of \( \theta \). Since the denominator is independent of \( \theta \), we need to optimize only \( L(\theta) g(\theta) \).

**Example 2.10**  
Assume a normal distribution with parameters \( \mu \) and \( \sigma^2 \), where \( \sigma^2 \) is known and only \( \mu \) is unknown. Then

$$
L(\mu) = \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}
$$

From statistics, it is known that the normal distribution is its conjugate prior, so we can select

$$
g(\mu) = \frac{1}{\bar{\sigma} \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(\mu-\bar{\mu})^2}{2\bar{\sigma}^2}}
$$

And therefore

$$
L(\mu) g(\mu) = \frac{1}{\bar{\sigma} \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(\mu-\bar{\mu})^2}{2\bar{\sigma}^2}} \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(t_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}
$$

which can be maximized. Its logarithm equals

$$
-\ln(\bar{\sigma} \sqrt{2\pi}) - \frac{(\mu-\bar{\mu})^2}{2\bar{\sigma}^2} - N\ln(\sigma) - N\ln(\sqrt{2\pi}) - \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{(t_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}
$$

This is a concave function in \( \mu \), and its derivative has the special form

$$
\frac{-2(\mu-\bar{\mu})}{2\bar{\sigma}^2} + \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{2(t_i-\mu)}{2\sigma^2}
= \frac{1}{\bar{\sigma}^2 \sigma^2} \left\{ -\sigma^2(\mu-\bar{\mu}) + \bar{\sigma}^2 \left( \sum_{i=1}^N t_i - N\mu \right) \right\}
$$

giving the solution

$$
\mu = \frac{\sigma^2 \bar{\mu} + \bar{\sigma}^2 \sum_{i=1}^N t_i}{\sigma^2 + N \bar{\sigma}^2}
$$
(2.25)

#### K-Nearest Neighbor Classifier

The kNN classifier (Cover and Hart 1967) is based on the following simple procedure. Assume we have \( N \) vectors; each of them is attached with a class label. We need to put a given vector into the most appropriate class. We select a positive integer \( k \ge 1 \) and determine the \( k \) closest vectors from the given \( N \) vectors by using any distance measure, such as the Euclidean distance. Then the selected \( k \) closest vectors “vote” about the class by selecting the class that appears the most times among the \( k \) closest vectors. In this way, we can order any set of vectors into given classes based on a given sample.

In a two-dimensional case, the Euclidean distance of vectors \( (x_c, y_c) \) and \( (x_i, y_i) \) is the following:

$$
D_i = \sqrt{(x_c - x_i)^2 + (y_c - y_i)^2}
$$
(2.26)

The Euclidean distance is based on the Pythagorean theorem. If the dimension is larger than two, say \( n \), then similarly

$$
D_i = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^n \left( x_c^{(j)} - x_i^{(j)} \right)^2}
$$
(2.27)

where the components of vector \( \boldsymbol{x}_c \) are \( x_c^{(j)} \) and those of vector \( \boldsymbol{x}_i \) are \( x_i^{(j)} \).

#### Kernel Density Estimation

This method is based on the following equation:

$$
p(x) = \frac{1}{Nh} \sum_{i=1}^N K\left( \frac{x - x_i}{h} \right)
$$
(2.28)

which can be interpreted as follows (Wand and Jones 1995). Let \( x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N \) be the sample elements. A kernel function \( K(x) \) is selected that is nonnegative and that has an integral of one (like the properties of any PDF). A parameter \( h > 0 \) is also chosen, which is called the bandwidth. Then Eq. (2.28) gives an estimate of the PDF from which the sample is generated, at point \( x \). Table 2.6 gives a collection of commonly used kernel functions.

**Table 2.6** Kernel functions.

| Kernel | K(x) | Domain |
|---|---|---|
| Uniform | 1/2 | (−1, 1) |
| Triangle | 1 − \|x\| | (−1, 1) |
| Epanechnikov | 3/4 (1 − x²) | (−1, 1) |
| Quartic | 15/16 (1 − x²)² | (−1, 1) |
| Triweight | 35/32 (1 − x²)³ | (−1, 1) |
| Gaussian | (1/√(2π)) e^(−x²/2) | (−∞, ∞) |
| Cosine | (π/4) cos(πx/2) | (−1, 1) |

#### Chi-Square Test

The chi-square test (Ross 1987) is used to test whether a given sample comes from a population with a specific distribution, and therefore it does not provide the distribution; it can only be used to check whether a user-selected distribution is appropriate.
