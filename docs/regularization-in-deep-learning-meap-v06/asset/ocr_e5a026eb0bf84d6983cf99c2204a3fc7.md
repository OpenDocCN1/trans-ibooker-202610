Figure 3.2 Fitting two two-dimensional observations using different model complexities. The left plot contains a 0th degree polynomial model that contains only one parameter, thus being a horizontal line. The middle plot has an equal number of parameters as the number of observations, making the solution unique and exact. The right plot contains models with more than two parameters, where the solution is non-unique due to being an underdetermined system.

It is evident that perfect interpolation when p = n, i.e., the number of features is equal to the number of observations. The perfect interpolation continues with p > n when the system of equations becomes underdetermined and over-parameterized, leading to infinitely many solutions that correspond to models of arbitrary shape. All these models pass through the observed data points and thus give zero training error.

Therefore, if we stretch the graph towards the right, i.e., keep increasing the model complexity, the training risk will continue to remain zero beyond the perfect interpolation point. What about the test risk? According to the classical view on generalization, the test risk will continue to shoot up when the model becomes even more complex. However, what is observed in practice is the "double descent" phenomenon on the test risk shown in figure 3.3, where the test risk starts to drop at some point even below the best risk obtained at the "sweet spot".
