Since \([\nabla_w Q(\boldsymbol{w}_t, D)]^2 \to 0\) and \(\varsigma > 0\), we have \(\mathbb{E}[Q(\boldsymbol{w}_{t+1}, D)] \le Q(\boldsymbol{w}_t, D)]\), thus proving that the sequence of loss iterates a super-martingale. It eventually converges to a stationary point in a local minimum based on Doob's martingale convergence theorem. Although the loss at the next iterate will not always decrease and may sometimes increase due to randomness in the subset \(D_t\), we can guarantee that, on average, it will decrease over time. The expected risk does not increase in expectation. Figure 8.2 summarizes the sketch of the proof of SGD convergence.

Note that not all estimators are unbiased. In fact, we rarely manage to have an unbiased estimator when using complex learners such as neural networks. However, things still work out quite nicely in practice. When the optimizer struggles due to high variance, we could improve such a situation by introducing a little bit of bias while greatly easing the high-variance issue, a topic on bias-variance tradeoff covered in chapter 2. There are many examples where we can obtain a good model using biased but lower-variance estimators, such as adding the LASSO penalty to the loss function covered in chapter 7. Note that having a biased estimator may not converge to a stationary point in a limited training time.

Now that we know SGD is a computationally economical algorithm that converges to a local minimum, the next question we would like to ask is how good the quality of the solution is. Is it the same quality as the one from the full-batch gradient descent algorithm, or better? As it turns out, SGD often delivers better solutions than GD because the resulting model generalizes better to the future unseen test set. Such generalization performance comes from the implicit regularization effect from SGD, as discussed in the following section.
