
Since SGD converges relatively slowly due to the stochastic noise, a natural question is if we can do something to suppress such stochasticity and (expectedly) accelerate its convergence. This question brings a series of SGD variants, with momentum and Adam being the most popular choices of optimizers in the community.

## 8.1.5 SGD variants: better or worse?

We have used the Adam optimizer to train a multi-class model in chapter 6. We also mentioned in chapter 2 that it could accelerate learning but may only sometimes result in good generalization performance. Should we stick to either Adam or SGD when training a deep neural network? Not necessarily. Many practitioners start the training process using Adam as it is less sensitive to the choice of the learning rate (more on this later) and switch to SGD after a while to gain good generalization performance. Combining a short-term myopic reward (as in the case of Adam) with a long-term non-myopic gain (as in the case of SGD) is a popular choice in the design of many policies, such as in the field of reinforcement learning and Bayesian optimization.

As a variant of SGD, Adam sits on two key aspects: smoothed updates using momentum and scaled learning rate using gradient norm. Let us illustrate the idea of momentum as a start. Imagine we are dealing with a two-dimensional parameter space where the loss function to be minimized is narrow, as shown by the elongated ellipses (level sets) in figure 8.8. Imagine the optimizer seeking a narrow valley’s trough (local minimum) (loss function). Due to a sharp slope along the direction perpendicular to the path toward the local minimum, the partial derivatives at the current location, as indicated by the solid dot, are very different in magnitude. Specifically, the partial derivative g2 will be much larger than g1 on the left panel, leading to oscillations across each update given a high learning rate and likely divergence from the local optimum.

Figure 8.8 SGD updates with oscillating trajectories due to disproportionate partial derivatives and a high learning rate on the left, and slow progress due to a small learning rate on the right.

On the other hand, decreasing the learning rate to a very small number eases the oscillations to some extent but, instead, leads to slow convergence; SGD will take a very long time to show decent progress toward the local minimum. In both cases, the actual convergence performance is highly sensitive to the learning rate hyperparameter.

One of the early approaches to tackle this problem is called momentum, which uses the moving average of the gradient in each SGD update and thus alleviates the high-variance problem in the gradients of SGD. Denote the original gradient vector as gt = ∇wQ(w, Dt) and its moving average as mt. The current moving average mt is calculated based on the following relationship:

mt = β1 · mt−1 + (1 − β1)gt

Where β1 is a hyperparameter that controls the weighting between the previous moving average and the current gradient, element-wise. The corresponding SGD update rule with a running average of gradients thus becomes:

wt+1 = wt − ηmt

Running the iterative updates using the averaged gradients will smooth out the trajectories since gradients of opposite directions will cancel out due to the averaging effect. Momentum thus smooths the stochasticity along the directions perpendicular toward the local minimum.

As a further improvement, the Adam optimizer uses an adaptive step size to estimate the norm of the gradients. As shown in the left panel of figure 8.8, the optimizer tends to move more in the direction of g2 since it is much larger than g1. The goal of Adam is thus to rescale the norm of the gradients so that each partial derivative in the gradient vector has a similar scale. Specifically, the running average of the gradient norm vector vt is defined as the following:

vt = β2vt−1 + (1−β2)gt²

Where gt² means squaring each element (partial derivative) in the gradient vector gt, and β2 is another preset hyperparameter. Once we have the mean gradient and its moving average norm, we can use the following update rule to choose the next weight value:

wt+1 = wt − η * mt / sqrt(vt + ε)

Where we take the square root of vt: to convert it to the same unit as the weights and use a small constant ε to avoid dividing by zero. The adaptive step size is indicated by η/(√(vt) + ε), where the new learning rate is different to each parameter instead of having a uniform learning rate for all the parameters; after all, some parameters tend to be more sensitive to a high learning rate than others. Figure 8.9 summarizes these two adaptive changes over SGD.

### Momentum

$$\mathbf{m}_t = \beta_1 \mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\mathbf{g}_t$$

$$\mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \eta \mathbf{m}_t$$

Using a smoothed vector of partial derivatives in the gradient vector via moving averages

### Adam

$$\mathbf{v}_t = \beta_2 \mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\mathbf{g}_t^2$$

$$\mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \eta \frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{\sqrt{\mathbf{v}_t + \epsilon}}$$

Smoothing both the first (mean) and second (variance) moments of the gradients in the iterative update policy

Figure 8.9 Illustrating the adaptive changes in momentum and Adam.

Overall, Adam estimates the gradient vector’s first and second moments (mean and variance) to update the weights. Since the norm of the gradient vector is estimated and smoothed, the updates will be much less sensitive to the value of the learning rate. And because of this, Adam is a widely used optimizer and converges relatively quickly. However, as later pointed out by Wilson et al. in their 2017 NeurIPS paper titled “The Marginal Value of Adaptive Gradient Methods in Machine Learning”, adaptively changing the gradients, as in Adam and despite their faster optimization nature, often harms the generalization performance as compared to stochastic methods like SGD. It turns out that such stochasticity is a blessing rather than a curse; it enables SGD to deal with tricky situations, such as preferring a low-variance flat minima region over a high-variance sharp minima region.

As an accelerated gradient-based method, Adam serves as an off-the-shelf optimizer when training deep neural networks. Many practitioners use Adam to train neural networks as this optimizer is not sensitive to how we set the hyperparameters, including the learning rate and the initial weights of the network. Using SGD, however, requires some amount of fine-tuning for these hyperparameters. Adam converges much faster than SGD, thus being a better optimizer from an optimization perspective. However, it suffers from poor generalization performance, a more critical pursuit when training models. As discussed in chapter 5, good offline generalization performance correlates with fast online optimization performance (i.e., converges fast) with an unlimited flow of new data. Since the training dataset is almost always limited in practical scenarios, we may not want to seek fast convergence as the only goal.

In summary, the gradient noise in SGD is an essential feature in the stochastic optimization process rather than a disadvantage. By steering away from regions with high gradient noise, SGD delivers better generalization performance than accelerated methods such as momentum and Adam. It is, therefore, essential to keep stochasticity as one of the magic ingredients to obtain a good generalization performance.

## 8.2 More on SGD convergence

Theoretical analysis on SGD requires each mini-batch to be taken with replacement; that is, a sample *can* be repeated in the next mini-batch. Let us use *t* to denote the *t*th iteration and *i(t)* as the index of the random sample used for SGD update of the *k*th iteration, using a single sample in each mini-batch. In each iteration, *i(t)*= {1, 2, ..., *N*} could be any sample within the training set. Our SGD update rule using a single observation at iteration *t* thus becomes:

**w**<sub>*t*+1</sub>=**w**<sub>*t*</sub> - \(\eta_t\) \(\nabla_w Q(\mathbf{w}_t, D_{i(t)})\)

Where *D*<sub>(*t*)</sub> represents the single observation currently sampled and *η<sub>t</sub>_ is the learning rate as a function of the time step. Processing a single observation makes the update *N_ times faster, where *N_ could easily be millions in modern applications. This idea, where a single random observation is used in each round of gradient update instead of the whole training set, was initially proposed by Robbins and Monro in their 1951 paper titled "A Stochastic Approximation Method" and remains the mainstream optimization method in modern neural networks today.

An interesting phenomenon observed in single-example SGD updates is that the descent is typically fast and stable at the initial stage, as indicated by a relatively smooth trajectory, followed by random oscillations within a specific region. Such fluctuations are a result of the stochasticity in the random mini-batches (single example) and a high level of sensitivity to the learning rate.

Figure 8.10 illustrates this behavior. On the left panel, we plot a typical trajectory using single-observation SGD. Note that the first four iterates, as indicated by the number 1-4, have relatively fast progress towards the local optimum on the right area, which confines the follow-up fluctuating updates. Suvrit Sra, who discussed this phenomenon in his YouTube tutorial on SGD, called the first region the far-out zone and the second region the confusion, indicating that the optimizer is now suffering from random fluctuations. We use the same name here.
