
## 1.2 Curse of dimensionality

Why is a modern machine learning model easily over-parameterized and prone to overfitting? The answer has to do with the rise of Big Data. Recent years have witnessed a significant increase in the four Vs. of Big Data: volume, the scale of data; velocity, streaming data at high frequency; variety, different forms of data; and veracity, the uncertainty of data. The four Vs. are primarily driven by advances in modern data collection touchpoints: live-streaming surveillance video cameras, tons of web searches and conversations, and ongoing business transactions of all sorts. Together, they present unprecedented and exciting challenges to both storage and modeling approaches.

One immediate challenge is the high dimensionality. The multitudes of features in the data also present the curse of dimensionality challenge to modelling, making it difficult to design an algorithm that can effectively learn the underlying pattern in high dimensions. Traditional statistical approaches such as linear regression work best when dealing with a small set of features. In the presence of high dimensionalities, such as image data that easily contain tens of thousands of features, it requires much more complex algorithms to identify, extract, and transform essential features and make good predictions. Figure 1.6 shows an example of image data.

![](img/ee98e91e6845f41077b163bec833ee40_13_0.png)

Figure 1.6 Example image taken at the CBD area of Singapore. A typical colour image like this one has shape (400, 225, 3), where 400 denotes the width, 225 denotes the height, and 3 means the number of colour channels, i.e., red, green and blue. This results in a total of 400*225*3=270,000 pixel values, i.e., features in a single image.

Similar challenges exist for text data as well. For instance, a document consists of a multitude of words, each of which could express a different meaning depending on the context. To effectively learn from text data and identify consistent patterns, the model needs to be able to derive word importance and their interaction effect. This process could easily result in tens of thousands of features.

Specific regularization approaches can effectively tackle the challenge of high dimensionality. For example, sparse learning aims to learn a small subset of significant features and discard the others. Such an approach essentially assumes that only a small subset of features is predictive, and the rest are removed by setting the coefficient to zero. Standard sparse learning techniques include Lasso and its variants, which work by adding a penalty on the magnitude of the coefficients, besides minimizing training error. For example, if we want to build a model to classify if the previous picture to be either country or city area, the features representing the tall buildings are more likely to be predictive than others. Using the sparsity constraints could help silence the irrelevant features by setting their resulting coefficient to zero, giving more focused attention to predictive features.

This type of regularization could be interpreted in multiple ways. For example, from a geometric perspective, adding such a penalty introduces a specific geometric constraint to the resulting estimated coefficients, causing some coefficients to approach or equal to zero. From a Bayesian perspective, a prior belief is introduced to enforce a particular distributional assumption for the coefficient estimation process, where a high probability of being zero is encoded in advance. From a robust optimization perspective, certain regularizers like Lasso offer protection to random noises in the data, making the resulting coefficients more robust and less sensitive to noises. We will cover these different perspectives in the later part of the book.

## 1.3 Understanding underfitting and overfitting

Underfitting refers to poor performance in training and test sets due to the trained model being too simple or the training data being too insufficient. Overfitting means the trained model performs well on the training set, a specific subset of the overall population, but does not generalize well to other test sets from the same population. In other words, the generalization error resulting from predicting the test set, or the generalization gap between the training set performance and the test set performance, is pretty big. However, why do we care so much about model generalizability, i.e., its predictive performance on the future unseen test set? To answer this question, let us go back to the exam example earlier. This time, we will use the amount of effort to proxy the model complexity; the harder we study, the more complex our mental model will be.

It helps to observe the evolution of model performance in both training and final exams. As shown in figure 1.7, as we spend more hours studying and honing our mental model, we improve our problem-solving skills and get better scores in both training (practice) and test (final) exams. We may also notice that moving from 60 to 70 marks is much easier than from 80 to 90, or even 100; the amount of effort needed exponentially increases for the same unit improvement in exam marks.

![](img/ee98e91e6845f41077b163bec833ee40_15_0.png)

Figure 1.7 The evolution of training and test performances as more efforts are invested in exam preparation. We start in the underfitting region, where training and test performances improve as the mental problem-solving model becomes powerful. The model starts to overfit after passing the sweet spot, as reflected by the decreasing test performance. When being in the overfitting region, it is essential to look out for the test performance since the training performance will (only) continue improving as the model gets even more complex.

However, if we continue to invest more hours into the study and aim for perfect scores in the practice exam, we may risk overfitting our mental model. At some point, we may start to memorize the solution to each training question, especially when we cannot work out an immediate solution. Given limited study time, perfectly memorizing the answers can directly get us full marks in the practice exam. Unfortunately, pushing too hard does not guarantee an equal payoff. Our test performance will start to drop due to a lack of generalization capacity, and the generalization gap will grow. Besides, given so many practice questions in the market, who knows the answers provided are noise-free and 100% correct? The point is, beware of lower test performance if we only focus on getting the correct answers instead of learning the patterns.

Remember, our goal is to train a good enough mental model through the practice exam and use the trained mental model to obtain high marks in the final test exam hopefully. A model that generalizes well to the test set means the complexity of the model is good enough, and the core problem-solving patterns learned can be well applied to new questions in the final exam. We wish to reduce the generalization gap between training and test performances as much as possible. However, a higher model complexity does not necessarily translate to a smaller generalization gap.

There is often a sweet spot that represents the best performance in the final exam, and it often is associated with good enough training performance in the practice exam. Generalizability starts to decrease after the sweet spot, leading us to the overfitting region. It is thus the sweet spot that we wish to reach. In modeling terms, the sweet spot represents the optimal model complexity, i.e., the weight configuration, which will expectedly lead to the lowest test error and best generalize to the unseen test set. The optimal model avoids underfitting and overfitting: it is complex enough to learn the underlying pattern in the training data and simple enough to avoid fitting spurious patterns due to data noise.

## 1.4 Understanding bias-variance trade-off

Underfitting and overfitting, as separated by the sweet spot in figure 1.7, are closely connected to the concept of bias-variance trade-off that occurs in regression, classification and reinforcement learning tasks. In regression setting, bias refers to how close the model prediction is to the target value, on average. The term average is very much related to expectation. In this case, we are using bias to describe the expected average distance between prediction and target. We want the model to have a low bias as much as possible, thus being close to the actual target value on average. A model with a high bias means it fails to capture the underlying pattern in the data and only generates a biased, off-target prediction. The trained model, if too simple, will likely end up underfitting the training data. In our exam case, both practice and final exam marks will likely be low if our problem-solving mental model is not honed to be accurate enough.

On the other hand, we may also suffer from high variance, a symptom of overfitting. Variance measures the variability of the model prediction; it shows how wide or narrow the range of model predictions could be. We want the model to have a variance as low as possible, thus producing more stable and reliable results. A model with high variance often results from too much focus on fitting the training data well, even to the point of exactly matching its predictions to the target labels. When overfitting occurs, the model performs very well on the training data but has a significant error on test data. In our exam example, overfitting could come from perfect data memorization to correctly answer every practice question. In the presence of potentially wrong answers in the practice exams and lack of generalizable and transferrable problem-solving skills, the trained mental model will likely generate inaccurate, unstable, and very different answers for a slight change in the test question. The significant fluctuation in model predictions, or high variance, is an obvious sign of overfitting.

Both bias and variance are two sources of error that we hope to minimize when training a model. However, it is not easy to reduce both at the same time. On the one hand, reducing bias will often make the model more complex, which increases the chance of overfitting and thus leads to high variance. On the other hand, reducing variance often comes with lower model complexity and likely leads to a high bias when ending up with a simple model. It is difficult to improve one while leaving the other uncompromised, let alone improving both together. This is the so-called bias-variance trade-off.
