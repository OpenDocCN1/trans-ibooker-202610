## 8.2.1 SGD in univariate linear regression

We can glean further insight into the convergence properties of SGD from a simple framework: a univariate model with the single-observation SGD update rule. Our objective function to be minimized thus becomes:

$$Q(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - f(\mathbf{x}_i; \mathbf{w}))^2 = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i w)^2$$

Where we multiply 1/2 to facilitate the mathematical analysis later on. As the objective function of this single-dimensional optimization problem is convex, we can differentiate it and set the gradient to zero to obtain the solution at the local minimum, which is also the global minimum for the current random sample $(x_i, y_i)$. All variables are now one-dimensional and thus easy to analyze, thus writing $Q(\mathbf{w})$ as $Q(w)$.

Since the cost function $Q(w)$ is quadratic in $w$, we can set its gradient to zero to obtain the closed-form expression for the optimal solution $w^*$ as follows.

$$\nabla_w Q(w) = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i w)x_i = 0$$

$$w^* = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}$$

In the context of SGD, the gradient will be calculated based on the cost of the $i^{th}$ single example, namely,

$$Q_i(w) = \frac{1}{2}(y_i - x_i w)^2$$

Again, setting the gradient to zero gives the optimal solution $w^*$ for the $i^{th}$ single example:

$$\nabla_w Q_i(w) = (y_i - x_i w)x_i = 0$$

$$w_i^* = \frac{x_i y_i}{x_i^2} = \frac{y_i}{x_i}$$

Where we have a total of $N$ optimal solutions $\{w^*\}_{i=1}^N$ based on $N$ randomly selected single examples. Out of this sequence of optimal solutions, we can obtain its highest and lowest candidates, i.e., $\max_i\{w_i^*\}$ and $\min_i\{w_i^*\}$, respectively. An interesting question pops up: how does the full-batch solution $w^*$ compare with $\min_i\{w_i^*\}$ and $\max_i\{w_i^*\}$?

To facilitate the analysis, let us assume that all input observations are positive, i.e., $x_i > 0$ for all $i \in \{1,...,N\}$. This is not an overwhelming assumption since we could shift all observations by the same constant until they are positive. Let us further denote $U = \max_i\{w_i^*\}$ and $L = \min_i\{w_i^*\}$. We can proceed with the following observation:

$$\frac{y_i}{x_i} \le U = \frac{y_U}{x_U}$$

Where we use $(x_U, y_U)$ to denote the example that gives the maximum solution $\max_i\{w_i^*\}$. Reshaping the terms and multiplying both sides by $x_i$, we have:

$$x_U y_i \le x_i y_U$$
$$x_U x_i y_i \le x_i^2 y_U$$

Now, adding over all $i \in \{1,...,N\}$ and switching terms give:

$$\sum_{i=1}^N x_U x_i y_i \le \sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2 y_U$$
$$x_U \sum_{i=1}^N x_i y_i \le y_U \sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2$$
$$w^* = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N x_i y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2} \le \frac{y_U}{x_U} = U$$

What this means is that the optimal solution using the full batch is upper bounded by the maximum solution based on a single observation.

We can further prove the lower bound in a similar fashion as follows:

$$\frac{y_i}{x_i} \ge L = \frac{y_L}{x_L}$$

$$x_L y_i \ge x_i y_L$$
$$x_L x_i y_i \ge x_i^2 y_L$$
$$\sum_{i=1}^N x_L x_i y_i \ge \sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2 y_L$$
$$x_L \sum_{i=1}^N x_i y_i \ge y_L \sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2$$
$$w^* = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N x_i y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N x_i^2} \ge \frac{y_L}{x_L} = L$$

Therefore, we have $w^* \in [L, U]$. This is the region where the true minimum lies. Figure 8.12 summarizes the derivation process.

Now let us re-look at the gradients from GD and its SGD counterpart, listed below.

$$\nabla_w Q(w) = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i w)x_i$$
$$\nabla_w Q_i(w) = (y_i - x_i w)x_i$$

Based on the previous discussion, we know that the mini-batch SGD optimizer will move toward the solution given by the full-batch GD optimizer since it is an unbiased estimator to the true gradient of the loss function at that particular point. Such movement can be characterized by two distinct types of behaviors in the two regions, namely the far-out region and the region of confusion.

When the SGD optimizer is located within the far-out region, i.e., $w < L$ or $w > U$, both the single-observation gradient $\nabla_w Q_i(w)$ and the full-batch gradient $\nabla_w Q(w)$ will take the same sign. In other words, SGD will take the same direction of travel as GD, and each GD update corresponds to a total of $N$ updates in SGD. The SGD will move towards the region of confusion. This explains the good and fast progress at the initial training stage, as shown in figure 8.10.

However, when the SGD optimizer enters the region of confusion, the uniform sign is no longer guaranteed. Depending on the specific observation used in gradient calculation, the SGD optimizer will instead bounce around within the region of confusion due to stochasticity in the direction of travel. This explains the oscillations at the later stage of training, also shown previously in figure 8.10.

Let us do a bit of visualization to put things in perspective. Recall the multiple mini-batch loss curves in figure 8.3 and figure 8.4. We can plot a loss curve for each example used in SGD, and each loss curve will be U-shaped due to being quadratic in $w$. As shown in figure 8.13, we plot out the upper bound $U$ and lower bound $L$ for the region of confusion, as indicated by the two dashed lines. The SGD optimizer will make good progress in the two surrounding far-out regions and move towards the region of confusion. Upon entering the region of confusion, however, the optimizer starts to fluctuate and jumps back and forth, forever. This explains why early stopping is a practically useful technique in training neural networks.

Note that falling into the region of confusion is not necessarily bad. When training large neural networks using real data, the loss curve will typically be highly dimensional and highly non-convex. Oscillations will push SGD away from sharp minima and move towards regions with small gradient norms, a property of implicit regularization as introduced in the previous section. However, when looking at this property from an optimization perspective, SGD is not a good optimizer due to these oscillations.

## 8.2.2 SGD's convergence in expectation

The SGD optimizer is a great tool that allows us to optimize over large datasets using randomized mini-batches and still obtain the same true gradient on average (the unbiasedness property). However, more than having an unbiased estimator is needed, as an estimator could suffer from a high variance problem but still displays the unbiasedness property. As a result, we would like to have an unbiased estimator that obtains a low variance with a high probability on average. And when the estimator's variance tends toward zero, we say that the estimator has converged.
