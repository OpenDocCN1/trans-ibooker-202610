# 2.6.4 Reduced generalization with high model complexity

The classical view on generalization essentially states that the two reducible sources of error, namely the bias and the variance, cannot be reduced simultaneously. When the bias is high and the variance is low, the model is relatively simple and tends to underfit the training data, which often happens at the initial stage of training. In this scenario, a common direction towards empirical risk minimization is to train a more complex model. Increasing model complexity often leads to relatively large decrease in the bias and a slight increase in the variance term, thus reducing the overall combined cost. This increased bias means that our model will be biased and off-target in expectation. However, the overall benefit in empirical risk minimization still outweighs the downside from a possibly biased estimation when building a generalizable model.

The classical view on generalization also indicates that a high-variance model is less likely to generalize to the future test set. When a model becomes arbitrarily complex, it tends to overfit the training data and even has the potential to perfectly interpolate the training data, resulting in zero training error. However, such a model tends to have a high variance as it also fits the noise in the training data. Therefore, when using the model to score new data points, a slight change in these data points would result in a very different output, making the prediction sensitive and unstable.
