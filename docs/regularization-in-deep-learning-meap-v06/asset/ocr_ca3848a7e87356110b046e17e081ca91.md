When the SGD optimizer is located within the far-out region, i.e., w < L or w > U, both the single-observation gradient ∇wQ(w) and the full-batch gradient ∇wQ(w) will take the same sign. In other words, SGD will take the same direction of travel as GD, and each GD update corresponds to a total of N updates in SGD. The SGD will move towards the region of confusion. This explains the good and fast progress at the initial training stage, as shown in figure 8.10.

However, when the SGD optimizer enters the region of confusion, the uniform sign is no longer guaranteed. Depending on the specific observation used in gradient calculation, the SGD optimizer will instead bounce around within the region of confusion due to stochasticity in the direction of travel. This explains the oscillations at the later stage of training, also shown previously in figure 8.10.

Let us do a bit of visualization to put things in perspective. Recall the multiple mini-batch loss curves in figure 8.3 and figure 8.4. We can plot a loss curve for each example used in SGD, and each loss curve will be U-shaped due to being quadratic in w. As shown in figure 8.13, we plot out the upper bound U and lower bound L for the region of confusion, as indicated by the two dashed lines. The SGD optimizer will make good progress in the two surrounding far-out regions and move towards the region of confusion. Upon entering the region of confusion, however, the optimizer starts to fluctuate and jumps back and forth, forever. This explains why early stopping is a practically useful technique in training neural networks.
