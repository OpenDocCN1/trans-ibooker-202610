A common choice is to use the second derivative of the fitted function and penalize the integrated squared second derivatives while minimizing the *residual sum of squares* (RSS) between the model prediction and the target outcome. Denote the penalty on the second derivative as $J(\mathbf{w}) = \int f''_w(x)^2 dx$. $J(\mathbf{w})$ will be used to control the curvature of the model. In this case, the resulting model, which is called the smoothing spline, has the following cost function:

$Q(\mathbf{w}) = \sum^n_{i=1} \left(y_i - f_w(\mathbf{x}_i)\right)^2 + \lambda \int f''_w(x)^2 dx$

where $\lambda$ is a weighting factor that controls the tradeoff between the goodness of fit to the training data and the roughness of the resulting functional form. This is a hyper-parameter that is often selected based on cross validation procedure. A large $\lambda$ generates a smooth curve while a small $\lambda$ results in a rough curve. As $\lambda$ approaches zero, the cost function will be RSS driven, leading to an interpolating spline that produces zero training error but takes a wiggly shape. When $\lambda$ approaches infinity, the roughness penalty based on the second derivative of the model becomes paramount, which constrains the form of the model to be linear. Note that the first derivative indicates the slope of the fitted curve, and the second derivative denotes the rate of change of the slope. The second derivative is a measure of the roughness of the curve and acts as a penalization term in the cost function. More roughness in the curve will result in a higher penalty in the cost function. Besides, this roughness penalty also saves the trouble of deciding how many knots to use when fitting the model.
