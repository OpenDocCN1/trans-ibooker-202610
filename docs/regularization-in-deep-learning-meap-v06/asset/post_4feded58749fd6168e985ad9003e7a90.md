Figure 3.3 The “double descent” curve that unifies the classical and modern views on generalization. When the model complexity is below the interpolation threshold, the classical bias-variance tradeoff dominates, highlighted by an increasing test risk as the model increases its representational capacity. In both cases, the training risk continues to drop as the model increases complexity and remains zero after passing the interpolation threshold.

This “double descent” phenomenon frequently occurs in machine learning models such as the polynomial regression in the previous chapter and deep learning models such as convolutional neural networks. As the model increases complexity in the underparameterized region, the test risk will decrease in the first descent and increase later on, following the U-shaped curve that highlights the bias-variance tradeoff. As the model becomes sufficiently complex to interpolate the training data, increasing the model complexity will enter the over-parameterized region and incur a second descent.

However, it remains an uncharted and exciting area of research as to why this almost universal phenomenon occurs. Things become counter-intuitive when models are over-parameterized in the modern regime. For example, the “double descent” phenomenon typically occurs when no particular explicit regularization technique exists in deep neural networks. It could, however, be avoided when using proper regularization techniques. Besides, a more extensive training set may hurt the test performance under the modern interpolation regime, which surprisingly contradicts our traditional view that more data could help the model learn. Understanding the model performance via the “double descent” phenomenon delineates the boundary of the classical statistical view. It sets the stage for further research on the statistical and computational aspects of model development, assessment, and selection in machine learning and deep learning models.

It is also important to note that each point on the risk curve represents a fully trained model using a particular algorithm such as stochastic gradient descent. Suppose we take a vertical cut at a specific model complexity. In that case, the training risk denotes the training performance of the best-trained model obtained with this particular model complexity. The test risk represents the corresponding risk at the test set using the best-trained model. Each level of complexity corresponds to multiple potential models, and the risk curves only show the performance of the best-trained model for each level of complexity.

### 3.1.2 Behind the “double descent” phenomenon

Despite excessive model complexity and near-perfect fit to the training data, increasing the model complexity after the interpolation threshold generates a low error in the test set. This empirical observation has guided the training of deep neural networks in practice: a large enough neural network is preferred to allow easy interpolation of the training dataset and thus result in good generalization performance in the test dataset. The preference over large and complex network architecture also stands even when the training data is corrupted with high noise levels.

The “double descent” risk curve is a framework that reconciles the over-parameterization scenario and subsumes the classical U-shaped risk curve on the bias-variance tradeoff. It reflects the modern interpolation regime instead of the classical regime separated by the interpolation threshold, i.e., the model that gives zero training error. The risk curve still follows the classical regime that balances underfitting and overfitting when the representational capacity is below the interpolation threshold. However, as the model increases capacity after the interpolation threshold, the test risk will enter the modern interpolation zone and decrease as the model becomes more and more complex.

Note that the representational capacity or complexity is represented by the number of parameters in the model. A more complex model means more parameters are used in the model architecture. As we increase the representational capacity by involving more parameters in the model architecture, we will observe a decreasing test risk even below the best risk achieved at the “sweet spot” in the classical regime. However, why is that the case? Why are larger models suddenly performing better even though they are overfitting?

One of the most widely accepted explanations lies in Occam’s razor mentioned in chapter 1: the simplest and smoothest model should be preferred when multiple models fit the data equally well. One way to measure model’s smoothness is the norm of the parameters. A model with a smaller norm in its weights is considered to be more regular and smoother. Such regularity and smoothness assumed in the model represent important inductive bias that complex models try to match. Therefore, when multiple models give zero training error, which could indicate equally good local (also global) minima, the model with the smallest norm in its weights will be selected. Such inductive bias is introduced when choosing a model from many potential candidate functions that fit the training data exactly.

In addition to the functional norm, other techniques can also be used to control the smoothness of the model. For example, stochastic gradient descent is an implicit regularizer that prefers a smooth and low-variance model out of many perfectly interpolating models. Averaging techniques such as bagging (bootstrap aggregation, to be covered in a later chapter) also plays some regularization effect on the smoothness of the fitted curve.

When multiple models give zero training error, stochastic gradient descent prefers a model with low variance, as represented by a small norm of the weights. While a model with a smaller norm gets selected when living in the over-capacitated region with a large function class, the resulting “simple” model also has a lower variance in the model predictions. The smaller-variance model is due to the implicit regularization effect when training deep neural networks using the stochastic gradient descent algorithm. There is an ongoing research direction that focuses on understanding this specific algorithm itself. We will discuss more on this in later sections on regularization using optimization.

### Norms

A norm can be considered as a real number that measures the magnitude of the weight vector W. One of the most commonly used norm is the lp norm, which is defined as lp = (Σ_{j=1}^{D} |w_j|^p)^{1/p} for a weight vector W^T = [w1, w2, ..., wD]. For example, when p = 1, we have l1 = |w1| + |w2| + ··· + |wD|, a form of penalty used in Lasso regression. When p = 2, we have l2 = √(w1^2 + w2^2 + ··· + wD^2), a form of penalty used in Ridge regression. Different norms have different regularizing impact on the model, which will be covered in a later chapter on regularization via model cost.
