
SGD iterations grows. The proof is based on a few assumptions, and readers who prefer less theoretical properties can safely skip this section.

The first assumption is on the Lipschitz smoothness of the gradient \(\nabla f(x)\), where \(f\) is a general function and can be interpreted as the cost function \(Q\). In other words, the function \(f\) has Lipschitz continuous gradient, whose definition takes the following form:

$$
\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \leq L \|x-y\|
$$

We can set \(x = \mathbf{w}_t\) and \(y = \mathbf{w}_{t+1}\) to characterize the distance in the gradients of two consecutive SGD iterates and use \(Q(x)\) instead of \(f(x)\), giving us the following:

$$
\|\nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t) - \nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1})\| \leq L \|\mathbf{w}_t - \mathbf{w}_{t+1}\|
$$

Since the cost function \(Q(\mathbf{w})\) is convex for both the case of regression using MSE (mean squared error) or classification using CEL (cross entropy loss), we can additionally obtain the following quadratic upper bound:

$$
Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1}) \leq Q(\mathbf{w}_t) + \nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)^T(\mathbf{w}_{t+1} - \mathbf{w}_t) + \frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1} - \mathbf{w}_t\|^2
$$

Since \(\mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t - \eta_t \nabla Q(w_t, D_{(t)}) = \mathbf{w}_t - \eta_t \nabla Q_t(\mathbf{w}_t)\), plugging in the previous inequality gives the following:

$$
Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1}) \leq Q(\mathbf{w}_t) - \eta_t \nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)^T \nabla Q_t(\mathbf{w}_t) + \frac{L}{2} \eta_t^2 \|\nabla Q_t(\mathbf{w}_t)\|^2
$$

Since we are interested in the average behavior of SGD iterates, we can take the expectation of both sides as follows:

$$
\mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1})] \leq \mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}_t)] - \mathbb{E}[\eta_t \nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)^T \nabla Q_t(\mathbf{w}_t)] + \mathbb{E}[\frac{L}{2} \eta_t^2 \|\nabla Q_t(\mathbf{w}_t)\|^2]
$$

As the expectation is taken with respect to randomness in the sampling of a single observation (with replacement) at each SGD iteration, we can move the constants outside the expectation operator and apply the expectation operator to each term due to linearity:

$$
\mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1})] \leq \mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}_t)] - \eta_t \mathbb{E}[\nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)^T] \mathbb{E}[\nabla Q_t(\mathbf{w}_t)] + \frac{L}{2} \eta_t^2 \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla Q_t(\mathbf{w}_t)\|^2]
$$

Now we introduce two additional assumptions to proceed. The first one is the unbiasedness of SGD compared to GD updates, i.e., \(\mathbb{E}[\nabla Q_t(\mathbf{w}_t)] = \nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)\). This is not a strict assumption since the SGD iterates will always get closer to GD as the number of iterations grows, given the law of large numbers. The second assumption requires us to bound the norm of the SGD gradient by a constant \(G\), namely \(\|\nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)\| \leq G\). Bounding the gradient ensures that the shape of the function \(Q(\mathbf{w})\) will not be too wiggly. Applying these two properties gives us the following:

$$
\mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1})] \leq \mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}_t)] - \eta_t \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)\|^2] + \frac{L}{2} \eta_t^2 G^2
$$

Moving around the terms and dividing both sides by $\eta_t$ give the following:

$$\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)\|^2] \le \frac{1}{\eta_t}\mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}_t) - Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1})] + \frac{L}{2}\eta_t G^2$$

Now we choose $\eta_t=c/\sqrt{T}$ so that the learning rate decrease as the total number of iterations $T$ proceeds, where $c$ is a constant. This gives:

$$\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)\|^2] \le \frac{\sqrt{T}}{c}\mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}_t) - Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1})] + \frac{L}{2}\frac{c}{\sqrt{T}}G^2$$

Note that the term on the left hand side represents the variance of the gradient norm.

We can add the previous inequalities from $t=1$ to $t=T$ on both sides, giving:

$$\sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)\|^2] \le \frac{\sqrt{T}}{c}\mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}_1) - Q(\mathbf{w}_{T+1})] + \frac{Lc\sqrt{T}}{2}G^2\\
 \le \frac{\sqrt{T}}{c}(Q(\mathbf{w}_1) - Q^*(\mathbf{w})) + \frac{Lc\sqrt{T}}{2}G^2$$

Where the second inequality follows as $Q^*(\mathbf{w})$ represents the (constant) global minimum and thus the expectation operator is removed.

Lastly, we divide both sides by $T$ to get the average behavior of the variance of the gradient norm:

$$\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla Q(\mathbf{w}_t)\|^2] \le \frac{1}{c\sqrt{T}}(Q(\mathbf{w}_1) - Q^*(\mathbf{w})) + \frac{Lc}{2\sqrt{T}}G^2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\left(\frac{Q(\mathbf{w}_1) - Q^*(\mathbf{w})}{c} + \frac{Lc}{2}G^2\right)\\
 = \frac{C}{\sqrt{T}}$$

Where we use $C$ to lump together the constants on the right-hand side.

In essence, we have proved that the variance of the gradient norm decreases on average as the number of SGD iterations increases. This means that the SGD trajectory will become more stable and less volatile as iteration proceeds, all the way until reaching a steady state, which we call convergence, at a sublinear rate of $1/\sqrt{T}$. ### 8.2.3 SGD: past, present, and future

SGD has been proposed for more than 70 years. Despite its remarkably simple rule, it remains the most widely used optimization procedure in training large neural networks. One of the biggest reasons goes to its better generalization performance on the test set than other alternatives, and understanding such generalization performance from a theoretical perspective is an active area of research. As we mentioned earlier, noise, or stochasticity, is typically bad for the optimization community but a good thing for generalization in the machine learning community.

We also discussed the gap in the theoretical analysis of SGD versus its practical usage on how data is randomly sampled in a mini-batch. Theoretical analysis sits on top of the assumption that each data point is randomly sampled with replacement to ensure the i.i.d conditions (independently and identically distributed). However, most deep learning toolkits provide procedures to randomly shuffle the entire training dataset and divide it into different mini-batches in advance, where the samples within these mini-batches stay fixed in later gradient calculations.

There have also been many revisions to SGD. Other than the popular “improved” optimizers like momentum and Adam, techniques such as gradient clipping and weight averaging are good tricks to improve the test set performance in most cases. Again, understanding the theoretical drivers remains an open research problem. If you have managed to reach this junction of the book, chances are you have used SGD in previous occasions and are as excited about its mystery and potential as the author.

## 8.3 Summary

- SGD provides an implicit regularization effect that helps improving the generalization performance of the neural network in the test set
- Both GD and SGD minimize the empirical risk when training a deep neural network, with SGD taking on stochasticity in the gradient based on a mini-batch (one observation)
- SGD provides an unbiased estimator to GD and enjoys convergence property for both convex and nonconvex function approximators at a sublinear rate (under certain assumptions on the gradient norm)
- The implicit regularization property allows the SGD optimizer to prefer a region with a low variance of the gradient norm; this variance becomes an explicit penalty term when viewing SGD mean iterates as a gradient flow
- SGD variants such as momentum and Adam help accelerate optimization but hurt generalization compared with vanilla SGD; faster convergence is not preferred in nonconvex optimization of deep neural networks
- SGD exhibits fast progress in descending the loss in the far-out zone but starts to fluctuate and oscillate once it enters the region of confusion in a univariate linear regression setting with provable theoretical analysis
- SGD’s convergence in expectation shows that the expected variance of the gradient norm gets smaller as the number of iterations increases
- Theoretical understanding of SGD’s generalization performance remains an open problem and an active area of research
