# 8.2.3 SGD: past, present, and future

SGD has been proposed for more than 70 years. Despite its remarkably simple rule, it remains the most widely used optimization procedure in training large neural networks. One of the biggest reasons goes to its better generalization performance on the test set than other alternatives, and understanding such generalization performance from a theoretical perspective is an active area of research. As we mentioned earlier, noise, or stochasticity, is typically bad for the optimization community but a good thing for generalization in the machine learning community.

We also discussed the gap in the theoretical analysis of SGD versus its practical usage on how data is randomly sampled in a mini-batch. Theoretical analysis sits on top of the assumption that each data point is randomly sampled with replacement to ensure the i.i.d conditions (independently and identically distributed). However, most deep learning toolkits provide procedures to randomly shuffle the entire training dataset and divide it into different mini-batches in advance, where the samples within these mini-batches stay fixed in later gradient calculations.

There have also been many revisions to SGD. Other than the popular “improved” optimizers like momentum and Adam, techniques such as gradient clipping and weight averaging are good tricks to improve the test set performance in most cases. Again, understanding the theoretical drivers remains an open research problem. If you have managed to reach this junction of the book, chances are you have used SGD in previous occasions and are as excited about its mystery and potential as the author.
