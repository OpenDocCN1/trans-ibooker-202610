In practice, multiple models generalize well to the test set, forming a collection of generalizable models, as represented by the dashed circle in figure 1.14. So our goal is to build a generalizable model that lives in the dashed circle. The purpose of regularizing an overfitting model is to control its complexity and thus increase the chance of reaching anywhere inside the dashed circle. A typical training process follows that, by minimizing the training error, or more specifically, the empirical risk (an indicator on model generalizability such as the average training loss of a model), the resulting best-trained model will hopefully generalize well to the test set, on average. This assumes the available training set and test set are similar in distribution, which may not be the case in practice. We will shed more light on the notion of empirical risk in chapter 2. However, for now, it suffices to know that our model evaluation criterion during training may not be empirical risk alone. A regularized version of empirical risk helps generalize to the test set, especially when the training set is not similar to the distribution test set.

To further elaborate on this point, it should be noted that the model that can perfectly predict the training data may not necessarily generalize to the test data. The reason is simple: when the model is exposed to different variations of input-output pairs and has learned from it, it is easy to transfer and apply the learned knowledge to new inputs within the range of observed variations. If the training set and test set at hand follow similar distributions, then our best-trained model will likely generalize to the test set. However, such distributional similarity may not always be available due to potential random noises or insufficient data available in the training set.

In order to close the generalization gap, we can try using deep neural networks, which operate on top of the "double descent" phenomenon, in a minimization setting. When using stochastic gradient descent to train deep neural networks, the optimization procedure effectively turns the high dimensionality from curse to blessing. When training deep neural networks, the stochastic gradient descent algorithm plays the role of implicit regularization, which tends to generate predictive models for both training and test set. Alternatively, we could apply more explicit regularization techniques to regularize the overly complex models.

Since different models tend to exhibit different traveling paths in the search space, some models have been empirically shown to reach the dashed circle more often than other models do. For example, sufficiently trained deep neural networks are usually very complex and can achieve very low or even zero training error. However, they still perform well on the test set in practice, which is somewhat counter-intuitive with our previous discussion - an overly complex model is more likely to overfit and thus less likely to generalize to the test set. As mentioned earlier, it turns out that this is the “double descent” phenomenon, which is unique to over-parameterized models such as deep neural networks.

Using deep neural networks, as the training proceeds and the model become more complex after passing a certain *interpolating threshold*, we observe that variance decreases despite zero bias. In addition, since the true function to be approximated is usually highly complex and non-convex, multiple local minima give equally good models, all of which could obtain zero training error or a minimal regularized loss value. In other words, we could end up with multiple equally good best models using the same training data. These multiple best-trained models could then expand to a circle, forming a total of three collections of models, as shown in figure 1.15.

It is not guaranteed to have a generalizable model when the training cost is zero or a particular regularization technique is applied during the training process. However, using over-parameterized models like deep neural networks, accompanied by proper regularization techniques, could greatly increase the chance of building a generalizable model.

### 1.6 Understanding the model training process

A typical model training process starts with some training data. In supervised learning tasks, the training data consists of input-output pairs. Each input entry could contain multiple features that describe one observation from different perspectives. The corresponding output contains the true target, acting as the correct answer to guide the training process. Model training aims to generate a mapping function, a model, that correctly maps a given input to the corresponding output.

A trained model consists of two parts: *parameters* and *architecture*. Parameters are the integral components of a model, and the architecture specifies how these components interact with the input data to produce the final prediction output. This predicted value is then compared with the ground truth's target output to produce an error metric jointly. Here, the error indicates the current cost on how close or far away it is between the prediction and the actual value. Following a particular optimization procedure, the training process adjusts the model parameters and sometimes the architecture to reduce the training cost. After adjusting the weights, the new error is calculated again, forming a feedback loop. The whole model training process is depicted in figure 1.16 below.

![](img/ee98e91e6845f41077b163bec833ee40_25_0.png)

**Figure 1.16** Example of a typical model training process. The workflow starts with the available training data and gradually tunes a model. The tuning process requires matching the model prediction to the target output, where the gap is measured by a particular cost function and used as feedback for the next round of tuning. Each tuning produces a new model, and we want to look for the model that minimizes the cost.

There are four components highlighted in figure 1.16: training data, model, cost, and optimization. Any or more of these components can be used as the touchpoint of regularization. For example, data augmentation, a widely used technique to train object detection algorithms, is a regularization technique that operates on the data. By adding random noise to input image data, such as shearing and rotation, a new and diverse set of images gets generated in various forms, which are then added to the original training set and exposed to the model for more robust training. Diversity in the training data helps generalization. We will share more details on how and why it works well for (typically) image classification tasks in the later section.

### 1.7 The many faces of regularization

There have been many different techniques proposed to play the role of regularization, focusing on data, model, cost, optimization, or some combination. Although being problem-specific, specific techniques tend to work better and give immediate improvement than others. The challenge, however, is that the existing literature on different techniques is very scrambled and primarily studied in separation from each other. This book intends to bridge this gap. By introducing popular techniques together in a unified framework, we hope to make the learning journey smooth and comprehensive via lots of practical and intuitive explanations and examples on the theory and practice.

Based on the previous model training workflow in figure 1.16, training a neural network starts with flowing the data into the model architecture, interacting with model parameters, outputting the prediction, calculating the error, and propagating the error feedback back to the model weights for iterative updates. This process naturally becomes the focus area of the book, covering regularization techniques on data, model architecture, cost function, and optimization procedure.
