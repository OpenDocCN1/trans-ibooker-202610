Note that a regularized model does not necessarily need to hit a zero training error to generalize well to the test data. When a model is overfitting, the classical statistics recommend using proper regularization techniques to shrink the model capacity and approximate the “sweet spot” in the classical U-shaped test risk curve. Alternatively, in the modern deep learning era, the popular approach is to increase the model capacity by throwing in more parameters, which would jump out of the overfitting region and generate a lower test risk. As introduced in the following section, this essentially corresponds to moving from the critical regime to either the under-parameterized regime or the over-parameterized regime, both of which have a low test risk, although the latter seems more approachable and simpler by simply adding more parameters in the neural network.

## 3.1.3 Extending the “double descent” phenomenon

The “double descent” risk curve is widely observed in training machine learning and deep learning models as a function of model complexity. So far, the model complexity has been focusing on the model’s size, i.e., the number of parameters used. It was later discovered that this phenomenon also exists as a function of training iterations for deep neural networks. This means that when the model architecture is fixed, training the network using SGD also exhibits a “double descent” test risk as the parameters converge through iterations.

One attempt that unifies the dependence of the “double descent” phenomenon is the notion of effective model complexity (EMC), which is defined as the maximum number of samples on which a training procedure can achieve close to zero training error on average. In the context of training deep neural networks, a training procedure specifies the size/complexity of the model and the number of epochs or complete passes of a given training set.

Given a specific training procedure, there are two types of “double descent” curves empirically observed:

- Complexity-wise “double descent”, where the doubly decreasing test risk curve is observed as the model uses more parameters and becomes more complex;
- Epoch-wise “double descent”, where for a model of fixed complexity, overfitting is initially observed as training iteration proceeds and later corrected by sufficient training epochs.

In addition, both curves are characterized by three different regimes by comparing EMC with the size of the training set:

- When EMC is sufficiently smaller than the training sample size, we are in an under-parameterized regime where the model underfits the training data. With a small number of features, each feature captures some information about the underlying pattern. Enhancing the training procedure in either the model size or the training epoch will lower both the training and the test risk. However, optimization is hard in this regime due to many local minima.
- When EMC is sufficiently larger than the training sample size, we are in an over-parameterized regime where the model interpolates the training data. Enhancing the training procedure in either the model size or the training epoch will lower the test risk. Optimization is relatively easy with more parameters than samples, since there are multiple interpolating solutions and some of the local minima obtained at these solutions are equally good global minima. This is similar to the underdetermined case when there are more unknown variables than the number of equations.
- When EMC is comparable to the number of examples in the training set, we are in a critically parameterized regime. Changing the training procedure may either increase or decrease the test risk. The fit for each feature becomes less reliable, which is atypical compared with the former two regimes.

Above is the informal generalized “double descent” hypothesis recently proposed in the research community. Under this hypothesis, the previous “double descent” risk curve in figure 3.3 could now be visualized with a new zoning approach that consists of three different regimes: the under-parameterized regime, the critical regime and the over-parameterized regime. As shown in figure 3.5, the under-parameterized regime indicates a model with a low EMC and a relatively big dataset; further exploitation such as increasing model complexity is needed to reduce the test risk. The same monotonic relationship between the model’s EMC and the test risk remains the same in the over-parameterized regime. The critical regime, however, does not guarantee a lower test risk when enhancing the model’s training procedure.

## A brief history of the “double descent” phenomenon

The “double descent” phenomenon has primarily been overlooked historically and only started to surface in recent years, largely due to the typical workflow on how classical models are trained in the past. The linear and parametric models often assume a fixed and limited capacity level given that only a few features are used. The number of features is often much less than the number of observations in the dataset. Powerful models such as the non-parametric family of models use smoothing and regularization techniques to control model complexity. In particular, regularization shrinks the effective capacity of the model and avoids interpolation, thus obscuring the occurrence of the “double descent” phenomenon.

The understanding of the “double descent” phenomenon was promoted by a few notable papers in this area. For example, Belkin et al. formally discussed the modern interpolation regime in their thought-provoking paper titled “reconciling modern machine learning practice and the bias-variance trade-off” in 2018. They provided evidence for the existence and ubiquity of the “double descent” phenomenon for a wide range of high-complexity models. In the following year, Nakkiran et al. studied this phenomenon in deep neural networks in their published paper titled “deep double descent: where bigger models and more data hurt,” which provided additional evidence on the doubly decreasing risk curve as a function of model size as well as training iterations. Besides, the authors also discovered the sample non-monotonicity phenomenon: having more training data may hurt test performance. This will be covered in a later section.

## 3.2 Double Descent in Polynomial Regression

In the previous chapter, we looked at fitting a nonlinear relationship between the univariate predictor X and outcome variable y using polynomial regression, where y is modelled using a certain degree of polynomial of X. In other words, we assume the model takes the form of y = fw(X) + ε, where fw(X) = Φ(X)w = Σp i=0 wiXi = w0 + w1X +···+wpXp is a polynomial function up to the pth degree of polynomial, and ε is an independent random variable with zero mean and constant variance. For example, when using up to the 2nd degree of polynomial, the prediction function takes the form of fw(X) = w0 + w1X + w2X2.

Training the model requires identifying the correct functional form of the predictor that best fits the training dataset. Increasing the degree of polynomial alone, however, will generate a wiggly fit with high variance. It would be good if we could generate an accurate fit using a high degree of polynomial, but at the same time control the wiggliness of the model by adjusting the smoothness of the fitted curve. In other words, a model that fits the training well should also be simple and smooth.

The answer is smoothing spline, which adds an explicit smoothness constraint when fitting the polynomial regression model. In the following section, we will first introduce the modeling details of the smoothing spline, followed by its implementation to connect to the “double descent” phenomenon.
