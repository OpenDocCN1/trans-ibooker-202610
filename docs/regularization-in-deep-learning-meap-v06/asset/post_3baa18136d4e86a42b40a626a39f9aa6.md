
\(\mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}, D_t)] = Q(\mathbf{w}, D)\)

This property says that the empirical risk when using mini-batch SGD and given a sufficiently small learning rate, does not increase in expectation compared to full-batch GD. And it follows intuition. SGD uses the average loss of a random subset of the training data to perform the gradient descent update. Since the expectation operator empirically corresponds to an average, these average losses \(\{Q(\mathbf{w}, D_t)\}^{t=1}_B\) (with a total of \(B\) mini-batches) are then averaged to approximate \(Q(\mathbf{w}, D)\). Moreover, since the expectation operator involves integrating the randomness in \(D_t\), the unbiasedness property essentially says that the expected average mini-batch loss in \(Q(\mathbf{w}, D_t)\) is thus equal to the average loss \(Q(\mathbf{w}, D)\) based on the whole training set.

The unbiasedness property of the SGD estimator is an important property to ensure the convergence of SGD, despite the random gradient estimates at each step. To prove the convergence of SGD, we rely on Doob’s martingale convergence theorem, which states that any super-martingale satisfying a specific boundedness condition must converge. We know that the loss function will be lower bound by zero; that is, the loss function we choose for either regression (i.e., mean squared error) and classification (i.e., cross-entropy loss) cannot be negative. Now the question is whether the sequence of random variables, namely the mini-batch loss term \(Q(\mathbf{w}_t, D_t)\), is a super-martingale.

Let us first introduce what is a martingale. A martingale refers to a stochastic process with a sequence of random variables, where the conditional expectation of the next value in the sequence is equal to the present value, regardless of all previous values. In other words, a martingale does not grow or shrink in expectation. On the other hand, the sequence of random variables observed in the form of loss iterates using SGD will be a super-martingale, which states that the future expected value is no bigger than the current value. Mathematically, we have:

\(\mathbb{E}[Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1}, D) \mid \mathbf{w}_t, \mathbf{w}_{t-1}, ..., \mathbf{w}_0] \le Q(\mathbf{w}_t, D)\)

Here, we condition the expected loss of the next iterate on the history of past iterates from \(\mathbf{w}_0\) to \(\mathbf{w}_t\). In other words, the stochastic gradient descent update algorithm could reduce the cost on average by making the average loss of the next iteration smaller than the current loss, when the loss iterates follow a super-martingale. Intuitively, this makes sense since in every single step of SGD update, the stochastic gradient vector \(\nabla_w Q(\mathbf{w}_t, D_t)\) points to the steepest descent of the current mini-batch loss function; we know that the expected loss at the next iterate \(Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1}, D_t)\) based on different mini-batches will be no bigger than the current loss \(Q(\mathbf{w}_t, D)\) based on the whole training set \(D\).

Now let us look at a simple one-dimensional example to show that the sequence \(\{Q(\mathbf{w}_t, D)\}\) is indeed a super-martingale, where you will find that the unbiasedness property of the SGD estimator plays a big role. In the following figure, we plot the loss function \(Q(\mathbf{w}, D)\) using MSE for a scalar weight \(\mathbf{w}_t \in \mathbb{R}\) and training set \(D\). For the next iterate \(\mathbf{w}_{t+1}\) that is not too far away from \(\mathbf{w}_t\), we can use the first-order Taylor expansion to approximate the value of \(Q(\mathbf{w}_{t+1}, D)\) based on the current loss value \(Q(\mathbf{w}_t, D)\) and the linear approximation, as indicated by the dashed line. This gives us the following:

Q(w_{t+1}, D) \approx Q(w_t, D) + (w_{t+1} - w_t) \nabla_w Q(w_t, D)

Figure 8.1 Approximating the loss value at the next iterate using first-order Taylor expansion.

Since SGD requires w_{t+1} = w_t - \nabla_w Q(w_t, D_t), we have w_{t+1} - w_t = - \nabla_w Q(w_t, D_t). Plugging back to the previous equation gives the following:

Q(w_{t+1}, D) \approx Q(w_t, D) - \eta \nabla_w Q(w_t, D_t) \nabla_w Q(w_t, D)

As we are interested in the average-case behavior of SGD updates, we apply the expectation operator to both sides, which gives:

E[Q(w_{t+1}, D)] \approx Q(w_t, D) - \eta E[\nabla_w Q(w_t, D_t)] \nabla_w Q(w_t, D)

where we have used the fact that E[Q(w_t, D)] = Q(w_t, D), since there is no randomness in the historical updates.

Now let us bring in the unbiasedness property of SGD estimator, i.e., E[Q(w, D_t)] = Q(w, D). Since the expectation operator and the gradient operator are exchangeable, we have the following:

E[\nabla_w Q(w_t, D_t)] = \nabla_w E[Q(w_t, D_t)] = \nabla_w Q(w_t, D)

Plugging in the previous Taylor expansion approximation gives:

E[Q(w_{t+1}, D)] \approx Q(w_t, D) - \eta \nabla_w Q(w_t, D) \nabla_w Q(w_t, D) = Q(w_t, D) - \eta [\nabla_w Q(w_t, D)]^2

Since \([\nabla_w Q(\boldsymbol{w}_t, D)]^2 \to 0\) and \(\eta > 0\), we have \(\mathbb{E}[Q(\boldsymbol{w}_{t+1}, D)] \le Q(\boldsymbol{w}_t, D)\), thus proving that the sequence of loss iterates a super-martingale. It eventually converges to a stationary point in a local minimum based on Doob's martingale convergence theorem. Although the loss at the next iterate will not always decrease and may sometimes increase due to randomness in the subset \(D_t\), we can guarantee that, on average, it will decrease over time. The expected risk does not increase in expectation. Figure 8.2 summarizes the sketch of the proof of SGD convergence.

Figure 8.2 Sketch of proof on the convergence of the SGD algorithm.

Note that not all estimators are unbiased. In fact, we rarely manage to have an unbiased estimator when using complex learners such as neural networks. However, things still work out quite nicely in practice. When the optimizer struggles due to high variance, we could improve such a situation by introducing a little bit of bias while greatly easing the high-variance issue, a topic on bias-variance tradeoff covered in chapter 2. There are many examples where we can obtain a good model using biased but lower-variance estimators, such as adding the LASSO penalty to the loss function covered in chapter 7. Note that having a biased estimator may not converge to a stationary point in a limited training time.

Now that we know SGD is a computationally economical algorithm that converges to a local minimum, the next question we would like to ask is how good the quality of the solution is. Is it the same quality as the one from the full-batch gradient descent algorithm, or better? As it turns out, SGD often delivers better solutions than GD because the resulting model generalizes better to the future unseen test set. Such generalization performance comes from the implicit regularization effect from SGD, as discussed in the following section.

## 8.1.3 Implicit regularization of SGD

To observe the difference in the learning behaviors between mini-batch SGD and full-batch GD, let us look at a simple cartoon example that involves two situations.
