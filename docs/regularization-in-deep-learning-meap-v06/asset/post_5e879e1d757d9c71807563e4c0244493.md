## 6.2 Regularizing deep neural networks via dropout

Recall that we briefly mentioned *ensemble learning* in the previous chapter, which is a form of aggregating multiple models developed based on multiple bootstrapped datasets for the sake of building a more generalized model. It turns out that we can also keep the same dataset unchanged and tweak the model architecture multiple times, each ending up with a different model and collectively aggregated to produce a final output. This is the idea of *dropout*, where we randomly (and temporarily) disable/remove certain nodes in a layer along with all the associated incoming and outgoing connected weights, according to a specific schedule and only during the *training* stage. This often happens in the hidden layer, although one can also apply it at the input layer.

By ensembling different model configurations, dropout leads to training multiple models, which contributes to reducing overfitting and improving the generalization performance. It can be considered as injecting random noises into the model architecture as a form of regularization. Plus, probabilistically dropping out random nodes in the network also accelerates computation during training.

One analogy on the dropout operation is as follows. In a classroom a teacher asks some questions, but the same two kids are always answering. The teacher asks them to stay quiet for some time and let other students participate. This way, other students get to learn better. Maybe they give the wrong answers, but the teacher (loss function) can correct them (weight updates using back propagation). By temporarily silencing a few extremely active students (nodes with high activation outputs), the whole class (layer) learns better about the topic.

Let us look at the dropout operation in detail.

### 6.2.1 Introducing dropout

Dropout was originally introduced by Srivastava et al (2014) in their seminal paper titled “Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting”. The central idea of dropout is to approximate the ensemble learning as if we were to train many different models and average their predictions, since explicitly building multiple models is computationally expensive when the dataset gets large and the model gets complex. The approximation happens by randomly disabling certain nodes in the network (multiplied by zero), making it look like a different model for the current training iteration. Doing so will prevent the model from developing too much reliance on a particular node (as represented by its large weights), since it may just randomly get dropped out of the network. When a seemingly important node is dropped out, the other adjacent nodes will assume the additional shared responsibility for the current training task and learn to be more robust and less prone to *co-adaptation*, where adjacent nodes exhibit similar behavior and thus hurt generalization performance due to the high correlation.

Consider the training process of a simple network consisting of two hidden layers in figure 6.10. At the first training iteration, the second node at the first hidden layer along with its associated incoming and outgoing weights are disabled. The same operation happens for the first node of the second hidden layer at the second training iteration. Note that both nodes are only temporarily omitted from the network and are restored upon training finishes. At the test stage, all nodes are present as in the original network and used for inference with scaled outputs.

In practice, the random disabling effect happens based on a preset probability upon defining the network architecture. In addition, we would obtain the same network architecture as the original version and would not apply dropout during the test stage.

Figure 6.10 Illustrating the dropout operation when training a simple fully-connected neural network with two hidden layers. The probabilistic removal of hidden nodes happens during the training iterations. Randomly dropping out nodes can also be considered as sampling a thinned network from the original network, achieved by cutting all incoming and outgoing connections of a particular node. Since most of the nodes are present across multiple sampled networks, or sub-models, the dropout process allows weight sharing among these thin networks across different iterations, transferring and communicating the weights across multiple sub-models. This weight-sharing scheme is in a similar vein to multi-task learning, a topic we will cover shortly in a later section of the chapter.

Note that dropout can be enabled at the test stage, whereby each prediction is generated by a slightly different sampled model, for a collection of predictions. These multiple predictions can then form a distribution, which is then used to quantify the uncertainty of the prediction using statistics such as the mean and the variance of the distribution, which approximates Bayesian inference that accounts for model uncertainty.

Besides offering an improved generalization performance by reducing overfitting, the dropout technique also brings the side effect of encouraging sparse representation in the hidden layer, instead of relying on explicit weight regularization added in the model's cost function.

Additionally, dropout will help keep the weights in the network small since the weight updates are spread out. Small weights are desirable since the model is less sensitive to small changes to the input features. We will cover more on regularization via the objective function in a later chapter, where penalties such as l1 and l2 norms play an explicit role in regularizing the model weights, keeping them small or even zero in value.

### 6.2.2 Inducing a sparse representation

CNNs are extremely good at learning geometric structures from image data. That is, good feature representations are automatically extracted and learned to capture the structural patterns at different levels. Among the many equally good feature representations, a sparse one is often preferred due to advantages such as improved robustness (i.e., fewer features selected) and faster computation (fewer weights are involved in model inference). Sparsity in feature representation is thus another inductive bias we place in the model, where only a subset of the original features (at the input or the hidden layers) is important. Hence, at the input level, sparsity can also be interpreted as an implicit feature selection mechanism.

There are many techniques that induce sparse learning, with Lasso regularization being the most prominent example. Lasso is a penalty term added to the objective cost function of the model so that only a subset of the original features will be selected in the final model. Such feature selection is achieved by setting the weights of unimportant features to zero.

There are two more commonly used Lasso variants: group Lasso and elastic net. The group Lasso assumes a group membership of certain weights, selecting or dropping the whole group of weights. The elastic net linearly combines Lasso and group Lasso, performing sparse learning at both individual weight level and the aggregate group level.

We will dive more into this particular technique in a later chapter on regularization via objective function. For now, let us put sparse learning in the context of a simple FCN. Figure 6.11 illustrates the effect of different sparsity-inducing techniques in feature representation. Without using any sparsity-inducing regularizer, the FCN as represented by the top-left figure will learn all the weights and keep all the resulting hidden features/nodes in the final model, which could be demanding in storage and time-consuming in inference in practical applications. A sparse model can often be learned to deliver the same predictive performance while remaining a much small size. Sparsity can take the form of weight-level selection using Lasso as shown by the top-right figure, or group-level selection of nodes using group Lasso as in the bottom-left figure, or a combination of both using the so-called elastic net regularization in the bottom-right figure.
