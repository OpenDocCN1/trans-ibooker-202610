The underlying sequence-to-sequence architecture is the backbone of transformer models.

Sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) transforms a given sequence of elements, such as words in a sentence, into another sequence, such as a sentence in a different language; sentences are sequence-dependent since word order is crucial for understanding a sentence. They have achieved great success in tasks like text summarization and image captioning.

Seq2Seq models are particularly good at translation, where a sequence of words from one language is transformed into a sequence of different words in another language. Google Translate started using a similar kind of a model in production in late 2016.

![](img/495f94a597dee9cc5b6b1e9654aa4339_14_0.png)

Figure 1-1. Seq-to-Seq Model (Neural Machine Translation)

Seq2Seq models consist of two parts: an Encoder and a Decoder. Imagine the Encoder and Decoder as human translators who can speak only two languages. Each has a different mother tongue; for our example, we’ll say the Encoder is a native French speaker and the Decoder is a native English speaker. The two have a second language in common: let’s say it’s Korean. To translate French into English, the encoder converts the French sentence into Korean known as context and passes on the context to the decoder. Since the Decoder understands Korean and is able to read that language, it can now translate from Korean into English. Working together, they can translate the French language to English, as illustrated by Figure 1-1.

## Transformers and attention mechanisms

Transformer architectures also transform one sequence into another one with the help of the Encoder and Decoder, but differ from existing sequence-to-sequence models. They were invented in 2017 to improve AIs’ performance on machine translation tasks. “Transformers started as language models,” Kilcher explains, “not even that large, but then they became large.”

To work with transformers, you need to understand one more technical concept: *attention*. An attention mechanism is a technique that mimics cognitive attention - It looks at an input sequence piece by piece and on the basis of probabilities decides at each step which other parts of the sequence are important. For example, as you read this sentence, you are always focused on the word you are reading, but at the same time your memory holds the most important keywords of the sentence to provide context.

Attention mechanisms work similarly. Going back to the Encoder and Decoder example, imagine that the Encoder writes down keywords that are important to the semantics of the sentence and gives them to the Decoder alongside the translation. Those keywords make the translation much easier for the Decoder, because it now knows what parts of the sentence are important and which terms give the sentence context.

The attention mechanism helps the transformer filter out noise and focus on what’s relevant: connecting two words in semantic relationship to each other that in themselves do not carry any obvious markers pointing to one another.

Transformer models benefit from larger architectures and larger quantities of data. Training on large datasets and fine-tuning for specific tasks improve results. Transformers are better at understanding the context of words in a sentence than any other kind of neural network.

## A Brief History of GPT-3

GPT-3 was created by, and is a significant milestone for, OpenAI, a San Francisco-based pioneer of AI research. Its stated mission is “to ensure that artificial general intelligence benefits all of humanity.” as well as its vision of creating artificial general intelligence: a type of AI not confined to being specialised tasks, instead performing well at a variety of tasks, just like humans do.

The mission statement goes on to detail OpenAI’s emphasis on the democratic and ethical aspects of AI. The democratic dimension lies in the decision to release the model via a public API, or application programming interface: a software intermediary that sends information back and forth between a website or app and a user.

APIs act as messengers, allowing developers to build new programmatic interactions between applications and users. Releasing the GPT-3 model via an API was a revolutionary move. Until 2020, the powerful AI models developed by leading research labs were available to only a select few of researchers and engineers working on these projects. The OpenAI API gives users all over the world unprecedented access to the world’s most powerful language model via simple sign-in. (OpenAI’s business rationale for this move is to create a new paradigm it calls “Model-as-a-Service”

where developers can pay per API call; we will take a closer look at this in chapter 3.)

OpenAI presented GPT-1 in June 2018. The developers’ key finding was that combining the transformer architecture with unsupervised pre-training yielded promising results. GPT-1, they write, was fine-tuned for specific supervised tasks to achieve “strong natural language understanding.”

In February 2019, OpenAI introduced GPT-2, which is bigger by one order of magnitude but otherwise very similar. The major difference between the two is that GPT-2 can multitask. It successfully proved that a semi-supervised language model can perform well on several tasks without receiving any training examples for that task.

OpenAI researchers experimented with different model sizes, while working on GPT-3. They took the existing GPT-2 architecture and increased the number of parameters by 100 folds. What came out as a result of that experiment is a model with new and extraordinary capabilities in the form of GPT-3.

While GPT-2 has 1.5 billion parameters and was trained on 40 gigabytes of text, GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters and was trained on 570 gigabytes of text. GPT-2 displayed some zero-shot capabilities on downstream tasks, but GPT-3 can carry out even more novel tasks when presented with example context.

OpenAI researchers found it remarkable that merely scaling the model parameters and the size of the training dataset led to such extraordinary advances. They are generally optimistic that these trends will continue even for models much larger than GPT-3, enabling ever-stronger learning models capable of few-shot or zero-shot learning just by fine-tuning on a small sample size.
