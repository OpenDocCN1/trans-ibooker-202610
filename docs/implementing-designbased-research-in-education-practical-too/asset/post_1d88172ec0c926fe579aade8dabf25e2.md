## Focus on Action

### Decision 1: Will This Study Involve an Intervention or Action of Some Kind?

The essential characteristic of DBR is that it aims to address a need in a real-world context. This entails action or intervention of some kind as part of the research process. Not all educational research includes this explicit focus on action. Educational research often aims to describe situations as they stand using quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods. For example, ethnographic approaches and phenomenological approaches aim to provide a rich description of particular contexts of study, but they do not aim to change those contexts. Other approaches focus on correlational or causal relationships in relation to past events; for instance, a cohort study that looks at the relationship between parental involvement in education and educational outcomes. Again, these studies do not aim to change the prevailing situation but to explore and understand it better.

The focus on action is not itself a sufficient condition for a DBR study as research with an action orientation can derive from many different world views and different research traditions and take different approaches. For example, a randomised control trial within a strong positivist paradigm can examine the outcomes of an action in a very controlled context. In contrast, a participatory action research project within a transformative paradigm can explore the direct and indirect impacts of action on the participants and stakeholders within a wide context of study.

Sometimes a study begins life in the researcher’s mind as an intervention study, but the initial exploration of the context expands, resulting in a full and rich work of its own.

### EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

A doctoral student wants to design, implement, and evaluate an intervention to positively impact on the self-efficacy of teachers in a certain context. However, as they explore the literature base and the research context, they realise that not enough is known about the context to design an intervention, and so they go deep into an exploration of the area as an ethnography or sequential explanatory mixed-methods research study or another approach which is appropriate to the research questions and context. If this is your situation, embrace it; do the exploration that your field requires and that can truly inform an intervention in this field in the future.

## Focus on Real-World Usability

### Decision 2: Is the Study Intervention Intended for Use in the Real World?

Decisions 1 and 2 are, of course, related, but it is possible to be action-oriented but take action in a controlled setting only. DBR always aims for real-world application in all of its messy circumstances. There are many “laboratory”-based studies that apply an intervention within a very controlled educational environment. Indeed, as we discussed earlier, the overemphasis on restrictive settings for interventions was a major driver in developing the DBR approach from the 1980s.

Experimental studies can be designed to test hypotheses through intervention, but the intervention may not be fully usable in a real-world context as that is not its primary goal. For example, if we wish to test a hypothesis regarding vocabulary development, we could design a learning intervention and test this through experimental study conditions, but although the intervention could prove effective in a controlled environment or in a one-on-one situation or with particular equipment or resources, it might not be feasible to run the intervention as designed in the messy world of a real classroom. Such a study would be valuable as a way to understand vocabulary development. However, given that usability was not part of the study’s focus, this may not be a study outcome.

DBR interventions may start out as small-scale pilots, even paper or wireframe prototypes for walking through with partners, but they develop through iterations of implementation, evaluation, and design into interventions of increasing scale in the real world. DBR researchers work through cycles to progressively identify the best problem to solve and refine a solution for the real-world context of the study. Effective solutions are those that address the correct problem and are usable in context. This is another example of why it is so important to focus on the research context in a holistic way that encompasses the individuals and the systems within which they interact. This allows the researcher to explore, with the research partners, what effectiveness and usability mean for these partners.

## Focus on Responsiveness

### Decision 3: Will the Research Be Responsive to Change during the Research Process?

DBR projects are highly dynamic as they are responsive to the context of study. The mindset of openness and curiosity entails that DBR researchers remain open to what may arise or be learned in the research context. This can affect the problem they intend to address, the solution they have designed, or adaptations that need to be made through an intervention based on what is happening in real-time within the study context. An experimental study cannot implement change in this responsive way.

The thinking behind this is that, because the real world is a changeable and messy place, in order to be effective, DBR projects need to be able to adapt. With respect to this notion that research must be adaptable and must maintain ethical accountability and rigour, all changes and their rationale need to be documented fully and consistently (Obczovsky et al., 2025).

## Focus on Collaboration

### Decision 4: Will We Develop the Project in Collaboration with Research Partners?

DBR, from its roots in Design, requires deep engagement with the individuals in the context of study, that is, all of the research partners. The process to identify a problem and refine a solution happens through collaborative enquiry and creativity. The collaboration is essential to the success of the endeavour, identifying the right problem to solve and iterating over solutions. Collaboration is built on a relationship of trust and mutual respect, which takes time and personal investment to establish (Kucharska, 2017). The researcher may be involved in the research context, but it goes beyond an exploration of their own practice or role to encompass and involve other partners. Different research partners may be involved to different degrees. For example, in a school context, the research partners can include children, teachers, parents, management, and more. Other research approaches might take place in the real world and be action-oriented and responsive but are not collaborative enterprises. Action research, for example, can be implemented within the researcher-practitioner’s immediate context of practice and not involve collaboration with others. Participatory action research, however, is inherently collaborative, as is DBR.

## Focus on Iteration and Non-Linearity

### Decision 5: Is the Approach Iterative and Non-Linear?

If you already know what you want to do, DBR is probably not the approach for you. This is because DBR is exploratory; it aims to examine a research context and theoretical field in order to identify a critical problem to understand and solve with the optimal solution through trial and further investigation. DBR progresses in iterative cycles of exploration, design, and evaluation whereby solutions are made tangible through prototyping and trialled in the research context. This is an inherently non-linear process where each iteration brings the researcher back to review their research questions and their designed solutions to refine and improve the intended outcome. The purpose of iteration is to learn and develop a greater understanding of the context and the underlying theory in order to come to a more appropriate solution. Other approaches, such as action research, progress in cycles as well. It should be noted here that DBR may move backwards and forwards even within one cycle of research, returning to refine the research question on the basis of the needs analysis, for example. In this way, DBR is inherently non-linear as it toggles between exploration and intervention and evaluation dynamically in order to address a complex problem in a theoretically grounded and contextually responsive way.
