## Iterate, Iterate, Iterate

The design process is inherently iterative. We go wide to start, generating lots of ideas; then we narrow it down for prototyping; then we make it tangible through prototyping in order to learn and prompt the next iteration, drawing on this new learning. This mindset recognises that designs are improved through iterations of failures and successes, all of which generate learning. Iteration can also bring us backwards into the previous phase to refine or change the core problem to address. DBR is non-linear. You must stay open and curious to what you can learn from the context and the process.

## Bias to Action: Make It Tangible

This mindset is critical to the design phase of DBR, as ideas are made tangible early through prototyping. The goal here again is not about perfection but building a version of your idea quickly in order to get feedback. A prototype can be a paper prototype or a sketch or a role-play or a wireframe or a storyboard. It needs to be a viable representation of the idea that you can test and get feedback on. The outcome of prototyping activity is not success or failure; it is learning what will allow you to move forward either by refining or by changing your question or by improving your idea or your prototype.

## Fostering a Creative Environment

At this phase of the DBR project, the tasks involve creativity to ideate and envision possible solutions to potentially very complex problems.

Definitions of creativity in the research are consistent in identifying originality or novelty as crucial to creative processes (Runco & Jaeger, 2012). In addition, they incorporate the dimension of usefulness or, more recently, satisfaction to address the internal and external recognition of the appropriateness or adequacy of creative acts and outputs (Abraham, 2025). This section sets out how to create an environment that can be conducive to generating and refining novel ideas that are satisfying and useful in how they address the problem space identified.

## Core Principles

Some core principles underpin the establishment of a creative environment. First of all, everyone can be creative. There is even some evidence to suggest that our brains may be hardwired to take pleasure in creative activity (Oh et al., 2020), which might suggest that creativity is even an evolutionary trait for the survival and adaptation of our species through millennia. Furthermore, there is evidence to suggest that there are traits, in particular openness, that are associated with creativity; that is, some people might naturally be more creative than others (Feist, 2019). There is also extensive evidence from empirical studies that creativity is a skill that can be cultivated, learned, and developed through practice (Runco, 2014). Some factors that support creativity include an attitude and environment of openness, playfulness, unstructured exploration, and non-judgmentalism (Bateson, 2015; Runco, 2014). Factors that inhibit creativity include restrictive structures and constraints or early or extensive judgement and criticism. In DBR in particular, which is firmly grounded in the theoretical and empirical evidence base of educational research, it is important to acknowledge that knowledge plays a critical and productive role in the creative process (Kenett, 2025). Establishing a creative environment requires us to strike a balance between generative and restrictive constraints, and this may vary across individuals and groups and sociocultural contexts.

## Preparing for Creativity

As DBR researchers at this design phase, there are a number of practical steps we can take to optimise the potential for creativity. We can plan the physical or virtual space and the activity types that will promote openness, curiosity, and playfulness:

- Do not use a space that is used for routine or standard work. You can even use the device of asking people to leave their normal roles and concerns in a physical or virtual ante-chamber – hang your everyday hat outside the creative room.
- Make space for exploration. Ensure that the space itself has tools and toys for exploration – from simple whiteboards and markers to Lego or Play-Doh. Having furniture that is movable or flexible can be really useful here, so that people can move around in the space as they need.
- Set the mood: creative ice-breakers can help lower tension and inhibitions to allow for more risk-taking. There are endless possibilities here, and you will need to think about your purpose, audience and context carefully in order to select the appropriate options. Some easy options include:
  - Person bingo for getting people to connect with each other;
  - What can we do with this? Pick an everyday object like a glass or a pen and ask people to come up with the most or the strangest uses for that object;
  - The mash-up: put the names of lots of everyday objects or products in a hat and ask people to pick out 2 and come up with as many ideas as possible for combining the two elements to make something new.
- Set some ground rules: this can be through discussion or modelling. The most crucial ground rule for creative exploration is “all ideas are welcome” – even the wild ones. The ground rule here is to suspend judgement or evaluation or critique until you explicitly look for it.
- Use language intentionally. How you phrase a creative prompt is crucial to maintaining an attitude of openness and possibility. The following prompts are well established in the design community to prompt creative activities, promote openness, and suspend judgement:
  - How might we…?
  - What if…?
  - Yes and …. (rather than “No but”)
  - In what world could this be true?
- Use constraints to get creative. For example:
  - Ask people to draw analogies – especially from nature or distant domains.
  - Envision future scenarios that are grounded in real experiences, for example, “imagine your students’ graduation 10 years from now.”
- Get outside and explore: getting out into nature and physical movement have been shown to promote creative thinking.

## Individuals and Groups for Creativity

There is extensive research going back decades on working individually or collaboratively to address design challenges. A core question here is: is it better to design in teams or as individuals? The answer is both, as there are pros and cons to both. There is evidence to suggest that collective intelligence as a measure of group creativity may be greater than individual intelligence (Woolley et al., 2010). Innovation and creativity can derive productively from the recombination of ideas across multiple individuals (Johnson, 2010). More specifically, more diverse teams may be more effective in solving problems than individuals or more homogeneous teams (Kurtzberg & Amabile, 2001). However, there is also evidence to suggest that teams sometimes gravitate towards less optimal solutions through social influence, that is, group think (Mason & Watts, 2012). For creative endeavours, a blend of individual and collective activities can optimise the collective intelligence of the group while addressing the negative impact of social influence (Bernstein et al., 2018). The optimal organisation for this phase of design should allow individuals space and time to imagine and put forward their own ideas and bring the collective together to expand, combine, and re-envision those ideas towards a creative solution. AI offers opportunities to support the creative process but can also generate a “group think” effect (Lazar et al., 2025). The evidence on how we can best leverage this is still emerging (Meincke et al., 2025). In bringing together the human and algorithmic collective to address design challenges, we must be mindful to reflect on how we work together to generate high-quality, diverse ideas.
