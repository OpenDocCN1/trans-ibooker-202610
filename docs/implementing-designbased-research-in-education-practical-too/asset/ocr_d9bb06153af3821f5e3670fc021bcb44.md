I am a white Irish woman, a mother, a sister, a researcher in higher education, an occasional artist, and much more. My personal identity and the theories I draw on are integral to my understanding of how people learn and develop intellectually and emotionally. I am particularly interested in how children's make-believe stories and lived experiences are articulated in research.

This description of my identity aims to illustrate how values and beliefs about education inevitably inform your research interests, decisions, and interactions. Recognising one's positionality is, therefore, essential: it enables researchers to be transparent about how their values and experiences shape the research process. A practical way to begin this work is through structured self-reflection, which helps uncover how different aspects of identity influence both the questions we ask and the relationships we form in our research.

# EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Early in my research career, I reflected on questions such as:

+   - Could being a white Irish woman affect how I conduct research in a country outside Ireland?
+   - Could my storytelling background endear me to participants with similar interests?

At first, I reassured myself with quick answers and moved on but later realised that such surface-level reflection did little to uncover how my identity shaped my research.

I recognised a lack of critical thinking in my own practice, an uncomfortable but important discovery. This prompted me to seek out a more structured way to interrogate my assumptions, which I found in the critical thinking approach developed by Elder and Paul (2019). Their model helped me move beyond reassurance to genuine reflection, enabling me to articulate my positionality with greater honesty and depth.
