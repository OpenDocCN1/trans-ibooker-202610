## Balancing Flexibility and Fidelity in Implementation

A central challenge in the implementation phase of DBR is navigating the balance between fidelity, which involves remaining faithful to the core design of an intervention, and flexibility, which involves adapting to the realities of diverse educational contexts. Rather than treating these as competing priorities, DBR frames them as complementary. Success often depends on preserving the essential features of a design while allowing for thoughtful adaptations that ensure it works effectively in practice (McKenney & Reeves, 2019; Penuel et al., 2011).

No two classrooms or institutions are alike. Learners bring different backgrounds, abilities, and interests; educators vary in their experience and pedagogical style; and resources differ across schools, universities, and community settings. Institutional expectations, curriculum requirements, and broader policy pressures also shape implementation. These differences mean that even the most carefully developed intervention is unlikely to transfer seamlessly without adjustment. Instead of seeing such variation as a threat to validity, DBR regards context as a central design variable (Barab & Squire, 2004; Reeves, 2000). The key is not to resist contextual influences but to anticipate them and build space for adaptation from the start.

Thinking about “context” broadly helps here. It is not only the physical space of the classroom but also the culture of learning, the rhythms of the timetable, the infrastructure available, and the wider expectations of the community. When implementation begins, these factors inevitably interact with the design. For example, a discussion-based activity might thrive in a seminar group accustomed to dialogue but falter in a setting where learners are hesitant to speak. Similarly, a digital tool may be highly effective in a technology-rich university but encounter connectivity or device-sharing challenges in less resourced contexts. DBR invites researchers to treat these scenarios as part of the design space rather than as challenges to fidelity. Adaptations can be made while keeping the underlying goals intact, for instance, replacing an online discussion forum with structured in-class debates if bandwidth is limited or providing sentence starters to scaffold participation in classrooms less familiar with collaborative dialogue. What matters is that the principle of the design (e.g., fostering active talk, ensuring immediate feedback) is preserved, even if the mode of delivery changes (Sandoval, 2014).

This mindset positions adaptation as an opportunity for learning. When interventions are adjusted to local needs, such as translating materials into learners’ first language, contextualising examples to cultural backgrounds or restructuring schedules so longer activities are spread across multiple, shorter sessions, these changes generate insight into how design principles can be realised in varied contexts (Jurow & Gutiérrez, 2016). Documenting such adaptations makes it possible to distinguish between the core components that must remain intact and flexible components that can shift without undermining the intervention’s integrity (Fixsen et al., 2005).

Ultimately, balancing fidelity and flexibility is less about compromise and more about intentionality. DBR practitioners are called to ask: which elements of the design are essential to achieving its goals? Which can be adapted to make the intervention viable in this particular context? Answering these questions enables both rigorous research and meaningful, context-sensitive practice.

## Core Components vs Adaptable Components

To maintain both fidelity and flexibility, it is important to distinguish between the core components and adaptable components of a design.

- **Core components** are the essential features that carry the theoretical foundation for how the intervention is expected to yield learning. They represent the “active ingredients” of the design. If removed or altered significantly, the intended effect would likely be compromised.
- **Adaptable components** are elements that can be modified to suit a particular context better without undermining the intervention’s integrity. These can shift in form, timing, or mode of delivery, as long as the underlying purpose remains intact.

This distinction provides a practical framework for managing change in the field. By explicitly naming which aspects are non-negotiable and which can be tailored, DBR teams empower educators to adapt interventions responsively while preserving the intervention’s theoretical coherence (Fixsen et al., 2005; McKenney & Reeves, 2019).

## Learning from Implementation Science

While DBR foregrounds iterative, context-sensitive design, it can benefit from insights developed in implementation science, a field dedicated to understanding how innovations are adopted, adapted, and sustained in real-world settings. Implementation science offers structured frameworks to analyse and support the scaling of promising interventions, particularly when navigating the tension between fidelity and flexibility, which is a core concern in DBR implementation.

To support principled adaptation and monitoring in DBR implementation, implementation science offers several practical tools and protocols. One useful tool is the IDEA decision tree (Iterative Decision-Making for Evaluation of Adaptations), introduced by Miller et al. (2020). The IDEA tree guides users through key decision points when making adaptations: assessing whether an adaptation is necessary, whether it affects a core mechanism, whether pilot data or stakeholder feedback is available, and whether changes can be reversed or need formal approval. By making these decision points explicit, the IDEA tree helps teams to avoid ad hoc or opaque adaptation choices and maintain transparency in how fidelity and variation are balanced (Miller et al., 2020).

Another complementary tool is the FRAME (Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications – Expanded), developed by Wiltsey Stirman et al. (2019). FRAME is a taxonomy and structured reporting framework for cataloguing adaptations: when they were made (pre-planned or reactive), who made them, what was modified (content, context, delivery, etc.), and the rationale behind them. Using FRAME (or comparable adaptation logs) encourages systematic documentation of changes across implementation cycles, which in turn supports later comparative analysis of which adaptations were beneficial and which compromised fidelity.

Finally, in recent practice, the MODIFI (Making Optimal Decisions for Intervention Flexibility during Implementation) method has been developed (Brewer et al., 2024). MODIFI is a practical tool that helps educators and researchers decide when and how to adapt an intervention during implementation, without compromising its core effectiveness. It guides users through a series of questions to determine whether a change is necessary, whether it affects a key part of the intervention, and what the potential consequences might be. The goal is to support thoughtful, informed decisions in real-time when adjustments are needed due to contextual factors like time constraints, learner needs, or resource limitations. By using MODIFI, teams can respond flexibly to challenges while still protecting the essential features that make the intervention work.

By embedding tools like IDEA, FRAME, and MODIFI into your DBR implementation cycles, you can strengthen the rigour of adaptation decisions. These tools complement DBR’s iterative, design-oriented philosophy by providing structured scaffolds for navigating messy real-world variation in ways that are transparent, traceable, and analytically useful. These frameworks complement more recent models of design-based implementation research (DBIR), which emphasise long-term, trust-based partnerships, shared problem-framing, and capacity-building routines (e.g., co-design cycles, joint work plans, and data use for improvement) to sustain innovation over time (Penuel & Toch, 2023).

DBIR shifts attention from “does the design work here?” to “how do systems learn to make it work reliably across settings?” prioritising implementation infrastructure, role clarity, and measurement for improvement. In practice, that means cultivating local champions, building coaching and professional development structures, and aligning policies and resources so adaptations remain faithful to core principles while supporting scale and durability.
