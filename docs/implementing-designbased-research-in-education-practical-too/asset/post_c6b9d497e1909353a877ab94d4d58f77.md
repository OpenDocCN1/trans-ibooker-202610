
## Establishing Roles and Norms Early

During implementation, clarity around roles and norms becomes operational: who does what, when, and on what authority? Because DBR invites flexible, context-responsive action, roles and norms should be explicitly negotiated and documented before the first cycle, then revisited between cycles.

Defining roles with clear boundaries is essential. The classroom educator (teacher or lecturer) typically takes primary responsibility for instruction and holds the authority to make in-the-moment decisions, such as adjusting pacing or addressing behaviour. Researchers or design leaders are responsible for maintaining the integrity of the intervention, managing data collection, and coordinating redesigns between cycles. Equally important is the establishment of collaboration norms. Teams should decide on how often and in what format communication will take place, for example, short debriefs after each session and longer analysis meetings at the end of a week.

Norms around feedback also matter: descriptive evidence should come before evaluative judgements, and discussions should distinguish between what happened and how it is interpreted. It is also wise to agree in advance on an adaptation protocol. For example, educators should have discretion over low-risk, in-the-moment changes such as rephrasing a prompt, extending a discussion, or swapping a resource. Medium-impact changes, such as reordering activities or substituting exemplars, should be flagged and reviewed by the team between sessions. High-impact changes, like removing a core component or altering assessment tools, may require explicit team approval between cycles. This process ensures that changes strengthen the intervention without eroding its theoretical backbone.

Clarifying data responsibilities and ethical safeguards is another crucial step. Teams should decide who collects which forms of data and agree on storage protocols, consent procedures, and privacy safeguards. Non-negotiables, such as the anonymisation of student work or restrictions on recording, should be documented at the outset.

Over time, DBR can contribute to capacity-building within schools. Educators engaged in the process often gain skills in data collection, critical reflection, and iterative planning. These tools enhance their professional practice beyond the scope of the project. Likewise, researchers deepen their understanding of real-world educational dynamics, enriching their own scholarship. Building trust is crucial here. Researchers must approach educators with humility, recognising the expertise they bring to the table. In return, educators are more likely to see research as a collaborative support, not an evaluation.

## PAUSE AND REFLECT ...

- Who will lead the instruction?
- How will data be collected (will the educator do quick assessments while teaching, or will the researcher take notes)?
- How will they handle unexpected issues together (e.g., if an activity isn’t successful, can the educator modify it on the fly)?

## Navigating Tensions and Misalignment

Collaboration is not always smooth sailing; differences of perspective may arise. An educator might feel a certain activity isn’t feasible, while a researcher, citing theory, may believe it is essential. It’s important to navigate these moments through dialogue and negotiation. Often, returning to shared goals helps; that is, the understanding that both sides ultimately want to improve student learning. Data can also inform these discussions: if a researcher is unsure why a teacher wants to drop a planned component, probing the teacher’s in-class observations or concerns (behaviour management? content overlap?) can reveal legitimate issues that the team can solve together, perhaps by modifying rather than dropping the component. On the flip side, researchers can share early data to explain why a component might be worth persisting with. Maintaining an open, non-judgemental stance is key so that practitioners feel comfortable voicing concerns, and researchers feel comfortable suggesting changes.

## Best Practices for the Implementation Phase

Drawing from real-world DBR projects, several best practices, which often evolve as DBR principles, can significantly enhance implementation. These principles support smooth rollout, promote collaboration, and increase the likelihood of sustainable impact.

## Early Stakeholder Involvement

Early stakeholder involvement ensures buy-in and contextual alignment (Coburn & Penuel, 2016). Engaging stakeholders early in the process is essential for the success of an intervention. Stakeholders may include educators, principals, students, and parents. Involving these stakeholders early in the DBR process ensures that diverse perspectives are incorporated from the outset. This early involvement helps tailor the intervention to meet the specific needs and constraints of each setting, fostering a sense of ownership and commitment among all parties (Fixsen et al., 2005).

## Why It Matters

Interventions grounded in early partnership input are more likely to be embraced and adapted effectively.

### PRACTICAL TIPS
- Host co-design workshops or focus groups.
- Use surveys or interviews to investigate potential concerns and ideas.
- Align intervention goals with existing school priorities.

## Develop a Clear Implementation Plan

A detailed plan provides structure, accountability, and clarity, enhancing implementation efficiency and effectiveness (Fixsen et al., 2005). A detailed and well-structured implementation plan serves as a roadmap for the entire process. This plan should outline clear objectives, define specific timelines, list required resources, and assign roles and responsibilities to every team member. Such planning is crucial, as it minimises ambiguity and ensures that all participants are aligned with the project’s goals (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012). Additionally, a comprehensive plan supports efficient resource allocation and provides a framework for monitoring progress, which is vital for making timely adjustments when challenges arise (Fixsen et al., 2005). The plan also acts as a communication tool that keeps all stakeholders informed and engaged throughout the implementation phase.

## Why It Matters

A structured plan ensures consistency and provides guidance on roles, responsibilities, and timelines.

### PRACTICAL TIPS
- **Define Learning Goals and Desired Outcomes:** Make objectives specific, measurable, and aligned to both theory and practice.
- **Assign Team Responsibilities:** Clarify who is responsible for teaching, observing, collecting data, analysing results, and documenting adaptations.
- **Prepare Training and Orientation Materials:** Ensure educators and partners are confident in using new resources, tools, or protocols.
- **Treat the Plan as a Living Document:** Build in review points to update the plan after each cycle of implementation.
- **Map Out Timelines in Detail:** Include start/end dates, milestones, and buffer periods for anticipated disruptions (e.g., school closures, exams).
- **Include Risk Management Strategies:** Plan contingencies for likely challenges (e.g., staff turnover, tech failures, learner absences).
- **Develop Clear Communication Protocols:** Decide how updates will be shared (weekly meetings, email summaries, shared online folders).
- **Embed Progress-Monitoring Tools:** Use checklists, observation rubrics, or short surveys to track fidelity and emerging challenges.
- **Plan for Partner Engagement:** Schedule points where feedback from educators, learners, or school leaders will be gathered and integrated.

## Provide Training and Ongoing Support

Partners and educators need sustained support to implement innovations with confidence and skill. In DBR, professional learning is not a one-off event but an ongoing process that evolves alongside the intervention itself. A single workshop at the outset rarely equips practitioners to handle the complexities of iterative design. Instead, embedded professional learning and training that is continuous, contextual, and responsive are essential
