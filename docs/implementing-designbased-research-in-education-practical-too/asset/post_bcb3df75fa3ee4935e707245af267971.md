## DBRC Framework (2003)

The DBRC framework (2003) is a seminal framework in educational design research that emphasises iterative, collaborative approaches to solving complex educational problems. This framework consists of key phases, including identifying significant real-world educational challenges, designing innovative solutions grounded in theory, implementing and refining these interventions in authentic contexts, and generating insights that advance both practical and theoretical understanding. The DBRC framework highlights the importance of tightly linking the design and research processes to ensure interventions are effective and sustainable. A hallmark of the framework is its focus on iterative cycles of design, testing, and refinement, which allows researchers and practitioners to adapt interventions based on feedback and observed outcomes.

The DBRC framework has significantly contributed to the field of educational design research by bridging the gap between theory and practice. It has shifted the research paradigm from isolated, theoretical studies to context-sensitive, collaborative approaches that prioritise real-world impact. By emphasising the co-creation of solutions with educators and focusing on scalability and transferability, the DBRC model has enabled the development of interventions that are both practically relevant and theoretically robust. Its integration of design, implementation, and analysis has become a cornerstone of modern educational design research methodologies.

| Features | • Emphasises iterative cycles of design, implementation, analysis, and revision. • Focuses on theory-building and addressing real-world problems. |
| Distinct Contribution | Established DBR as a paradigm uniting innovation and theory-building. |

| Example in Context | A school wants to improve collaborative problem-solving in mathematics. Researchers and educators work together to develop a new curriculum unit with collaborative tasks. After initial implementation, researchers collect data on learner engagement and problem-solving strategies, they refine the tasks, and they re-test them, iterating until optimal outcomes are achieved. |

## Barab and Squire’s Developmental Research Model (2004)

Barab and Squire’s Developmental Research Model (2004) emphasises the co-evolution of theory and practice in educational contexts. This model is grounded in the principle that educational innovations should not only address immediate classroom challenges but also contribute to broader theoretical understanding. They state that DBR “requires more than simply showing how a particular design works but demands that the researcher generate evidence-based claims about learning that address contemporary theoretical issues and further the theoretical knowledge of the field” (pp. 5–6). The Developmental Research Model (2004) features iterative cycles of design, implementation, and refinement, where interventions are developed in authentic learning environments and continuously adjusted based on feedback from partners. A unique aspect of this model is its focus on ecological validity, ensuring that interventions are context-sensitive and applicable across diverse educational settings.

Advocating for a symbiotic relationship between research and practice has greatly enriched our understanding of DBR. It highlights the importance of addressing real-world educational problems while simultaneously generating insights that advance academic theories. By situating research in authentic settings and engaging practitioners as co-researchers, Barab and Squire’s approach ensures that innovations are both practical and sustainable. This model has been instrumental in fostering the development of adaptable, scalable educational solutions and has shaped the way researchers think about the iterative nature of DBR as a dynamic process that evolves in response to contextual needs and challenges.

| Features | - Prioritises the simultaneous advancement of theoretical understanding and practical application. - Emphasises repeated cycles of design, implementation, and refinement to improve interventions progressively. - Ensures interventions are developed in authentic learning environments, reflecting real-world challenges and needs. - Involves educators, learners, and other partners as co-researchers, fostering collaborative problem-solving. - Stresses the importance of creating solutions that are context-sensitive and scalable to different educational settings. - Encourages continuous adjustment of interventions based on feedback and outcomes to ensure they remain effective and relevant. - Seeks to produce practical solutions while generating insights that advance broader educational theories. - Focuses on designing innovations that are not only effective but also adaptable for long-term use and across diverse contexts. |
| Distinct Contribution | Framed DBR as the co-evolution of theory and practice in authentic settings. |
| Example in Context | A school implements a new game-based learning platform for history lessons. Educators and researchers collaborate to integrate the platform into lessons, iteratively refining the game’s design and instructional alignment based on classroom observations, educator feedback, and learning outcomes. |

## Collins, Joseph, and Bielaczyc’s Design Experiments (2004)

A key strength of Collins, Joseph, and Bielaczyc’s Design Experiments (2004) framework is its use of multiple data sources to capture the complexity of classroom interactions, enabling researchers to address both immediate educational challenges and broader systemic issues. Collins et al. (2004) introduced the term “design experiments,” reflecting a focus on the empirical study of learning in naturalistic settings. Their model views classrooms as laboratories for testing how learning theories operate in practice. It differs from other DBR models in its strong empirical orientation: the systematic collection and triangulation of quantitative, qualitative, and process data to understand the mechanisms of learning. While the DBRC and Barab and Squire stress partnership and theory-practice co-development, Collins and colleagues focus on methodological rigour, establishing DBR as a scientifically credible approach for analysing complex pedagogical phenomena.

| Features | • Iterative design cycles to study learning in naturalistic settings. • Focuses on understanding the complexities of teaching and learning. • Employs a cyclical process of design, implementation, observation, and refinement to improve interventions. • Aims to produce practical solutions for immediate educational challenges and generate theoretical insights for broader applicability. • Encourages close collaboration between researchers and practitioners to ensure interventions are relevant and grounded in classroom realities. • Uses multiple data sources (e.g., qualitative and quantitative) to capture the nuanced dynamics of educational environments. • Seeks to develop interventions that can be adapted to various contexts and sustained over time. • Builds on existing educational theories while also contributing to their evolution through empirical findings. |
| Distinct Contribution | Positioned classrooms as research laboratories and sites of advanced methodological rigour. |

### Example in Context

A research team wants to develop a personalised reading programme for third- to sixth-grade learners aimed at improving comprehension and engagement. The programme integrates adaptive digital tools, educator-guided activities, and peer collaboration. To evaluate and refine the intervention, the team employs a robust, multifaceted data collection strategy:

+   1. Quantitative Data:
+      - Pre- and post-assessments measure reading comprehension and vocabulary development.
+      - Engagement metrics track time spent on the digital platform and task completion rates.
+   2. Qualitative Data:
+      - Classroom observations capture educator-learner interactions and learner collaboration during activities.
+      - Focus groups with learners provide insights into their experiences and preferences.
+   3. Process Data:
+      - Learning analytics from the digital platform tracking individual learner progress, highlighting patterns and areas where the system can be improved.
+   4. Educator Feedback:
+      - Regular interviews and surveys gather educator perspectives on the programme’s usability, alignment with curriculum goals, and observed learning outcomes.

Through iterative cycles of data analysis and intervention refinement, the researchers identify what works, what doesn’t, and why. For example, they may find that learners struggle with certain adaptive tasks and use this information to modify the programme’s difficulty adjustment algorithms. This comprehensive data collection approach not only improves the reading programme but also contributes to theories on personalised learning and digital intervention design.
