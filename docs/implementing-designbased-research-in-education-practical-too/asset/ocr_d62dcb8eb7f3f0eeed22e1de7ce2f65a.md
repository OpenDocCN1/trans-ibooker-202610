This reflects Fishman et al.’s (2013) concept of “principled adaptation,” which preserves the theoretical core of an intervention while allowing its form to be adapted in response to contextual needs. One way to ensure flexibility is by providing implementation options, so instead of a single prescriptive model, researchers should offer a range of delivery methods or tools that serve the same pedagogical goal. For example, a feedback strategy designed to increase learner reflection might consistently include peer review, but the format, such as using digital tools or paper, or delivering feedback orally rather than in writing, can vary depending on classroom conditions.

It is critical for research teams to articulate clearly what constitutes the “core” of the intervention, as these are the aspects that are grounded in theory and supported by empirical evidence. Everything else should be seen as flexible and be able to shift in response to setting-specific needs such as technological access, scheduling structures, teacher expertise, or cultural relevance. Fostering flexibility requires a mindset shift for both researchers and educators. Researchers must let go of control and trust educators to innovate within parameters. Educators, in turn, must feel empowered and supported to make decisions about how best to integrate the intervention into their specific contexts.

# KEY TAKEAWAY

Flexibility is not a threat to rigour. In DBR, principled adaptation ensures both relevance and integrity, making interventions more sustainable and more likely to succeed across varied contexts.
