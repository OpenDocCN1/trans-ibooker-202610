
3. Even if you include open questions, questionnaires are limited in the depth and richness of the perspective they can provide. They are valuable because they are short and easy to complete, and for that very reason, the data you can generate from them may be short and a bit thin.

4. Questionnaire responses are subject to response bias effects whereby people provide inaccurate or misleading responses for a range of reasons, from a desire to comply with what they think the researcher wants (the good respondent effect) to recency or ordering effects of the questions in the questionnaire. These effects must be mitigated in the questionnaire design.

5. It might be difficult to get people to respond to the questionnaire, and it can take time and effort to build up the number of responses that you want.

6. Finally, and maybe most importantly, people can only respond based on what they know or are consciously aware of. Sometimes people are not consciously aware of the issues or problems that you are trying to identify in the research context, and this may only become apparent through observation. Or they may frame what they want or need in terms of what is already available. The famous quote probably never spoken by Henry Ford illustrates this idea: “If I had asked my customers what they wanted they would have said a faster horse.”

This review of the pros and cons of the questionnaire highlights that preparation is vital to successful questionnaire use. If you decide to use a questionnaire, you can mitigate most of the risks by very careful questionnaire design and piloting, and this requires time and effort. If, on balance, a questionnaire is an appropriate instrument for you to use with your research partners in your research context, here are some Dos and Don’ts to make the best use of this valuable method:

## PRACTICAL TIPS: DOS AND DON’TS

### Dos

- Plan your questionnaire carefully: look for existing instruments in your field, use your theoretical framework to focus the items, make sure you are asking what you want to find out, identify what demographic information you need, consider response bias effects, evaluate validity and reliability.
- Pilot your questionnaire: as many times as you need to be sure that people are answering what you think you are asking them. Think-aloud protocols are very good for this, as they can highlight poor phrasing, ambiguity, or misunderstandings in questionnaire items. They will also give you a good indication of how long the questionnaire really takes to complete.
- Ask open questions as needed. The more open questions you have, the richer the data from the questionnaire.
- Consider the language and literacy needs of your research partners: use plain English guidelines in drafting your questions; if necessary, get the questionnaire translated into the main language(s) of your research partners; highlight read-aloud options for online questionnaires.
- Make it as easy as you can for people to respond. Offer the questionnaire in multiple formats (paper, online, phone); offer a wide timeframe to respond; make the questionnaire as streamlined and fast as you can.
- Offer participants the chance to follow up if appropriate.

### Don'ts

- Don't ask leading questions.
- Don't forget to include important demographic questions if needed (age, gender, etc.).
- Don't make the questionnaire too long because people will not finish it. Use your pilot to check how long the questionnaire takes. Every second you have your participants' attention is valuable, so make the best use of it: don't ask questions you don't need; don't ask too many open questions.

## Interviews (Individual, Group, and Focus Group)

Another staple of social science research, interviews are a very useful way to generate data about a research context. This method is about engaging in communication with people directly to learn more about the research context. The most common model for an interview is a spoken engagement, usually face-to-face. However, there are many more options available. It can be face-to-face or over the phone or online. The communication could use a range of media. It could be spoken, written, or use visuals or signs or a combination of these, as appropriate to the research context and the research partners. As researchers, we have to remember that an interview is a social encounter. It is an opportunity to develop and deepen relationships with research partners as well as to generate data on the research context. The mindsets of curiosity and empathy are particularly important in conducting productive and insightful interviews – staying open, curious, and non-judgemental in the discussion.

Interviews and focus groups are more time-consuming to conduct than surveys, but they can provide a much richer and in-depth perspective. Furthermore, they are more flexible, as the researcher can delve into interesting areas that come up through interviews. There are lots of options and variations in interviews that make this method highly flexible and adaptable for any study.

### PRACTICAL TIP

Some questions you might ask yourself to decide how to structure your interviews and focus groups include:

- Should I do individual interviews or bring people together in groups?
- Should I engage with my research partners face-to-face or over the phone or online?
- What media should I use? Spoken, written, visual media, or a combination of these?
- Should the engagement be synchronous (communicating with each other in the moment) or asynchronous (asking questions and getting responses over an extended time period)?
- Should I offer my research partners the option of using other languages in the interview?

Your responses to these questions will be guided by your knowledge of the research context, your preliminary engagements with your research partners, and your consideration of what will give you the most valuable insights for the DBR project.

For the student engagement example at the start of the chapter, there are many people in the research context that it would be useful to talk to, but we would have to carefully consider how to organise these in order to do the best we can with the resources we have available.

## INDIVIDUAL OR GROUP?

Individual interviews can provide great depth and allow us to delve into particular experiences with individuals. This can provide a very rich perspective on the research context from the individual’s point of view. You can be certain that this person’s voice is heard as part of the research as they do not have to compete for space as they might do in a group setting. For this reason, individual interviews can be valuable, if not essential, to hear the voices of participants who are from minority or marginalised groups or just those who have less power in the research setting, such as educational support workers in a school setting. If the focus of discussion is personal or sensitive, an individual interview might be most appropriate to allow honest and open communication on a topic that the individual might not like to discuss in front of a group. It also allows for individual aspects of an experience to be explored, more than in a group setting. Furthermore, as it is just a one-to-one interview, the logistics of organisation can be managed to best suit the participant.

However, there are circumstances where a group interview might be more appropriate. The group interactions in group interviews can generate interesting insights: group members can ask questions and pursue avenues that you might not have thought of yourself. The group participants can provide a variety of perspectives on the same issue or experience, which can both deepen and provide contrasts or additional validation of points raised. Conversely, however, group interviews can make for “group think” or a “party line” response where members of the group are unwilling or unable to raise a dissenting voice in the group setting. In a group setting, some voices can dominate, and others can be silenced for a range of reasons. Therefore, group interviews require skilled facilitation to ensure that all voices are heard and there is space for contrasting views. It is also important to consider the group composition carefully to promote positive interactions and reduce any problematic group dynamics. Finally, from a logistical point of view, group interviews can be both easier and harder to organise than individual ones. While group interviews allow you to talk to more people at once, it can be very difficult to find a time and place that works for everyone, and therefore organising the schedule of interviews can absorb a lot of time.
