## KEY TAKEAWAY

The goal for this phase of DBR is not to find solutions. It is to find the current best question to address. Remember: the question does not have to be perfect; it just needs to be good enough to start with. You will be able to refine and develop this through your project cycles.

## Chapter Summary

This chapter focused on the crucial first phase of DBR: finding the right problem to solve within your specific research context. Rather than seeking a perfect question, the goal is to uncover a question that is meaningful,

actionable, and grounded in the realities and needs of your research partners. This “best question for now” serves as a springboard into the design phase and will evolve through iterative cycles of enquiry and collaboration. Approaching this phase, human-centred mindsets of empathy, curiosity, and self-awareness will bring you closer to this goal. The methods outlined offer flexible options that you can layer together, helping you move beyond assumptions and towards a deeper understanding of your partners’ needs. Remember at this stage you are not looking for ideal solutions, just powerful questions that will drive the design phase forward. Stay open, stay curious, and listen to your research partners, and you will get there.

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Dara and Luca have completed the first stage of their Design-Based Research (DBR) project in the area of post-primary mathematics education. They have conducted a systematic review of the literature on student engagement and learning in mathematics, as well as a broader narrative review of the relevant fields. They also reviewed the state-of-the-art in digital tools being used to support mathematics learning in secondary schools. Their need-finding work with their research partners (including students, teachers, school leaders, and policy-makers) identified the following problem to solve in this current phase: students often struggle to see the relevance of mathematics, especially calculus, to their everyday lives, and this negatively impacts their motivation and engagement. This aligns with findings in the literature, which highlight the importance of contextualising mathematical concepts to enhance student understanding and interest. So, what is next? Dara and Luca now need to draw

on their theoretical and empirical knowledge, as well as the expertise of their research partners, to design a solution that is feasible within the constraints of the post-primary school settings in their study.

## Introduction

This chapter focuses on the design phase of DBR. Having identified the “best problem to solve for now” in the previous phase, this design phase creatively generates, refines, and formulates possible, tangible solutions to that problem. This phase draws heavily on the mindsets and methodologies of Design Thinking, Systems Thinking, Futures Thinking, and Humanity-Centred Design so that best practices in design can inform educational research. It remains firmly grounded in educational theory, also ensuring designs are evidence- and theory-informed. In this chapter, we explore the key mindsets for this design phase and the three steps that we iterate through to get closer and closer to a solution: (1) generating ideas, (2) refining and developing ideas, and (3) making it tangible – prototyping. At each iteration, what we learn will help us improve, whether that is by moving forward to the next stage or by moving backwards to change or refine the problem space or solution selected. The goal of all of these steps is to learn more and get closer to addressing successfully the needs of our research partners in their context.

## Mindsets for the Design Phase

### Stay Curious

Curiosity is a key mindset in DBR: remaining open to what might happen rather than being fixed in our assumptions. To stay curious means embracing uncertainty and being willing to explore the unexpected. This mindset also invites a spirit of play where we engage in creative exploration without a fear of failure, trying out possibilities, and experimenting. Curiosity and playfulness help us to put aside judgement and to imagine possible solutions and futures that may diverge from our own experiences and assumptions. Design Thinking is not about getting things right the first time but about trying things out and learning from this and moving forward in iterations.
