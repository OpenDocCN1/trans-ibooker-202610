
## FACE-TO-FACE OR NOT?

Whether you decide on individual or group interviews, you can choose to run your interviews face-to-face or online (incorporating telephone and web-based interviews). There are, of course, trade-offs with these options. Face-to-face interviews allow for greater responsiveness to non-verbal cues and body language. There are fewer possibilities for technical difficulties in a face-to-face setting compared with online, which may encounter connectivity and other problems. Depending on the setting, it may be easier to maintain focus in a face-to-face setting compared with the possible distractions when interviewees are online. Even though people are becoming more and more used to online interactions, it can still be difficult to manage turn-taking and discussion in an online environment. For example, it may be easier to use silence in a face-to-face setting to make space for responses, while online silence can be misunderstood as distraction or connection problems. However, despite these shortcomings, online options are often more accessible for a wider range of researchers and research partners. Online offers greater flexibility and convenience, as it is less reliant on time and space issues. As there is no travel time required for researchers or participants, it is considerably more time efficient. Researchers and participants may have the luxury to take part from a venue of their own choosing where they feel safe and comfortable. For this reason, and because of the option not to share video, online options may be useful for discussing more sensitive topics. When it comes to groups that may be typically hard-to-reach, you will need to consider carefully which option would make it easier for people to take part. Online might be easier if groups are geographically remote or have limited time available due to the nature of their activities, for example, shift work or night workers. However, if the groups are hard-to-reach because they are marginalised or vulnerable populations, face-to-face in venue that is easy and very familiar for them might be more beneficial in supporting their participation. For all of your research partners, you need to try to put yourself in their shoes when it comes to what kinds of engagements work best for them, and your job as a researcher is to try to create that environment face-to-face or online.

## WHAT MEDIA TO USE?

Most often interviews are spoken interactions that can be audio- or video-recorded depending on consent and the technology and equipment available. However, there are other options available. Research partners might like or need to use other media, such as written output, drawings, visuals, or signs and gestures, as an alternative or additional means of expression. Partners may also have different languages, including sign language, that they wish to use to express themselves. These alternative methods of expressing meaning can be very rich and insightful and allow individuals to participate who might otherwise be unable or unwilling to do so.

Rather than a spoken interview, an email or text message exchange can take place as a written interview. This can be particularly helpful where the interviewee might need some time to consider and craft their answers. This can also allow for the use of more than one language for questions or answers. The use of translation tools could facilitate this and be more feasible than live interpretation. Interviewees can offer a visual representation rather than a spoken one in response to a question, such as a drawing. This can be particularly effective with younger children. In both face-to-face and online interviews, media can be combined to provide a rich communicative repertoire that can then be analysed together. For example, participants can be invited to write their thoughts on physical or virtual post-its or whiteboards. Online whiteboards offer participants the opportunity to contribute in a range of ways (drawing, post-its, graphics, images) in addition to speech, and all the spoken and written interaction can be recorded. For group interviews, this can be particularly useful as it can allow people to share their thoughts all at the same time, even if some of them do not get to speak. This kind of input can be unstructured as an option available or structured as part of a workshop format designed to elicit discussion. Some examples of structured formats include:

- **The “Bus-Stop” or “Carousel” Strategy**: This is a cooperative learning strategy applied to a research scenario. You decide on three to four key questions that you write on stations on a real or virtual wall. The group is divided into small groups of two to three, and each group is assigned a question. Each group discusses and writes a response to their question on the wall. Then the groups circulate to the next board and read the question and response and discuss and add something new to the discussion. By the end, everyone will have had the opportunity to respond to each of the questions and to engage in a discussion on it with all the group – either synchronously or asynchronously as they read previous responses.

- **Jobs-To-Be-Done (JTBD) Workshop**: This is commonly used in innovation workshops in industry and applied to a research scenario. The key insight here is that, rather than asking people what they need, you ask them about what they do – the jobs that they need or want to get done. These jobs persist over time (e.g., to cook food, to travel, to get warm). It changes the perspective from how people do things to what they are trying to accomplish by those actions. In a JTBD workshop, the interviewer might explore a routine or process in the research text and look to find “pain points” – difficulties or obstacles – within that process. The workshop can use a JTBD canvas where participants can include their input in writing, or it can be summarised there by the facilitator. The canvas sets out a section for job steps, success criteria, emotions, and circumstances. As an example, a job from a teacher’s perspective might be “I want the kids in my class to progress.” One part of this job is to give kids feedback on how they are progressing.

- **Three-Step Interview**: This is another cooperative learning methodology that can be deployed here. In this method, the participants interview each other in groups of three, taking it in turn to act as interviewer, interviewee, and notetaker. After the three iterations, each small group consolidates and summarises what they have discussed and presents this back.

A further advantage of these structured formats is that it allows the research partners to consider what has emerged from their discussions live as they share discussions and also consolidate the responses.

For all of these interview options, stay curious and have empathy and keep listening.

## PRACTICAL TIPS: DOS AND DON'TS

### Dos

- Plan your questions and materials carefully, drawing on your theoretical framework to provide focus.
- Lead with curiosity (not judgement): use phrases like “I am curious,” “I wonder.”
- Approach the discussion always with respect, and consider this for the time and place of the interview as well as in your phrasing of questions.
- Provide the rationale for your questions: The reason I am asking is because ....
- Use language appropriate to your interviewee(s). Using plain English guidelines should help you avoid jargon and complex language. Be especially vigilant when you are working with children, people who do not share the first language of the interviewer, or people who for any reason might encounter barriers of understanding or participation through language.
- Ask open questions:
  - Draw on all the senses: “what does that look, feel, sound like?”
  - Draw on the emotions: “how do you feel?”
  - Dig deeper: use the five whys.
- Offer your research partners different media for expression as appropriate and make sure you can record these: speaking, writing, drawing, signs, gestures, etc.
- Be willing to deviate from the script. This follows from leading with curiosity. If a response is surprising or interesting or unusual, you can follow that avenue rather than rigidly following a question schedule.
- Remember to hit record! Ideally record your interviews using two different devices to have a failsafe in case one option fails.

In group settings:
- Consider carefully who you can or should bring together in a group. Consider power dynamics, official roles, intercultural aspects, etc.
- Set ground rules at the start in relation to confidentiality, respectful communication, and openness to all voices.
- Try to ensure equal participation across the group.

### Don'ts

- Don’t lead your participants’ answers: for example, “You probably think...”.
- Don’t argue or debate or force your opinion. This is not about you.
