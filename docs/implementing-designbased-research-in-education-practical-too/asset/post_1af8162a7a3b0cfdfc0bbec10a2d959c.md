
## Challenges in the Analysis and Exploration Phase

While the problem identification and analysis phase is essential for ensuring that a DBR intervention is both relevant and impactful, it is not without its challenges. Because DBR is conducted in authentic educational contexts, researchers must navigate the complexities and constraints of real-world practice.

- **Complexity of Educational Settings**: Schools and classrooms are dynamic systems, influenced by multiple factors such as curriculum demands, learner diversity, educator expertise, resource availability, and policy requirements. These factors interact in unpredictable ways, making it difficult to isolate a single, clearly defined problem. As Barab and Squire (2004) argue, the authenticity of DBR research brings richness but also increases the messiness of design, requiring flexibility and openness to an evolving understanding of the problem.
- **Diverse Partner Opinions**: All partners, such as educators, school leaders, parents, and learners, may hold differing priorities and expectations. For example, an educator may be most concerned with immediate classroom management issues, while school leaders may prioritise alignment with school improvement plans. Negotiating these perspectives requires careful communication, transparency, and compromise. Penuel and Gallagher (2017) highlight that engaging partners in co-design is essential for building shared goals, but it can also be time-consuming and politically sensitive.
- **Time Constraints**: Conducting a comprehensive needs analysis can be difficult within the limited timelines imposed by school calendars, funding cycles, or research deadlines. Researchers must strike a balance between gathering sufficient data to understand the problem deeply and moving forward with the design of interventions in a timely manner. Reeves (2006) notes that this tension between rigour and practicality is a defining feature of DBR and must be managed through iterative cycles that allow problems and solutions to be progressively refined.

Addressing these challenges requires a pragmatic mindset. Researchers should expect to revisit and refine the problem statement as new insights emerge, maintain open channels of communication with partners, and adopt iterative approaches that balance thoroughness with feasibility. By acknowledging and planning for these challenges, the problem identification and analysis phase can remain both rigorous and productive.

## PRACTICAL TIPS

### TIPS FOR OVERCOMING CHALLENGES

- Keep all partners informed of progress and decision-making to build trust and understanding.
- Be open to revisiting and adjusting the problem statement as new data are gathered and analysed.
- Use school assessment data or previous research findings to complement new data collection efforts.

The analysis and exploration phase is crucial for setting a strong foundation for DBR. Through comprehensive needs analysis and close collaboration with partners, researchers can ensure that their interventions are grounded in real-world educational challenges. This phase not only sharpens the focus of the research but also increases the likelihood that the intervention will be practical, effective, and embraced by those it aims to serve. ## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Teachers and researchers begin by analysing classroom assessment data, observing lessons, and interviewing learners. They identify a significant problem: learners understand theoretical principles (e.g., Newton’s laws) but cannot transfer this knowledge into practical investigation. The team conducts a needs analysis, noting that lessons often lack structured scaffolds for linking theory to practice. In collaboration with educators, they define the research question: how can technology-supported simulations improve learners’ ability to apply physics concepts in experimental contexts?

## Design and Construction Phase

The design and construction phase is the second crucial step in the DBR process (McKenney & Reeves, 2012). It builds upon the insights gained during the analysis and exploration phase, moving from conceptual understanding to a tangible intervention. At this stage, researchers and partners collaboratively translate theory and data into actionable designs, producing prototypes that can be trialled, critiqued, and refined. Unlike traditional research approaches that often separate design from implementation, DBR treats design as an evolving, theory-informed process shaped by iterative testing and adaptation (Collins et al., 2004).

This section explores the core components of effective design:

- Foundations of the design phase: from theory to practice;
- Developing prototypes: iterative and adaptive designs;
- Collaborative engagement in the design process;
- Iterative design cycles.

### Foundations of the Design Phase: From Theory to Practice

The design process begins with translating theoretical frameworks into practical, classroom-ready solutions. Drawing from the earlier analysis, researchers establish clear objectives that address the identified educational challenge and map them to the relevant learning theories. Equally important is ensuring that designs are data-driven. Evidence gathered during the needs analysis, such as learner performance gaps, educator feedback, or contextual barriers, directly informs design decisions. For example, if the assessment data reveal that learners struggle with transferring abstract knowledge into practice, the intervention might incorporate simulations, guided enquiry, or modelling strategies. In this way, theory and data are interwoven to ensure the design is pedagogically robust and contextually relevant (Plomp, 2013; Reeves, 2006).

### Key Considerations for Design

- **Alignment with Theoretical Frameworks**: Ground the intervention in established theories to enhance pedagogical soundness.
- **Data-Driven Decisions:** Use empirical insights from the analysis phase to ensure designs meet the identified needs.
- **Balancing Feasibility and Innovation:** Designs should be innovative enough to extend practice but realistic enough to integrate smoothly into classrooms.

### Developing Prototypes: Iterative and Adaptive Designs

Prototypes are central to the design phase, serving as early versions of the intervention that are deliberately incomplete and open to refinement. They may take the form of lesson plans, teaching materials, digital tools, or assessment frameworks. A prototype is not a final product but a working model that can be tested, critiqued, and improved.

Designers are encouraged to start simple, perhaps with low-fidelity prototypes such as paper sketches, mock-ups, or pilot lesson scripts, before investing in more complex resources. This ensures that revisions can be made quickly and with minimal cost. Each prototype should be adaptive, capable of evolving in response to feedback from teachers, students, and researchers. As Barab and Squire (2004) note, the strength of DBR lies in its capacity to produce “flexible innovations” that emerge from iterative cycles of design and use.

## PRACTICAL TIPS

### STRATEGIES FOR PROTOTYPE DEVELOPMENT

- Begin with simple versions of the intervention, such as paper-based worksheets or lesson outlines, before developing more advanced versions.
- Design prototypes that can be modified based on feedback and preliminary results, maintaining the iterative nature of DBR.
- Work closely with educators and school staff during this phase to create interventions that fit seamlessly into the existing curriculum and teaching practices.

### Collaborative Engagement in the Design Process

Collaboration is not optional in DBR, as it is the engine that drives the design process. Educators and school staff bring invaluable contextual expertise, while researchers contribute theoretical and methodological knowledge. Engaging both groups in co-design workshops, feedback sessions, and classroom trials ensures that prototypes are both grounded in evidence and feasible for day-to-day practice.

Such collaboration also fosters professional growth for educators, who often gain rich insights into research methodologies, and for researchers, who deepen their understanding of the complexities of classroom practice. Moreover, collaboration enhances ownership: when educators are actively involved in shaping prototypes, they are more invested in successful implementation and sustainability (Penuel & Gallagher, 2017).

## PRACTICAL TIPS

### STRATEGIES FOR EFFECTIVE COLLABORATION

- **Design Workshops:** Host workshops where researchers and educators brainstorm features, discuss potential challenges, and co-create materials.
- **Feedback Loops:** Implement structured feedback sessions where practitioners can review prototypes and suggest modifications.
- **Classroom Trials:** Arrange for small-scale, informal trials in classrooms to gather preliminary insights and make immediate adjustments.

### Iterative Design Cycles

DBR recognises that no design is perfect on the first attempt. Prototypes undergo multiple cycles of testing, feedback, and revision, with each iteration bringing the intervention closer to an effective and scalable solution. These cycles may begin with small pilot trials in a single classroom, followed by refinements, and then expanded to multiple sites for further testing.
