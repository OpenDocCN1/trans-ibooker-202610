+   - **Uncontrolled Variables**
+   
+     Unlike laboratory conditions, authentic educational settings come with numerous uncontrollable factors such as learner behaviour, educator practices, varying resources, and school culture. For example, a lesson may be disrupted by timetable changes, or an educator might interpret the intervention differently from how it was designed. Rather than viewing these as failures, DBR treats them as valuable data points that highlight how an intervention functions in complex, real-world systems. For researchers, this means building flexibility into research plans and recognising that “messy” data often provides the richest insights.
+   - **Ethical Considerations**
+   
+     Conducting DBR in schools involves balancing the dual roles of researcher and partner. Ethical issues, such as ensuring minimal disruption to learning, safeguarding anonymity, and obtaining informed consent, can be more complex in naturalistic settings than in tightly controlled studies. Researchers should work closely with supervisors and schools to create clear consent processes, use child-friendly explanations, and maintain transparency about how data will be used. Importantly, they should design interventions that add value to learners’ experiences, so participation feels meaningful rather than burdensome.
+   - **Logistical Constraints**
+   
+     Scheduling, negotiating with school principals, and adapting to timetables can significantly affect how smoothly research unfolds. School life is often unpredictable. For example, assemblies, inspections, or unexpected absences can disrupt planned activities. For researchers, it is wise to plan for more time than initially anticipated, maintain open communication with educators, and be prepared to adjust research activities without losing focus on the overall goals. Even small pilot sessions can provide valuable opportunities for iteration if larger plans are delayed.

# *Implications for Researchers*

Recognising these challenges early allows researchers to set realistic expectations. Rather than aiming for “perfect” implementation, researchers should focus on documenting adaptations, reflecting on what they reveal about the relationship between theory and practice, and viewing obstacles as opportunities to refine both the intervention and their own understanding of the DBR process.
