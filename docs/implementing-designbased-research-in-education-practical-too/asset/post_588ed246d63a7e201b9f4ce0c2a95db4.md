## Aligning Evaluation with Goals and Principles

DBR is committed to addressing practical problems in real educational settings while also generating new theoretical insights. To achieve this dual purpose, evaluation must be aligned with both the research goals and design principles from the outset. Rather than treating evaluation as an afterthought, DBR embeds it throughout the iterative design process. This integration ensures that evaluation measures capture not only how well the intervention works in context but also how it contributes to the theoretical understanding. A DBR study is, therefore, considered successful only when it both addresses a genuine educational problem and advances scholarly knowledge. This requires an evaluation process capable of producing evidence of practical impact as well as theoretical significance (Bakker, 2018; Design-Based Research Collective, 2003).

## Methods for Evaluation

A defining feature of DBR is that interventions are guided by clearly articulated conjectures and design principles, which explain how and why an innovation is expected to be effective (McKenney & Reeves, 2019; Sandoval, 2014). Conjectures are theory-informed assumptions, such as the belief that specific scaffolds can strengthen learners’ cognitive abilities or that a new formative assessment approach may enhance classroom engagement. These conjectures form the foundation for developing evaluation criteria.

In practice, evaluation measures should be closely aligned with the design principles. For example, if a design principle emphasises improved student collaboration, suitable evaluation methods might include recording the amount of time spent in collaborative work or analysing the quality of students’ interactions.

A conjecture map is a particularly useful tool for this process. It visually represents the relationships between contextual factors (e.g., cultural, institutional, technological, interpersonal), design features, and the expected learning outcomes. Unlike design principles, general guidelines refined across multiple DBR cycles, design conjectures are context-specific hypotheses tested within particular settings. During evaluation, researchers align conjectures with observed outcomes by examining whether the intended learning processes occurred and whether the design features functioned as expected. Sandoval (2014) argues that mapping conjectures to DBR outcomes enables researchers to test whether the theoretical concepts embedded in a design hold true in practice. This process strengthens the connection between theory and implementation, resulting in design principles that are both empirically validated and theoretically grounded (Reeves & McKenney, 2012).

In essence, aligning evaluation with the guiding principles allows researchers to determine not only whether a design is effective but also why it is effective. This is a pivotal contribution of DBR to the advancement of educational theory.

## Evaluation in Practice

In the context of complex innovations in real-world settings with multiple potential outcomes, researchers collaborating with educators must generate evidence that is both practical and scholarly. This evidence should contribute to improving practice and generating knowledge across diverse educational levels.

To begin, researchers can formulate a conjecture that predicts the best research-informed guess and reasons behind how specific design features are expected to yield learning outcomes. Subsequently, a design principle should be established based on the evidence generated through the testing and refinement of the design.

There are various methods for evaluating learning outcomes, as shown in these two examples.

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

## EVALUATING OUTCOMES: EXAMPLE 1

Conjecture: Introducing a novel discussion strategy in preschool settings will support the development of young children’s conversational dialogue by providing socially meaningful opportunities for communication.

Design Principle: The sustained implementation of planned discussion strategies embedded in everyday classroom routines can foster the development of conversational competency in preschool children.

**Evaluating Outcomes:** In a preschool setting, a DBR study is designed to enhance the conversational dialogue of young children through a novel discussion strategy. The evaluation process may involve incorporating audio and video recordings of classroom discussions, documenting observations in reflective journals, transcribing all text, coding, and generating themes. Data analysis is supported through reflection on the study conjecture and design principle.

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

## EVALUATING OUTCOMES: EXAMPLE 2

**Conjecture:** Embedding structured self-regulated learning supports such as goal-setting tools, reflection prompts, or planning templates will activate learners’ meta-cognitive awareness and promote independent learning.

**Design Principle:** Integrating explicit scaffolded opportunities for planning, monitoring, and reflecting within subject-based tasks can effectively support the development of self-regulated learning in secondary school students.

**Evaluating Outcomes:** In a secondary school setting, a DBR study is undertaken to foster learners’ self-regulated learning. The evaluation process may incorporate pre- and post-intervention self-regulation questionnaires, interviews, and an analysis of student work to identify the growth in the intended skills. Data analysis is supported through reflection on the study conjecture and design principles.

In both examples, evaluation methods are chosen deliberately to align with the intended outcomes. Qualitative observations and fieldnotes capture subtle changes in dialogue or behaviour, while quantitative measures (such as surveys or tests) can assess knowledge or attitudinal shifts. By aligning data collection with conjectures, principles, and the theoretical framework, DBR evaluations reduce the risk of overlooking key design elements or outcomes (Bakker, 2018; Reeves, 2006).
