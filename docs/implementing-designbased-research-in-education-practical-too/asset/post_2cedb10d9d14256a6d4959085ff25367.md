
## Balancing Theoretical Rigour and Practical Adaptability

Maintaining the integrity of the original theoretical framework while allowing for practical adaptations can be complex. Strict adherence to theory risks overlooking the realities of classrooms, while excessive adaptation risks diluting the conceptual contributions of the research. As Easterday et al. (2018) argue, the power of DBR lies in making theory “do work” in practice, but this inevitably requires ongoing negotiation. Recent work also highlights the risk of theory drift where repeated adaptations may move an intervention so far from its theoretical roots that its explanatory value becomes blurred (McKenney & Reeves, 2019; Reimann, 2021).

## Addressing This Challenge
- Document decision points carefully, noting why adaptations were made and how they affect theoretical grounding.

## Resource Constraints

Implementing theory-informed practices in real-world settings often demands substantial resources, such as time, expertise, funding, and institutional support, that may not always be available. Sustained collaboration with educators, iterative redesign, and longitudinal data collection are resource-intensive processes. Without sufficient support, researchers may face difficulties sustaining cycles long enough to generate robust findings, or they may have to compromise on the depth of data collected. This highlights the need for careful project scoping and for research-practice partnerships that can share responsibility for resource allocation.

## Addressing This Challenge
- Scope projects realistically at the outset, designing cycles that are feasible within available resources rather than overly ambitious.

## Contextual Variability

Theories may need significant adaptation to fit varied educational contexts, which can complicate the research process. For instance, an intervention grounded in a cognitive learning theory might work well in a small-group setting but need substantial modification in whole-class contexts or in schools with limited digital infrastructure. As Cobb et al. (2021) note, DBR must grapple with the tension between local adaptation and generalisation and how to honour the specificity of context while still producing theoretical insights that are transferable. This variability is not a weakness but a defining feature of DBR, demanding transparency in how and why adaptations are made.

## Addressing This Challenge
- Design with adaptability in mind, building flexible intervention features that can be adjusted without undermining theoretical integrity.

Taken together, these challenges remind us that DBR is not a “shortcut” between theory and practice but a demanding methodology requiring careful negotiation, strategic resource planning, and ongoing reflexivity. Addressing these tensions explicitly strengthens both the practical outcomes and the theoretical contributions of DBR.

## Iterative Cycles in DBR

DBR stands out due to its non-linear, iterative nature, distinguishing it from traditional, linear research methodologies. Iterative cycles form the core of DBR, facilitating a dynamic process where each cycle builds upon the insights of the previous one. This approach not only enhances the researcher’s ability to adapt to real-world contexts but also fosters the continuous improvement of educational interventions.

### The Essence of Iterative Cycles

In DBR, an initial design is implemented and assessed, followed by reflection and analysis. These reflections prompt modifications and adaptations, which are then tested in subsequent cycles. The iterative nature ensures that each cycle is responsive to what has been learned, allowing for refinements that align with the practical needs of participants and the theoretical goals of the study. McKenney and Reeves (2019) emphasise that the strength of iteration lies not only in refining the product but also in deepening researchers’ understanding of how designs function across varied contexts. In this way, iteration serves as both a mechanism for product improvement and a driver of theoretical advancement, ensuring DBR maintains its relevance to practice while contributing to broader knowledge. Cumulative, multi-cycle iteration strengthens both the credibility of design claims and the theoretical contribution of a study. As Zheng et al. (2022) note, it is through successive cycles that researchers move beyond local problem-solving to generate more generalisable insights about learning processes and design principles. Each iteration therefore functions not only as a test of the intervention’s practicality but also as an opportunity to refine the underlying theoretical conjectures that guide the design.

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

A team developing the Reading Pathways app begins with a prototype. In the first cycle, educators report that, while learners enjoy the stories, they struggle with navigation and need more immediate feedback. Based on these insights, the researchers redesigned the interface with audio prompts and adaptive scaffolds, which include immediate, tailored feedback. In the next cycle, the app is tested again, this time showing improved engagement. Each round of feedback not only improves the app but also generates insights that have the potential to contribute both to practice and theory.

## Flexibility and Adaptability

A significant advantage of the iterative process is its inherent flexibility. Unlike one-time trials, DBR is designed to adapt dynamically to the complexities of real-world educational environments (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012; McKenney & Reeves, 2019).

- **Responding to Unexpected Challenges:** Issues encountered during implementation, such as technological breakdowns, curricular misalignments, or differences in learner readiness, can be promptly addressed in subsequent cycles. This responsiveness prevents stagnation and ensures that the intervention remains viable in authentic contexts (Collins et al., 2004).
- **Integrating Participant Feedback:** DBR values the perspectives of educators and learners, embedding their input into the redesign of interventions. Feedback loops allow researchers to refine activities and resources in ways that are contextually relevant and meaningful for participants (Penuel & Gallagher, 2017). This participatory stance fosters ownership and enhances the likelihood of sustainability.
- **Refining Theoretical Underpinnings:** Iteration also enables researchers to revisit and refine the theoretical frameworks guiding their designs. Each cycle provides empirical evidence that either supports, challenges, or extends existing theory (Sandoval, 2014). In this way, DBR not only improves practice but also advances conceptual understanding, positioning flexibility as a strength for both research and innovation.

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

During the second iterative cycle of Reading Pathways, educators reported unexpected challenges. Some classrooms had unreliable Wi-Fi, leading to frequent app crashes. Instead of abandoning the project, the research team developed an offline mode so learners could continue reading even when connectivity failed. This quick adaptation kept the intervention viable in real-world contexts. Educators also asked for a way to track individual learner progress more clearly. This insight informed the next redesign: an educator dashboard for monitoring word-level progress.
