Sometimes a study begins life in the researcher’s mind as an intervention study, but the initial exploration of the context expands, resulting in a full and rich work of its own.

# EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

A doctoral student wants to design, implement, and evaluate an intervention to positively impact on the self-efficacy of teachers in a certain context. However, as they explore the literature base and the research context, they realise that not enough is known about the context to design an intervention, and so they go deep into an exploration of the area as an ethnography or sequential explanatory mixed-methods research study or another approach which is appropriate to the research questions and context. If this is your situation, embrace it; do the exploration that your field requires and that can truly inform an intervention in this field in the future.

# Decision 2: Is the Study Intervention Intended for Use in the Real World?

Decisions 1 and 2 are, of course, related, but it is possible to be action-oriented but take action in a controlled setting only. DBR always aims for real-world application in all of its messy circumstances. There are many “laboratory”-based studies that apply an intervention within a very controlled educational environment. Indeed, as we discussed earlier, the overemphasis on restrictive settings for interventions was a major driver in developing the DBR approach from the 1980s.

Experimental studies can be designed to test hypotheses through intervention, but the intervention may not be fully usable in a real-world context as that is not its primary goal. For example, if we wish to test a hypothesis regarding vocabulary development, we could design a learning intervention and test this through experimental study conditions, but although the intervention could prove effective in a controlled environment or in a one-on-one situation or with particular equipment or resources, it might not be feasible to run the intervention as designed in the messy world of a real classroom.
