Screen capture can be hugely valuable in recording computer-based activities such as how students set about a writing task or how teachers provide feedback through a learning management system. Remember that the observations can be narrow or broad. They can focus on very specific activities: doing homework, marking assessments, turning on the classroom equipment, classroom interactions, etc.

Researchers often start with unstructured observations in order to learn more about an environment and, from this, develop a more structured rubric that they can use for further observations. In order to be sure that you are fully exploring the research context, you will need to move around and go to different areas within the setting. You can try the “zoom-in, zoom-out” technique to change your perspective and observe at different levels or with different focus, as you progress. For example, you might zoom-in on one individual in the lunchroom, or you might zoom-out to get a bird’s eye view from above. The important thing for this need-finding phase is to generate enough observational data from the situation to be able to identify issues and needs within the research context through analysis. In this need-finding phase, you are seeking out surprises or unusual behaviour or workarounds or contradictions, as these can point to underlying needs in the research context that are not being met. When do you know you have completed enough observations? When you are beginning to recognise or predict behaviour that is repeated, you may have reached “saturation.” When you have noted some surprises, or you can explain an unusual behaviour or contradiction, you may be homing in on the best problem to solve in the research context for now.

# PRACTICAL TIPS: DOS AND DON'TS

Dos

+   1. Plan your observation schedule carefully — even if it is to be quite unstructured.
+   2. Pilot and practice your observation schedule and process — it is not easy.
+   3. Record the key information on where and when the observation is taking place.
