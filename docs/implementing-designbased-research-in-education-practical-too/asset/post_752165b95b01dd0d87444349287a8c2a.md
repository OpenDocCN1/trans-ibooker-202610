## THE LITERATURE REVIEW

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Imagine walking into a vast, sunlit library. Shelves stretch in every direction, filled with the voices of those who came before, educators, researchers, and authors of fiction and real-world stories. It isn’t silent, not really. If you pause, you’ll sense the quiet hum of ideas in conversation: what’s been tried, what’s failed, what has sparked change. Jerome Bruner, a key figure in educational theory, argued that the purpose of teaching is to go beyond the information given. He believed education should open possibilities for what could be and not just pass on what is. In a sense, that same principle guides the literature review. This is where your research begins. The literature is not just background reading. It’s a map of the territory: where others have ventured, what they’ve discovered, where the gaps and tensions lie. It tells you what has been, what is, and hints at what might still be possible. To conduct a literature review is to open the door to this living archive. It is your first step towards generating something new. You begin by asking:

- What do we already know?
- What remains uncertain?
- And where, now, might we go?

## Introduction

A comprehensive literature review is a critical first step in any Design-Based Research (DBR) study. This chapter explains why the review matters, the key decisions to consider before starting, and the different types of reviews that may be employed. It includes a real-time demonstration of a rapid literature review, providing readers with a practical sense of the process while also addressing common challenges and limitations. With this foundation in place, we now turn to why the literature review is indispensable for DBR and how it establishes the basis for the study.

## The Rationale for the Literature Review

In educational research, DBR plays a vital role across contexts ranging from early childhood education to higher education and beyond. As a methodology, DBR seeks to build evidence-based teaching and learning practices by drawing on existing knowledge where others have designed and tested innovations in real-world settings.

If your aim is to generate new educational practices, understanding prior work can both inspire your design and strengthen your study. Reviewing the literature allows you to see how others have approached DBR, the methods they have used, the findings they have generated, and the implications for both research and practice. It is equally important to examine the theoretical frameworks that underpin DBR studies, since these shape both design choices and analytical approaches. A review also ensures that your work does not simply duplicate what has already been done.

Ultimately, a literature review provides inspiration and direction while ensuring that your study is both grounded and original. With this rationale established, the next step is to consider the different review approaches available and their respective strengths and limitations.

## Types of Literature Reviews

Choosing the right type of literature review depends on your research goals, the time and resources available, and the depth of analysis you require. There are several types of reviews, each with its own strengths and limitations. Common approaches include systematic reviews, scoping reviews, rapid reviews, narrative reviews, and meta-analyses, among others. See Grant and Booth (2009) for a thorough overview of literature review types.

- **Systematic reviews** aim to synthesise knowledge by assessing the quality of existing research, identifying what is already known, and pinpointing gaps in the evidence (O’Reilly et al., 2022).
- **Scoping reviews**, by contrast, are used to map a broad area of research and highlight emerging trends, with less emphasis on evaluating quality (Arksey & O’Malley, 2005). While both approaches are valuable, they are also time-intensive and may not be feasible for all projects.
- **Rapid reviews** provide a more efficient alternative by summarising current evidence quickly, often to inform policy or practice (Wollscheid & Tripney, 2021). The trade-off is that rapid reviews may not capture every relevant study or explore all nuances in the literature.

In this chapter, a rapid review is used. This review type is particularly useful when reviewing recent publications, as it allows researchers to narrow database searches or exclude certain study types in order to manage time effectively (Smela et al., 2023). By sharing a rapid review in real-time, this chapter aims to give new researchers a practical sense of the process and highlight the key decisions that shape both the scope and the outcomes of a review.

Once you have chosen the most suitable type of review, the challenge becomes how to organise the process in a clear and manageable way.

> **KEY TAKEAWAY**
> 
> No single review type is “best”; the most appropriate choice depends on the purpose of your study, the questions you are asking, and the resources you have available.

## Structuring the Review

Creating a clear structure is an important first step. It can reduce the overwhelming feeling that often comes with searching for knowledge gaps across a large body of academic literature, while also helping you track your progress. Whether the review is written as a thesis chapter or an academic paper, a typical structure includes an abstract, introduction, methods, findings, discussion, and conclusion.

The methods section of the review is particularly significant. It should detail the type of review conducted, search strategies used, inclusion/exclusion criteria, approaches to analysis and any limitations. Always check with your institution and the target journal's guidelines for required formats of expectations.

## The Rapid Review Approach

Literature reviews help address urgent questions by drawing together current, evidence-based research. While systematic reviews often involve large teams and extended timelines, a rapid review can be conducted by a single researcher working within tighter constraints. Even so, careful planning is required to maintain quality and transparency.

Wollscheid and Tripney (2021) note that rapid reviews are more prone to bias than systematic reviews, largely because they cover a smaller portion of the literature. To reduce this risk and increase rigour, researchers can follow established reporting frameworks such as the PRISMA guidelines (Liberati et al., 2009). PRISMA provides a systematic way of documenting each stage of the review, from search strategy to inclusion criteria.

Using a structured approach like PRISMA not only strengthens transparency but also makes it possible for others to replicate the process. In theory, if the same criteria outlined in this review were applied again by another researcher, the results should be identical. Reporting in this manner enhances both the reliability and credibility of the review (Levac et al., 2010).

Rapid reviews can be efficient and practical, but following a clear reporting framework is essential to safeguard rigour and trustworthiness.

## Rapid Review in Real-Time

The following section models how to structure and document a rapid review for inclusion in a thesis or journal article. It is important to note that this represents only one possible approach; there are many effective ways to report a rapid review depending on context, purpose, and disciplinary conventions.

A useful starting point is the introduction. Although some researchers write this section after completing the review, drafting it early can serve as a working guide that clarifies focus and intent. The introduction should explicitly outline the review’s purpose, scope, rationale, and relevance and conclude with a set of guiding questions or objectives that justify the use of a rapid review approach.
