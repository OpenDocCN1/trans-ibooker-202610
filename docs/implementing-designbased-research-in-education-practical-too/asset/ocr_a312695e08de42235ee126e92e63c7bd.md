It became clear for some researchers that the simplifications required to construct experiments or interventions in laboratory settings made it problematic, if not impossible, to relate or extrapolate research findings to real-life educational settings. Ann Brown (1992), one of the pioneers of DBR, in her discussion of her evolution as a researcher taking a design approach, described this evolution during her own career as “an increasing trade-off between experimental control and richness and reality” (Brown, 1992, p. 152) and highlighted the need to consider context more holistically and comprehensively in her research. There was a greater acknowledgement of the situated nature of educational experiences and that context did not constitute a set of factors to control but rather was critical to the enactment and experience of educational practices, policies, and programmes in the real world. Leo Van Lier (2010), writing in the context of language learning, captures this imperative for ecological validity as follows:

> Research often isolates particular pieces of the complex puzzle in order to study them in detail. However useful this may be, it obscures the dynamism of the actual teaching and learning work that goes on, and cannot show the emergent and contingent nature of that work.
> (Van Lier, 2010, p. 5)

This emphasis on taking a holistic and ecological approach to real-world contexts was very much the focus of some of the first examples of DBR in the literature, for example, the Design Experiments of Brown, Collins, and Cobb in the early 1990s (Brown, 1992; Cobb et al., 2003; Collins, 1992).

A second consideration in the development of DBR in the early 1990s, drawing on the pragmatist philosophy of John Dewey, for example, was a greater consideration of the need to develop and validate useful theory for practice and policy. This was by no means new in the social sciences and can be summarised by Kurt Lewin’s phrase: “there is nothing as practical as a good theory” (Lewin, 1943, p. 8). This resonates with the need for a more holistic perspective of research contexts: not only should research acknowledge the full context of the educational situation deeply, but it should also generate knowledge and theory that are useful in that context. This positions DBR in the pragmatist paradigm (Weaver, 2018).
