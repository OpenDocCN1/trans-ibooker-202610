
Coke example, a leap was made from falling sales to a problem with the flavour formulation, whereas a more open exploration at the start of the project might have yielded a different problem from taste to solve. This imperative involves staying human-centred but also keeping an open and curious mindset to make sure one goes wide and explores possibilities before converging on a solution. The mindset and methodologies of Design Thinking can provide a framework for such exploration.

Finally, design needs to make ideas tangible early so that they can be explored and tested and improved in an iterative fashion. A large-scale public failure such as New Coke could be avoided through an openness to iteration throughout the design process. The path to good design is not linear. It involves being open to small-scale successes and failures along the path, which brings us through iterative cycles of exploration, design, and testing. Iteration can involve change to all and any aspects of a project – not just changes to a designed solution but also changes to the base problem we are looking to solve. In this mindset, it is no longer a question of success and failure; instead, all outcomes generate learning and are equally valuable. DBR aims to design solutions to complex educational problems in the real world. Design Thinking offers mindsets and methodologies that DBR researchers can adopt in order to achieve these aims.

Chapter 2 compares and contrasts the different models and frameworks for DBR that have evolved over time. The last two sections of this chapter outline the mindsets that underpin DBR and provide a decision matrix to differentiate DBR from other educational research methods.

## Mindsets for DBR

This section examines the mindsets that underpin DBR. These mindsets are realised in practice through methodologies within the DBR process. They are phrased as mindsets rather than characteristics because they are our orientations as researchers to our work in the world rather than features of the approach.

### Be Human-Centred

This is a baseline orientation for all DBR work. It situates people at the heart of the whole design and research enterprise. The term “human” is critically important here. It is not “user-centred,” a term widely used in industry, as it focuses on all the individuals involved, not just direct “users.” For educational researchers, it is not learner-centred or child-centred or teacher-centred; rather, it is human-centred. This requires us, as researchers, to focus on all of the people in the research context in a holistic way, acknowledging and exploring the many roles these people may inhabit in their own lives (parents, teachers, learners, friends, peers, mentors) and their human dimensions beyond mere demographics. As educational researchers, this mindset draws us away from a narrow focus on specific educational roles and demographic characteristics in order to situate each person firmly at the centre of the multiple systems that interact in their lives.

### Be Holistic

This mindset follows on from being human-centred. If we are to be genuinely human-centred, this entails that we consider the whole person when it comes to our research partners, not just their particular role or actions within our specific research context. Furthermore, we must consider the research context itself, holistically, as intersecting systems of connected and interacting individuals. Complexity is a given, and context matters. For example, a classroom context is its own complex system of individuals interacting, and it is situated within school and community systems that interact with the classroom system and so on. Indeed, Don Norman, one of the pioneers of Human-Centred Design and Design Thinking, represents this complexity using the term Humanity-Centred Design (2023). This phrase acknowledges that we exist in interconnected networks of individuals within systems with a wide range of factors that influence our behaviours and needs and that it is only by working within these full ecological systems that we can design successfully.

In real terms, it may not be possible to account for, track or measure, all variables within a given environment. However, within DBR, we intentionally take the real world with all of its complexities, ambiguities, and messiness as the research context, rather than a laboratory simplification:

> We have to accept human behaviour the way it is, not the way we would wish it to be.
> 
> (Norman, 2013, p. 7)

We can integrate the tools of Design Thinking, Systems Thinking (Arnold & Wade, 2015), and even Futures Thinking (Canina et al., 2020) to put this mindset into practice. Furthermore, as educational researchers, theory can support us in adopting and operationalising a holistic perspective. For example, bioecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006) highlights the interplay of systems inhabited by a person of interest in their development, or sociocultural theory (Vygotsky, 1978) examines the dynamic processes in the interactions of a learner with others and their environment that support learning and development. The theoretical framework can help us as researchers to maintain this focus on the holistic view of the research context and our research partners as humans who are dynamic agents within this context.

### Iterate, Iterate, Iterate

In DBR, there is no single optimal solution that is envisioned from the outset. Solutions are designed through an iterative process of exploration, prototyping, and evaluation that is ongoing. This iterative process is fluid and non-linear. At each stage, change is possible: changes to the problem definition, changes to the solution, changes to the context, changes to the underpinning framework. The iterative process is documented rigorously to articulate the rationale and the impact of the changes made along the way.

### Bias to Action

DBR always seeks to make changes in the world. It is interventionist in nature. The focus on multiple iterations pushes us as researchers to make our ideas tangible through rapid prototyping and to evaluate them in the real world. In DBR, the goal of evaluation is to learn more in order to move forward and improve.

### Stay Curious

Finally, key components of DBR are curiosity and openness to what may arise. This relates to an important Design Thinking process: reframing. If we are to stay curious as researchers, it means that we must try to put aside what we think and believe will be effective in order to stay open to what might happen. Logistically, what this means is we may need to draw on many data sources and adopt different lenses or perspectives to record a rich and diverse view of what happens in the research situation to facilitate retrospective analysis, if needed. It also encourages us to stay open to serendipity and what might arise through the research that can change and inform what we do and understand.

## DBR and Other Educational Research Approaches

This chapter has set out why DBR has developed as a valuable methodological approach in education and what the key mindsets are that underpin it. For all its benefits, however, it may not always be the most appropriate approach. Research is all about making decisions. Almost always, there is not only one correct approach to address a research question. There are multiple approaches, each of which comes with its own advantages and disadvantages, its own biases and blind-spots, its own principles and processes. The researcher must decide which approach aligns best with who they are, what they aim to achieve, and what they can do within the context in which they work. The researcher must find that sweet spot between what is feasible and what is desirable while always maintaining rigour. No choice will be perfect, but many choices could be appropriate in context.
