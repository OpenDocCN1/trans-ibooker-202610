
## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Returning to the pre-service educator vignette, the intervention centred on workshops where learners collaboratively planned and reflected on mathematics lessons. The core components included active collaboration among pre-service educators; structured opportunities to connect lesson design with key mathematical concepts and curriculum goals; and cycles of practice teaching, feedback, and reflection.

These features were non-negotiable because they carried the theoretical foundation of the intervention, that is, helping novice educators learn mathematical pedagogy by doing and by critically reflecting on their practice in relation to curricular expectations.

At the same time, several adaptable components emerged:

- The format of collaboration (working in pairs, triads, or larger groups);
- The duration and frequency of workshops (single extended block vs shorter, repeated sessions);
- The types of mathematical tasks used as exemplars (problem-solving activities, manipulatives-based tasks, or real-world applications).

In one cohort, time constraints led the team to divide longer workshops into shorter, weekly sessions. In another, exemplars were drawn from measurement and algebra topics to align more closely with learners’ upcoming school placements. These adjustments responded to local needs without undermining the intervention’s theoretical backbone.

By explicitly distinguishing between core and adaptable components, the team provided clarity for both researchers and participants. This not only strengthened implementation but also generated valuable insights into how the design could be adapted for use across different teacher education contexts without losing its essential character.

In DBR, such distinctions allow interventions to travel across settings while remaining anchored to their theoretical rationale. They also foster trust among practitioners, who are empowered to make local adjustments without fear of “breaking” the design. Over time, documenting how adaptable components shift across contexts enriches both theory and practice, showing the many ways in which a core principle can be realised.

## PRACTICAL TIP

Develop a simple guide or matrix for participants outlining which parts of your intervention are core and which are flexible. This can be a powerful communication and training tool. By explicitly identifying these beforehand, you give participants guidance on where they have leeway to improvise.

## Collaboration between Researchers and Practitioners

Effective DBR hinges on genuine collaboration between researchers and the educators who enact the intervention. This partnership is not peripheral; it is the engine that drives the entire process. Without strong relational trust and shared ownership, even the most carefully designed intervention risks faltering when it meets the realities of classrooms or lecture halls.

Crucially, DBR is not research done on schools or educators but research done with them. Educators are engaged as co-designers, co-implementers, and co-learners, bringing contextual expertise that is every bit as valuable as the methodological expertise of the researcher (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012). This distinguishes DBR from related approaches such as action research: while both value practitioner voice, DBR positions collaboration at the heart of rigorous, design-driven enquiry, ensuring interventions are both theoretically grounded and practically viable. As Bakker (2018) emphasises, DBR is research conducted “with educators, for educators.” This principle becomes especially salient during implementation, when designs are stress-tested in authentic contexts.

In this sense, collaboration is not simply a desirable feature of DBR; it is a condition for success. Implementation cycles thrive on open dialogue,
mutual respect, and the recognition that both educators and researchers are contributing to knowledge-building. The human aspect of DBR, therefore, is not just about partnership in design but about co-navigating the uncertainties, adaptations, and reflections that arise when ideas are brought to life in practice.

## Co-Design and Shared Ownership

In the implementation phase, collaboration often takes the form of co-design meetings and continuous dialogue. During this phase, researchers bring ideas from theory or prior studies, while educators contribute practical wisdom and an evolving knowledge of real-world interactions around what engages students, what fits the school culture, and what constraints (time, curriculum, resources) must be considered. Ideally, the intervention is not something handed to educators; rather it is a journey of discovery designed jointly. When educators help shape the intervention, they are more invested in implementing it with fidelity and insight, which consequently fosters shared ownership of the research design and process.

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

When the first cycle of mathematics workshops began in the teacher education programme, collaboration between university lecturers and school-based mentors quickly proved essential. The lecturers brought a strong theoretical framework for designing tasks that linked mathematical concepts to curriculum standards, while the mentors contributed practical insights about what pre-service educators typically struggled with during school placement.

As the cycle unfolded, unexpected issues emerged: students were enthusiastic about creative problem-solving activities but often overlooked opportunities to make explicit curricular connections. Instead of treating this as a failure, the research team and mentors met after each session to compare notes. Mentor observations provided fine-grained details of how students approached planning, while the lecturers analysed student reflections and lesson drafts. Together, they decided to
introduce exemplar lesson segments co-developed by both university and school staff, ensuring they were both theoretically robust and grounded in classroom realities.

This iterative partnership illustrated that the success of the implementation phase did not lie in one group’s expertise alone. It was the dialogue between lecturers and mentors — the blending of theory and practice — that enabled the intervention to adapt productively and remain faithful to its underlying goals.

A hallmark of excellent collaboration in DBR is continuous, well-articulated communication. During the implementation phase, it is wise to schedule regular check-ins (weekly brief meetings or even informal chats) to discuss how the intervention is progressing. These meetings can bring to the surface minor issues before they become big problems. For example, an educator might report that students are confused by certain instructions after two sessions in contrast to waiting a longer time to voice their feelings and concerns. This communicative interaction allows the team to tweak the structure or strategy, or amend the instruction, or even provide an extra example in the next class. Ongoing communication also helps maintain alignment with the goals; it keeps everyone informed about any adaptations the teacher had to make on the fly and the rationale behind them. This feeds into the iterative process — by the time you formally conclude a cycle, much of the reflection might already be underway through these conversations. Some teams keep a shared journal or online document where educators log daily experiences with the intervention, and researchers can respond or annotate with questions. This creates a rich, shared record and reinforces a sense of joint enquiry.
