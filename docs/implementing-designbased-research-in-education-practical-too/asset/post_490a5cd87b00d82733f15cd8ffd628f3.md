## Part 2: Positionality and Participation: Navigating Ethics and Roles in DBR

### Positionality: Considerations for Ethical Research

Drawing upon our opening vignette, prior to the implementation of methods and the acquisition of data, there is a researcher, an individual shaped by lived experiences, cultural background, and personal identity. Just as communication facilitates connections between individuals, positionality reminds us that we never approach research as entirely neutral. Our perspectives, assumptions, and predispositions profoundly influence the formulation of research questions, the establishment of relationships, and the construction of interpretations.

In this section, we shift from the external practices of collaboration to the internal practices of reflexivity and ethical considerations. We explore how the identities of researchers, the voices of participants, and the dynamics of trust and power collectively contribute to the development of DBR. By examining positionality and participation, we acknowledge that research encompasses not only the generation of knowledge but also the intricate relationship between knowledge, individuals, and each other.

### Positionality and Identity

Positionality refers to how an individual’s worldview, identity, and experiences shape their perspectives and practices. It is not only about recognising who you are but also about acknowledging how self-understanding influences the way you design, conduct, and interpret research (Lin, 2015; Svyantek, 2021). For example, if asked, “Catherine, what is your positionality?” I might respond:

I am a white Irish woman, a mother, a sister, a researcher in higher education, an occasional artist, and much more. My personal identity and the theories I draw on are integral to my understanding of how people learn and develop intellectually and emotionally. I am particularly interested in how children's make-believe stories and lived experiences are articulated in research.

This description of my identity aims to illustrate how values and beliefs about education inevitably inform your research interests, decisions, and interactions. Recognising one's positionality is, therefore, essential: it enables researchers to be transparent about how their values and experiences shape the research process. A practical way to begin this work is through structured self-reflection, which helps uncover how different aspects of identity influence both the questions we ask and the relationships we form in our research.

### EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Early in my research career, I reflected on questions such as:

- Could being a white Irish woman affect how I conduct research in a country outside Ireland?
- Could my storytelling background endear me to participants with similar interests?

At first, I reassured myself with quick answers and moved on but later realised that such surface-level reflection did little to uncover how my identity shaped my research.

I recognised a lack of critical thinking in my own practice, an uncomfortable but important discovery. This prompted me to seek out a more structured way to interrogate my assumptions, which I found in the critical thinking approach developed by Elder and Paul (2019). Their model helped me move beyond reassurance to genuine reflection, enabling me to articulate my positionality with greater honesty and depth.

### A Critical Thinking Strategy

Paul and Elder’s approach provides a systematic way to examine our thinking by focusing on seven elements of reasoning (Elder and Paul, 2019; Paul, Niewoehner, and Elder, 2019). The elements of reasoning can be used with reflective prompts to make reflection more concrete and help researchers dig beneath first impressions.

- **PAUSE AND REFLECT**
- 1. **Purpose:** What am I trying to achieve here? Why does this issue matter?
- 2. **Question:** What exactly am I asking myself, and is it the right question?
- 3. **Information:** What do I know, and what do I still need to find out?
- 4. **Concepts:** What key ideas or theories shape the way I’m looking at this?
- 5. **Assumptions:** What am I taking for granted? Are those assumptions fair?
- 6. **Inferences:** Do my conclusions actually follow from the evidence I have?
- 7. **Point of View:** Whose perspective am I using? How might others see this differently?

When considering your own positionality, you can draw on a critical thinking framework to examine your thinking. Imagine a university student researching critical thinking in early years classrooms. Using Paul and Elder’s prompts, their reflection might look like this:

- **PRACTICAL TIPS**
- **Applying the Seven Elements to Positionality**

**Purpose:** I want to show that even very young children are capable of critical thinking.
**Question:** Am I asking “How do children question and reason?” or “How can teachers encourage questioning?” My framing reveals whether I focus on the child’s or the teacher’s role.

**Information:** My background in exploring teaching practices makes me value argument and evidence, but I may overlook other forms of reasoning (e.g., play, gesture, or storytelling).
**Concepts:** I lean on philosophical models of inquiry, which shape how I define “critical thinking.”
**Assumptions:** I assume that developing critical thinking is universally beneficial, but families or educators may value harmony over debate.
**Inferences:** If children disagree with each other, I might interpret it as evidence of reasoning, but it could also simply reflect social dynamics.
**Point of View:** I tend to see from the teacher’s perspective; I need to bring children’s voices more centrally into the analysis.

This exercise shows how structured reflection may move your thinking beyond surface awareness towards a deeper understanding of how positionality shapes the study.

Note: The usefulness of critical thinking tools will vary depending on your goals and preferences. Early career researchers are encouraged to adapt these strategies to their own needs. Doing so not only strengthens academic practice but also supports the development of a reflective, critically aware professional identity.

### Positionality and Research

Every research project is shaped not only by questions, methods, and analysis but also by the researcher’s positionality. It influences design, collaboration, and interpretation, as well as how we navigate challenges and disclosures (Secules et al., 2021). Awareness of positionality highlights the need to avoid assuming stability in our own or others’ perspectives (Holmes, 2020). In essence, it means recognising that we do not possess all the answers and that some of our answers may be incomplete or biased.

Brady (2021) extends this conversation by arguing that educators and professionals must first understand their dispositions in order to hold themselves accountable.
