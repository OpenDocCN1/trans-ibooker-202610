## Understanding People and Their Roles in DBR

DBR seeks to improve educational practices that foster meaningful teaching and learning. To achieve this, researchers collaborate with educators and students to find solutions to real classroom challenges. The success of a DBR study, its design, implementation, and redesign depend on the active engagement of all team members, including both researchers and participants. This section explores the composition of the research team, their roles in the DBR process, and their interconnectedness within the wider educational community.

### Who Is the Research Team and What Are Their Roles?

DBR brings individuals together to work as a research team, most often within authentic settings such as classrooms. Team members may take on multiple roles, including facilitating dialogue about educational challenges, conducting pilot studies, analysing findings, and sharing insights with others.

Assigning roles that reflect participants’ skills and expertise is crucial. This not only supports the effective functioning of the team but also fosters competence, recognition, and motivation. Roles and responsibilities should be aligned with each person’s capabilities, authority, and availability, while also respecting individuals’ existing commitments.

Therefore, planning a DBR study begins with an honest consideration of participants’ limitations such as time constraints, scope to manipulate variables, strengths, and enthusiasm for involvement. Paying close attention to these factors helps the project move forward smoothly, with minimal disruption to classroom routines.

### Allocating DBR Roles

Understanding the interests of partners such as educators and learners helps to determine how participants are represented and engaged in the research design. Motivation plays a pivotal role in DBR, compelling team members to move towards shared objectives: this information would have been outlined in your research proposal and participant information forms. Participants’ motivations may stem from a commitment to educational reform, addressing learners’ needs, professional development, or simply the desire to engage in something new and meaningful.

In a DBR study, roles can remain consistent or change over time. Shifts often occur naturally as relationships develop and individual strengths become clearer, allowing responsibilities to be shared or delegated to the most suitable participants. At the same time, if someone is confident and comfortable in their role, the researcher should avoid disrupting it during an implementation phase unless there is a strong reason to do so. Across different stages of the study, roles may still evolve, offering both flexibility and stability for the team. Approaching roles in this way supports ethical and respectful collaboration while also protecting the dignity and autonomy of participants, researchers, and wider partners. ### Understanding Boundaries

In DBR, individuals establish boundaries that shape interactions among researchers, participants, and partners. These boundaries can be ethical, cultural, temporal, collaborative, or emotional. Respecting them is essential for both ethical practice and effective teamwork.

- **Ethical boundaries** involve providing clear and accurate information, ensuring informed consent, protecting confidentiality, and addressing power dynamics, particularly in school settings. Ethical practice goes beyond signed consent forms; it requires an ongoing commitment to safeguarding participants’ rights.
- **Cultural and contextual boundaries** reflect the values, norms, and practices of the setting. Researchers may need to adapt methods to local needs while still upholding research integrity.
- **Temporal boundaries** concern participants’ time commitments and classroom responsibilities. Tasks must be feasible, and researchers should maintain engagement throughout iterative DBR cycles by keeping participants informed and valued.
- **Collaboration boundaries** support shared decision-making. Clear agreements on roles and responsibilities, ideally established in an initial meeting, help avoid mismatches between tasks and skills. For example, in a study on children's word recognition, one educator might prefer reading a story aloud while another leads oral narration; assigning roles based on what is most likely to meet the aims of the lesson or intervention empowers the team and strengthens trust.
- **Emotional boundaries** acknowledge the effort and energy participants invest. Respecting these boundaries sustains ethical, professional relationships, which often evolve as skills and confidence develop across the lifespan of a research project.

### Focus on Phases

The phases of a DBR project often mark shifts in influence and key relationships, with different team members taking on more active roles. While leadership may change across phases, the researcher’s steady presence is important as it provides direction and ensures quality.

- **Phase 1: Problem Identification and Analysis:** This phase usually involves collaborative discussion of the research problem and potential solutions. Depending on the design, it may be student-led (e.g., empowering student voice) or researcher-led (e.g., through a literature review).
- **Phase 2: Design and Development:** Often led by the researcher, but educators may assume a prominent role, depending on the study’s objectives and design.
- **Phase 3: Implementation:** Typically educator-led, since it involves piloting the study in practice. The researcher supports this phase by assisting and facilitating.
- **Phase 4: Evaluation and Reflection:** A collaborative phase where the team analyses findings in relation to the literature, existing knowledge, and the project’s aims, enabling collective decision-making.
