+   - **Collaboration boundaries** support shared decision-making. Clear agreements on roles and responsibilities, ideally established in an initial meeting, help avoid mismatches between tasks and skills. For example, in a study on children's word recognition, one educator might prefer reading a story aloud while another leads oral narration; assigning roles based on what is most likely to meet the aims of the lesson or intervention empowers the team and strengthens trust.
+   - **Emotional boundaries** acknowledge the effort and energy participants invest. Respecting these boundaries sustains ethical, professional relationships, which often evolve as skills and confidence develop across the lifespan of a research project.

# Focus on Phases

The phases of a DBR project often mark shifts in influence and key relationships, with different team members taking on more active roles. While leadership may change across phases, the researcher’s steady presence is important as it provides direction and ensures quality.

+   - **Phase 1: Problem Identification and Analysis:** This phase usually involves collaborative discussion of the research problem and potential solutions. Depending on the design, it may be student-led (e.g., empowering student voice) or researcher-led (e.g., through a literature review).
+   - **Phase 2: Design and Development:** Often led by the researcher, but educators may assume a prominent role, depending on the study’s objectives and design.
+   - **Phase 3: Implementation:** Typically educator-led, since it involves piloting the study in practice. The researcher supports this phase by assisting and facilitating.
+   - **Phase 4: Evaluation and Reflection:** A collaborative phase where the team analyses findings in relation to the literature, existing knowledge, and the project’s aims, enabling collective decision-making.
