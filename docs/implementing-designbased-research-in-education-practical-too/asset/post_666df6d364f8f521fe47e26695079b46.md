From a practical point of view, self-documentation offers advantages over observation: the research context is where your research partners spend time in their normal lives, and so they can generate data over a longer time period than it might be feasible for you to observe in person. Furthermore, they will be involved in the normal activities of the setting and can document those from the inside, without any observer effects. However, self-documentation is hard work for your research partners. They would need to be very committed to the project goals to sustain this workload over an extended period. For this reason, it is very important to develop and pilot your self-documentation methods with your research partners so that it is as easy as possible for them to integrate into their normal processes or workflows. For example, teachers probably do not have time to type out observations on their computer during the day, but maybe they could jot down a few lines in a small notebook that fits in their pocket or bag. Or students might not want to write about what is important to them in their school, but they don’t mind snapping some pictures of their favourite places in the school and sharing those with you.

## PRACTICAL TIPS: DOS AND DON'TS

Dos
- 1. Think through the ethics and consent issues associated with self-documentation, in particular if you are working with children and young people.
- 2. Work with your research partners on what is the focus and mechanism for self-documentation.
- 3. Based on this collaboration, provide very clear instructions.
- 4. Have modest expectations – self-documentation is hard work, and people may not have the time or energy for it.

Don’ts
- 1. Don’t forget about data protection constraints, in particular for an image or video.
- 2. Don’t ask for too much.
- 3. Don’t forget to run a pilot to make sure what you are asking people to do is clear, feasible, and useful.

## Experiential Research

This approach draws on Design Thinking rather than educational research approaches. In Design Thinking, this is termed Immersive Empathy. The goal here is to immerse yourself in the research context and undertake the activities and processes in that context yourself in order to understand and empathise with your research partners more fully. Shadowing a student is a very good example of this. If your research context is a school, take a typical student timetable and follow it for a day. This can allow you to experience the mental, physical, sensory, and social demands of a school day: what it is like moving from class to class mentally and physically, how much time you have to interact with others during the day, where and when you can eat or rest, where or when you get any time to move or stand or walk. Without living that experience, it can be difficult to understand or empathise with the young learners in the setting. It is important to try to immerse yourself as much as possible in the environment: take your time, wear the appropriate clothes, bring the appropriate equipment, get involved, insofar as is possible, in the activities. It might be necessary to simulate some aspects of the experience to make it as complete as possible. For example, what is the school day experience like if you are visually impaired or hungry or have a mobility issue or don’t speak the language of instruction or have trouble taking things down from the board? You can take steps to simulate these experiences as best you can. Of course, there are limitations to how realistic it is to immerse yourself fully in the experience, but even taking that step to be fully out and embedded in the real-world research context is valuable and can generate insights and promote empathy.

## PRACTICAL TIPS: DOS AND DON'TS

Dos
- 1. Consider your personal biases and how to mitigate the effects of these.
- 2. Plan for the immersive activity carefully. You will probably need to observe the setting in order to determine where and when you should join and what you will need.
- 3. Immerse yourself in the moment. This is like taking on a participant-as-observer role except that the core focus here is participation in the moment to experience and feel first-hand what it is like to be in that environment.
- 4. Record your observations as soon as possible after the experience.

Don'ts
- 1. Don’t analyse — try to remain immersed in the moment, analyse later.
- 2. Don’t assume your experience is the “typical” experience, as yours is only simulated and time limited.

## KEY TAKEAWAY

Remember that what people say is not always the same as what they do, think, or feel. Use a range of methods to try to explore the full empathy map of Say-Think-Feel-Do.

## Analysis and Synthesis

The goal of this phase in DBR is NOT about finding solutions; it is still all about finding the best questions to address, for now, in your research context. All of the methods we have discussed will generate lots of data about your research context from a range of perspectives. The purpose of this step of the need-finding phase is to distil all the insights down in order to identify a good problem to solve in the design phase. This stage is all about synthesising the learning from the empathy work you have completed. In educational research terms, this is an analysis phase drawing on the relevant analytic tools, as appropriate (thematic analysis, conversational analysis, content analysis, visual analysis, etc.). In Design Thinking terms, this is called synthesising insights for action. There are many guides to data analysis for different data types that can allow you to identify the needs of your research partners in their particular context. You are looking for consistency and contradictions in your data in order to deepen your understanding of the research context. Challenges, issues, barriers, workarounds, or pain points

that you identify in the data can constitute a problem to solve or a need that is currently not met. You should discuss these with your research partners to validate your assumptions and conclusions, as they are the experts in the research context. Your analysis will almost certainly generate many candidates for the best problem to solve. All of these possible problems or needs should be recorded for the final step in this phase: identifying one among the many that you have found to address in the next phase.

For this final step, the candidate problems should be categorised, clustered, consolidated, and prioritised, ideally with your research partners and your research team (even if that is only you and your supervisor). This can be done face-to-face or online in a workshop format with post-its or vignettes or photos or other representations of the identified issues that you can work through with your partners. The initial categorisation, clustering, and consolidation task will reduce the problem space. However, in order to prioritise and select, you will need to apply constraints and judgement. Some constraints will relate to feasibility, others to impact, and yet others to desirability. For this step, you and your research partners need to specify your constraints (budget, time, control, possible impact, etc.) and prioritise and select the most appropriate problem to solve based on that, as in the example below. It is very important at this stage to select something that you and your research partners can get behind and are motivated to solve. Without this motivation, your DBR project could lose momentum and fail to progress.
