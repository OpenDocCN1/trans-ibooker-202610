+   - Pilot your questionnaire: as many times as you need to be sure that people are answering what you think you are asking them. Think-aloud protocols are very good for this, as they can highlight poor phrasing, ambiguity, or misunderstandings in questionnaire items. They will also give you a good indication of how long the questionnaire really takes to complete.
+   - Ask open questions as needed. The more open questions you have, the richer the data from the questionnaire.
+   - Consider the language and literacy needs of your research partners: use plain English guidelines in drafting your questions; if necessary, get the questionnaire translated into the main language(s) of your research partners; highlight read-aloud options for online questionnaires.
+   - Make it as easy as you can for people to respond. Offer the questionnaire in multiple formats (paper, online, phone); offer a wide timeframe to respond; make the questionnaire as streamlined and fast as you can.
+   - Offer participants the chance to follow up if appropriate.

# Don'ts

+   - Don't ask leading questions.
+   - Don't forget to include important demographic questions if needed (age, gender, etc.).
+   - Don't make the questionnaire too long because people will not finish it. Use your pilot to check how long the questionnaire takes. Every second you have your participants' attention is valuable, so make the best use of it: don't ask questions you don't need; don't ask too many open questions.

# Interviews (Individual, Group, and Focus Group)

Another staple of social science research, interviews are a very useful way to generate data about a research context. This method is about engaging in communication with people directly to learn more about the research context. The most common model for an interview is a spoken engagement, usually face-to-face. However, there are many more options available. It can be face-to-face or over the phone or online. The communication could use a range of media.
