# SCALING UP DESIGN-BASED RESEARCH INTERVENTIONS

# EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT: SETTING THE SCENE

At St Brigid’s Primary School, educators and researchers worked side by side to develop *The Literacy Links Project*, a co-designed early literacy intervention rooted in phonemic awareness and explicit decoding instruction. Following strong outcomes over two years, the research team faced a new question: how could they bring this success to other schools without losing what made it work?

## Introduction

The question of how to scale educational innovations lies at the heart of many challenges in educational research and practice. In Design-Based Research (DBR), where interventions are co-developed and refined within real-world settings, scaling is not a matter of replication but of responsive adaptation. Unlike experimental research paradigms that prioritise standardisation and control, DBR acknowledges the inherent variability of educational contexts and seeks to generate usable knowledge that is both rigorous and relevant (Design-Based Research Collective, 2003; McKenney & Reeves, 2019).

As DBR projects mature, researchers often aim to extend their reach beyond the original setting and turn their attention to how to scale up their intervention. However, this scaling process introduces new complexities. Transferring an intervention from one school or system to another often involves navigating contextual differences such as policy, practice, culture, and capacity (Penuel & Gallagher, 2017). These differences require that interventions be flexible enough to adapt while preserving the integrity of their core principles. Therefore, scaling becomes a continuation of the design process.

Recent research emphasises the need for iterative and situated approaches to scaling within DBR. Fishman et al. (2013) argue for “principled adaptation,” a process that maintains fidelity to the intervention’s theoretical underpinnings while allowing flexibility in form. This approach recognises that rigid replication can be counterproductive in context-specific settings. Penuel et al. (2017) highlight the value of research-practice partnerships that rely on sustained collaboration with stakeholders who are recognised as co-designers of the intervention, not just end-users. They emphasise the importance of aligning innovations with the priorities, capacities, and lived experiences of the people who will implement them, thus making scaling a participatory and context-responsive process.

This chapter explores the practicalities and theoretical considerations of scaling up DBR projects. It draws on real-world examples, offers actionable strategies, and poses reflective questions to support both researchers and educators in navigating the nuances of scale. It attempts to reframe scaling, not as a technical task of implementation, but as an opportunity to deepen understanding, improve design, and expand educational impact. This chapter will also explore the following areas:

- Scaling as a continuation of the design process;
- Building a coalition of partners;
- Planning for flexibility in mind; and
- Designing for sustainability from the start.

## Scaling as a Continuation of the Design Process

### EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

St Brigid’s educators began mentoring colleagues in the new schools but quickly discovered a number of unanticipated challenges. The daily timetables in other schools were organised quite differently, and some classrooms had as many as 30 children compared with the smaller groups that had participated in the original pilot. Staffing patterns also varied, with some schools sharing special education teachers across multiple classes. Rather than attempting to replicate the exact routines from the pilot site, the team treated these differences as opportunities for new learning. Using feedback logs and short reflective meetings, educators experimented with alternative grouping structures, such as rotating literacy stations and shared intervention blocks. These adaptations allowed each school to preserve the core features of the intervention while tailoring delivery to their own local realities. In this way, scaling became a process of redesign rather than simple replication.

In DBR, scaling should not be seen as the final step in a linear sequence but rather as an extension of the design process itself. Rather than seeking to “finish” a design once it proves effective in one setting, DBR emphasises the importance of continued enquiry, reflection, and refinement as the intervention moves into new contexts. Viewing scale-up as an extension of the design process reframes it as a dynamic, iterative endeavour, prompting researchers and teachers to treat each new implementation site as a living laboratory for continued learning and refinement. As interventions enter new schools, they interact with local structures, norms, and challenges. These encounters reveal both expected and unforeseen effects, offering new opportunities for learning and improvement (Barab & Squire, 2004).

DBR encourages researchers to remain open to evolution while recognising that even successful interventions will likely require adjustment in response to different contexts. It also highlights the need for feedback loops at every stage of the scale-up, where insights from each implementation cycle contribute to the ongoing development of the design (Collins et al., 2004). Importantly, seeing scaling as a continuous aspect of the design process also empowers teachers. Rather than simply delivering a packaged programme or intervention, teachers are invited to engage in iterative, context-sensitive decision-making. This cultivates professional agency and promotes a deeper understanding of the intervention’s purpose and potential. Furthermore, by maintaining a design mindset during the scale-up, research teams are better positioned to capture the complexity and variability of educational environments, which leads to more nuanced findings and strengthens the external validity of the intervention.

## Understanding That Scaling Is Not Copy-Pasting

Every educational context is different. What works in one setting may not automatically translate to another. In DBR, scaling should not be viewed as a linear process (McKenney & Reeves, 2019) where an effective intervention can simply be replicated across contexts with the same expected outcomes, as educational settings vary enormously in terms of learner demographics, teacher expertise, cultural values, technological access, curriculum structures, and institutional support. Assuming a one-size-fits-all approach can lead to misalignment between the design and the realities of practice in new environments.

The process of scaling in DBR must, therefore, be reframed as a continuous part of the design process. Each new implementation should be treated as a fresh iteration that acknowledges and embraces contextual differences. This approach aligns with the principle of “re-contextualisation,” where interventions are adapted to fit new environments without compromising their core design principles (Dede et al., 2005). Researchers and educators must begin by deeply understanding the characteristics of the new site: what are the learning goals? What resources are available? What constraints might affect implementation? Tools such as needs-analysis assessments, ethnographic observations, and semi-structured interviews can provide valuable insights into the specific affordances and limitations of the new setting.

Cultural responsiveness also plays a critical role in scaling. A digital storytelling programme that worked well in an urban school with high English proficiency may need substantial modification for use in a rural, multilingual context. In such cases, not only content but also pedagogical strategies must be adjusted to reflect the students’ linguistic and cultural backgrounds (Gay, 2010). Another key consideration is system readiness as implementation success hinges on whether a school is equipped, structurally, politically, and professionally, to adopt the intervention. Coburn (2003) emphasises that, for reforms to scale meaningfully, they must be adopted deeply and broadly across multiple levels of the system, including educators’ instructional practices and whole-school policies.
