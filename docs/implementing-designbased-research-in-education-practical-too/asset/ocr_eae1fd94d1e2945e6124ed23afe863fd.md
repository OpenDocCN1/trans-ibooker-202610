Meaningful engagement extends beyond school leaders and classroom educators to include support staff, parents and, where appropriate, learners themselves. Each of these groups brings distinct perspectives that can inform how an intervention is understood, implemented, and sustained. For example, educators and support staff can highlight practical constraints and opportunities within daily routines, while parents can offer insights into how family contexts, language, or cultural practices may influence the success of the intervention. Involving learners helps researchers understand motivation, relevance, and accessibility from the learner’s perspective.

Building meaningful buy-in begins with trust, and one of the most effective ways to build trust is through evidence. Sharing data from the pilot phase, especially qualitative evidence such as educator reflections, learner work samples, or classroom observations, can illustrate the intervention’s value to partners. Other formats such as visual graphics, short case studies, and informal learning conversations often resonate better than formal reports. Overall, these tools help partners to understand not only what was done, but why it mattered.

Equally important is identifying and empowering local champions. These are often respected educators who are open to innovation and can bridge the research team’s work with the realities of school life. By modelling the intervention, sharing honest accounts of its implementation, and celebrating small wins, they help introduce new practices, sometimes even schoolwide. Their credibility stems from lived experience, and their support can significantly shape a school’s perception of an initiative or intervention. Coburn (2005) reminds us that scale is not only about reach but also about depth, sustainability, and ownership. Champions are central to cultivating these dimensions. Sustained communication supports this work, and project teams should establish regular channels for sharing updates and inviting feedback, whether through staff meetings, newsletter updates, or online communities. When educators feel that their contributions are recognised and their concerns addressed, they are more likely to see themselves as co-constructors rather than passive recipients.
