
DBR is structured as an explicitly collaborative process throughout. Our research partners are crucial at all stages of the research and very definitely at this design stage. Your research partners bring their expertise from the research context with their own diverse perspectives. These perspectives are hugely valuable in generating and refining ideas to address the problems you identified at the previous stage. This design process is often called co-creation: the process of engaging “stakeholders” in designing solutions intended for their use. The processes outlined below for each stage allow us as DBR researchers to engage authentically in co-creation, not just tokenistic consultation. Our research partners are our design team, and we can prepare the ideation and design activities to leverage our collective energy and creativity for better solutions.

## PAUSE AND REFLECT

Before you begin your design activities, consider these questions:
Who needs to be in your design team for this phase, and what support or training do they need to engage fully in the design process?
What kind of physical or virtual spaces do you have available, and which of these would be best suited to a creative, open, and playful design process?
How can you structure and facilitate the design process activities to maximise your design team’s energy, playfulness, and creativity? This can range from very practical questions about how much tea and coffee you will need to what would be the best ice-breaker for this group.

## KEY TAKEAWAY

Creativity doesn’t just happen. You need to plan the appropriate conditions for it.

## Steps in the Design Process

This stage of the design process is where we need to get creative and imagine and build a possible solution to the problem we identified through

need-finding. This process moves through cycles of divergent and convergent thinking to design and then prototype creative solutions to complex problems.

This progresses in three stages:

- 1. Generating ideas (divergent thinking);
- 2. Refining ideas (convergent thinking);
- 3. Making ideas tangible to learn more (prototyping).

Move through these stages with openness, curiosity, and even playfulness and be ready to accept that the first solution will not be the final one and you are on the right track.

## Step 1: Generating Ideas

The first stage in the DBR design phase is ideation – the generation of a wide range of ideas. The goal is to generate as many ideas as we can, to diverge and go wide. At this stage quantity matters. We do not evaluate or judge for quality or appropriateness or scale or anything. We just go wide with our thinking. All ideas, no matter how crazy or obvious or difficult or silly, are welcome. The more ideas we can generate, the more we might unlock new possibilities.

The base process here is brainstorming, but this can be organised in different ways to reflect our understanding of effective ideation in the specific context of our DBR project with our research partners. There are many parameters that we can manipulate during this process: individual vs collective activity, online vs offline; asynchronous vs synchronous, duration and sequencing; tools; framing and how to stretch beyond habitual thinking. Regardless of the format, some foundational principles help ensure the process is productive. IDEO, leaders in Design Thinking, outline seven key rules for effective brainstorming (IDEO, 2025):

- PRACTICAL TIPS
- 1. Defer judgement — avoid evaluating ideas too early.
- 2. Encourage wild ideas — the more imaginative, the better.
- 3. Build on the ideas of others – use “yes, and…” rather than “but.”
- 4. Stay focused on the topic – keep the energy directed.
- 5. One conversation at a time – ensure every voice is heard.
- 6. Go for quantity – set ambitious goals and exceed them.
- 7. Use visuals or other media – drawing or modelling can unlock new thinking.

In this section, we set out concrete strategies and techniques to construct ideation processes that leverage these seven rules and generate an abundance of ideas that will take us beyond the mundane and the predictable to solve the complex problem we are looking to solve.

## Articulate the Focus

The most important aspect for ideation is to articulate the focus very clearly to keep people on track. “How Might We” (HMW) questions can provide a targeted focus while also encouraging an open mindset. Framing the question in this way also suggests multiple possible solutions as opposed to a single one. In the example above about mathematics engagement, we could start from a HMW question like:

### How Might We Help Learners Feel That Calculus Is Relevant to Their Real Lives?

This question is focused on the problem we identified through our need finding work: that students are not engaged because they cannot see the relevance or usefulness of mathematics. The HMW question does not refer to any possible solution, rather it is focused on a clear outcome and is phrased positively. HMW questions can take practice to formulate in this way. The table below demonstrates how it is useful to draft multiple HMW questions in order to arrive at one which is focused, open, broad, and positively framed for the ideation work.

| HMW Question | Is It Good? | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| How might we get learners more engaged in maths? | No | It is too broad and doesn't reflect the insight from the need-finding phase |
| How might we use learners' pastimes to help them see the relevance of calculus? | No | It includes a solution (use learners' pastimes) and so limits the solution space |
| How might we keep learners on task in calculus class? | No | It focuses on a symptom, not a cause of disengagement. It does not focus on the main desired outcome. |
| How might we help learners feel less disconnected from calculus? | No | It is phrased negatively |
| How might we help learners feel that calculus is relevant to their real lives? | Yes | It focuses on the problem we identified and our desired outcome; it is broad, it is phrased positively and does not include a solution |

Once we have a clear focus for the ideation activity, we can set about structuring the idea generation process to best suit our configuration of research partners.

## Working Alone and in Teams

Ideation is probably most effective as a blend of individual and collective activity. This stage should provide space for individual input and creative energy, but it also thrives on diversity: bringing together people with different backgrounds, experiences, and perspectives to help us break out of habitual thinking and explore new directions. Individual activity can be structured within an overall collective workshop, as in the 6-3-5 brainwriting method below, or it can be managed asynchronously with individuals generating ideas independently and recording this before coming together at a later stage as a collective. Where there are power dynamics that may require careful unpacking in the collective (e.g., worker-manager; student-teacher; different communities), this can even be managed anonymously.

For collective activity, it is particularly important to be clear on rule 1, to defer judgement. The role of the researcher-facilitator at this stage is to create a risk-free space free of judgement, where all can participate evenly and where ideas are built on constructively and creatively. Above we discussed the importance of the team composition and leaving traditional roles at the door before starting an ideation session. In an ideation session, certain activity structures can allow this space for each individual’s voice to be heard: for example, think-pair-share: and 6-3-5 brainwriting. Collective activity also needs structure or framing to ensure that the group builds on ideas constructively and without judgement. We can use prompts for building on ideas, which suspend judgement and frame the development of ideas positively. For example, using the prompt “Yes and” is much more open and productive for developing ideas than “No but.”

The researcher-facilitator should consider carefully the research partners that need to come together to generate possible solutions, their needs, their existing relationships, their perspectives, and their goals. There are many factors that can impact participation: age, disability, gender norms, race, role, and many more. Some research partners might need particular support or training in order to participate fully. It is the responsibility of the researcher-facilitator to ensure appropriate and equitable participation in this design phase across research partners. The ideation activities in this phase must be scheduled, structured, and implemented to achieve this objective.

## Working with Flexible Materials

The end goal of this ideation stage is to have lots of ideas to input into the convergent phase. It is important to consider how the participants will represent and gather and showcase their ideas. One of the most valuable tools for a face-to-face setting is using the simple post-it. Follow the rule to allow a single idea per post-it, and they become a flexible mechanism for the divergent and convergent stages of design. They allow the participants to represent their ideas as text or visually or both. They can be collected and showcased on vertical or horizontal spaces, and, most importantly, they can be regrouped, categorised, prioritised, and manipulated easily when you move to the convergent stage of the design process.
