# Be Curious

As with empathy, this mindset is central to this phase of the research and flows through into the methodologies. Leading from curiosity, not judgement, allows us to achieve all of the goals set out at the start of the chapter: it allows us to build relationships of trust, as it demonstrates a clear interest in what partners have to say, and it allows us to engage in the exploration necessary to develop a deep understanding of the research context and the lived experiences of the people in it.

The dictionary definition of curiosity is “an eager wish to know or learn about something” (2025). More precisely, Wagstaff et al. (2021, p. 363) define it as “the desire for knowledge and sensory experiences that sparks exploratory behaviour.” This orientation to investigation and exploration just for the sake of learning is crucial to a curious mindset in DBR.

As with empathy, this is a mindset that drives action. At its simplest, it is just to add the phrase “I’m curious” as a preface to questions. It clearly states to your research partner that you just want to learn; you are not looking to judge or to criticise or to evaluate; you just are really curious to find things out about their world, their experiences, and their needs.

# Be Aware of Your Own Lens

All of us have our own lenses and biases which influence how we see the world. No one is free of this, not the Dalai Lama or the smallest child. We are all influenced by the accumulation of all our past experiences. This mindset acknowledges this and seeks to address its impact on our research activity. This is about bias, which can be negative or positive but is never neutral. Both empathy and curiosity are tools to tackle our biases, helping us to reach out to our research partners without judgement and with an openness to learning about their worlds.

It requires conscious effort to surface and address biases. If you are working alone, you can do this by making those biases explicit to yourself by answering questions like “what are my life experiences in this context, with these people, in this job, etc.?”. It is also something that can be done with others, where you can invite your research partners and fellow researchers to act as your bias checkers, to call you up when you might need to remember to acknowledge and try to put aside your own biases. And you can offer to be that same gentle bias checker for them.
