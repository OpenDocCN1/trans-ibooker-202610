## PHASE 1 ANALYSIS AND EVALUATION

## FINDING THE RIGHT PROBLEM TO SOLVE

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Sam and Freddy are researchers in AI. They have built a really great chatbot tool that generates self-reflective prompts and responses for university students on placement because the research literature says that students need to reflect on their practice on placement in a dialogic way in order to develop and improve their practice. They want to test the effectiveness of this tool with a group of student primary teachers out on their first placement. So, they design a study to get student teachers to use their chatbot during the day for just ten minutes to reflect on their teaching and to measure the students’ self-efficacy before and after the study and run a few focus groups. The university lecturers are interested in trying this tool, so they allow Sam and Freddy to run their intervention. However, when the research team get out into the field, they find that most of the student teachers don’t use the tool at all. During the day, they have no time to sit at a computer. Some of them try to use the chatbot in the evening, but they are focused on planning, and only a few of them give it the time. At the end of the trial, in their focus groups, the students tell them that the chatbot was useful when they got to use it, but because they weren’t being assessed on it and it wasn’t integrated with their Virtual Learning Environment (VLE) and it wasn’t on their phone, they just didn’t have the time or headspace to use it. It didn’t serve any need for them.

If Sam and Freddy had observed some of the students first, or even just talked to them about what their challenges were from the start, their chatbot design would have been radically different. In fact, maybe they might not have made a chatbot at all. Read this chapter so you won’t make the same mistake yourselves.

## Why? Finding the Right Problem to Solve

> We fail more often because we solve the wrong problem than because we get the wrong solution to the right problem.
> 
> (Ackoff, 1974, p. 8)

Ackoff often talked about the impact of doing the wrong things righter. This just makes us more wrong. Doing the right things wrong, however, at least results in learning. This phase of DBR is focused on identifying the right problem to solve for the context you are working in. The research literature and the state-of-the-art review will identify the relevant theoretical constructs, the gaps in knowledge, what has been shown to work in other contexts, and what solutions are currently available. They will not tell you anything much about the particular context you are working in, however. They will also most likely not tell you anything about the life experiences of the people in your particular context of study. They will not help you to identify what is the right problem to solve for the people in your context or what is the best research question to address in this context.
