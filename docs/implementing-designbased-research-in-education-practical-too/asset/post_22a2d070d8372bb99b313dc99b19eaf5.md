## Phases of Iteration

- **Initial Testing**: Conduct a trial run to gather feedback on usability and engagement.
- **Feedback and Analysis**: Collect data from educators and learners, analysing both qualitative and quantitative insights.
- **Revision and Redesign**: Modify the prototype based on the analysis, preparing it for further testing.

The design and construction phase bridges theoretical understanding with tangible solutions, setting the stage for real-world testing. Through collaboration, iterative cycles, and careful attention to context, researchers create prototypes that are not only innovative but also ready for practical application. The adaptability of DBR ensures that each iteration moves the project closer to a refined, effective educational intervention. The scenario below illustrates how this phase is applied in practice.

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Using insights from the analysis, the team develops a prototype intervention: an interactive simulation tool that allows learners to manipulate variables (e.g., mass, force, velocity) and instantly see outcomes. Accompanying lesson plans integrate short, educator-led explanations with structured learner enquiry tasks. Educators co-design classroom activities to ensure alignment with curriculum goals and practical feasibility. Early prototypes are tested with small pilot groups, and feedback highlights the need for clearer prompts and scaffolding questions, which are incorporated into revised designs.

## Implementation and Spread Phase

### The Implementation Phase

The implementation phase is the third and critical stage in the DBR process, where the educational intervention is actively introduced into a real-world setting. This marks the transition from design to practice and serves as a test of the intervention’s effectiveness, usability, and feasibility in authentic educational environments. Implementation is primarily concerned with trialling the intervention in its immediate context, gathering data on how it functions, and identifying areas for refinement. Activities in this phase include introducing the intervention into classrooms and collecting both qualitative and quantitative data on its impact. The goal is to determine whether the design addresses the identified problem effectively and to generate insights that will inform subsequent iterations.

### The Spread Phase

The spread phase, by contrast, extends beyond the immediate trial context. Once the intervention has been refined through multiple implementation cycles, researchers and educators consider how it can be shared, adapted, or scaled for use in other classrooms, schools, or systems. Spread focuses on questions of transferability and sustainability: what adaptations are necessary for different contexts? How can the intervention’s principles be disseminated to wider professional communities? How might the findings inform policy or practice at scale? Unlike implementation, which emphasises testing in situ, spread is about translation and scaling, ensuring that the lessons learned in one setting can inform and benefit others.

### Key Distinction

- **Implementation:** Localised trial in a specific setting, aimed at testing feasibility and collecting data for iterative refinement.
- **Spread:** Extending refined interventions to broader contexts, often involving adaptation, professional development, and dissemination of findings.

In the following section, we take a closer look at the implementation phase.

## Preparation for Implementation

Before launching the intervention, researchers must ensure that all necessary preparations are completed. This includes training educators, distributing resources, and finalising any logistics.

**PRACTICAL TIPS**

- Conduct workshops or training sessions to familiarise educators with the intervention, ensuring they feel confident and equipped to deliver it effectively.
- Ensure that all materials – whether digital tools, printed worksheets, or manipulatives – are readily available.
- Inform the school community, including parents and learners, about the purpose and structure of the intervention to foster a supportive environment.

## Data Collection during Implementation

Collecting data during the implementation phase is vital for understanding not only whether an intervention is effective but also how and why it works in practice. Unlike more traditional research designs that rely heavily on summative assessments, DBR emphasises ongoing, formative data collection that captures the complexity of learning and teaching in authentic settings (Collins et al., 2004). This evidence provides the foundation for an iterative refinement of the design and contributes to theory-building.

### Types of Data Collection

- **Observational Data**: Researchers may conduct classroom observations to note how educators and learners use the intervention, looking for signs of engagement, confusion, or challenges.
- **Learners’ Assessments**: Pre- and post-intervention assessments help determine the initial impact on learning outcomes.
- **Educator and Learner Feedback**: Interviews, focus groups, or surveys provide firsthand accounts of user experiences, offering valuable qualitative data.
- **Usage Data**: For digital interventions, usage logs can provide insights into which features are most utilised and any points where users encounter difficulties.

## Challenges Associated with Implementation

The implementation phase often reveals unanticipated challenges that require researchers to adapt quickly and make context-sensitive decisions. Because DBR is situated in authentic educational environments, it must contend with the realities of complex, dynamic, and sometimes unpredictable school systems. Importantly, researchers should not be discouraged if an intervention does not go exactly as planned. In DBR, unexpected outcomes are not failures but valuable sources of insight. The key is to investigate why challenges occurred and use this knowledge to inform redesign and deepen theoretical understanding (Barab & Squire, 2004).

- **Technical Issues**
  Technology-enhanced interventions may face disruptions such as software glitches, weak connectivity, or lack of devices. While frustrating, these problems highlight the need to design for flexibility and ensure support structures are in place.
- **Resistance to Change**
  Educators and learners may be hesitant to adopt unfamiliar practices, particularly if they increase workload or feel misaligned with established routines. Rather than viewing this resistance as a barrier, researchers can explore the underlying causes, which often reveal valuable contextual constraints or professional developmental needs (Reeves, 2006).
- **Variability in Engagement**
  Learner participation is rarely uniform: some learners engage deeply, while others disengage due to motivation, confidence, or access issues. Instead of viewing uneven engagement as a weakness of the design, researchers can analyse patterns to understand for whom and under what conditions the intervention works best (Penuel & Gallagher, 2017).

The implementation phase is a learning opportunity for both researchers and educators. By carefully observing and collecting data, researchers can identify key aspects of the intervention that need to be refined or expanded upon. This phase is crucial for ensuring that the intervention is both effective and applicable in real-world educational settings. Any data and/or insights gained can inform the subsequent phase of evaluation and reflection, where the intervention's success and areas for improvement are assessed in greater depth. Recent perspectives further emphasise the importance of designing for adaptability and embodiment, highlighting how learning emerges through enacted activity within real contexts (Poizat et al., 2024). ## Evaluation and Reflection Phase

The evaluation and reflection phase is the fourth and final phase and is fundamental to the DBR process, where researchers systematically assess the impact of the intervention and reflect on both its successes and its limitations. Unlike traditional evaluation, which often seeks a definitive judgement about effectiveness, DBR evaluation is developmental: its purpose is not only to determine if an intervention works but to explore how, why, and under what conditions it works (Collins et al., 2004). By combining multiple sources of data, this phase aims to generate a comprehensive picture of the intervention in action.
