
# PHASES OF DESIGN-BASED RESEARCH

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT: SETTING THE SCENE

At Riverbend Secondary School, a group of science educators notice that many learners are struggling to engage with enquiry-based learning in physics. Although they can recall formulas, they find it difficult to apply concepts in practical experiments. A research team partners with the school to co-design an intervention that makes physics concepts more accessible through hands-on, technology-enhanced approaches. Together, they embark on a DBR project aimed at improving learners’ problem-solving skills in real-world contexts.

## Introduction

Design-Based Research (DBR) is distinguished by its iterative and cyclical nature, where phases of enquiry and innovation build upon one another to create both practical solutions and theoretical insights. Central to this process is the careful sequencing of phases, which guides researchers from identifying authentic educational challenges through to designing, implementing, and evaluating interventions. These phases are not linear steps but interconnected, adaptive cycles that ensure responsiveness to the complexities of real-world classrooms.

This book draws on the phases articulated by McKenney and Reeves (2012), whose model – analysis/exploration, design/construction, evaluation/reflection, and implementation/spread – offers a clear and widely used framework for structuring DBR. Their work emphasises both the dual outcomes of DBR (practical improvements and theoretical contributions) and the importance of iteration across phases to refine interventions over time. By systematically moving through problem analysis and exploration, design and construction, implementation, and evaluation and reflection, researchers generate interventions that are contextually relevant, evidence-based, and continually refined.

Beginning with analysis and exploration, this chapter provides an overview of each phase, establishing a foundation for more detailed explorations that follow in subsequent chapters.

## Analysis and Exploration Phase

The first phase of DBR is problem analysis and exploration (McKenney & Reeves, 2012). This foundational stage sets the trajectory for the entire research project and ensures that the intervention or solution developed is relevant and effective within real-world educational settings. In this phase, researchers focus on identifying a significant educational problem and conducting a comprehensive needs analysis to establish the basis for the study. In this section, we will explore the following:

- Identifying a significant educational problem;
- Conducting a comprehensive needs analysis;
- Collaboration with educators and partners;
- Challenges in the analysis and exploration phase.

### Identifying a Significant Educational Problem

The process begins with pinpointing an issue that is both critical to educational practice and underexplored in existing research. A well-defined problem serves as the foundation for the entire DBR process, as it ensures that the intervention developed will address an authentic need rather than a theoretical abstraction. To achieve this, researchers must consider not only gaps identified in the academic literature but also the pressing challenges experienced by educators, learners, and partners in everyday practice. Importantly, the problem should be one that, if addressed, can meaningfully influence teaching and learning outcomes at both local and potentially broader levels. As McKenney and Reeves (2012) emphasise, grounding DBR in real-world problems in practice strengthens the relevance, feasibility, and eventual impact of the research, while simultaneously contributing to theoretical advancement.

### PRACTICAL TIPS

#### STEPS FOR EFFECTIVE PROBLEM IDENTIFICATION

- **Review Existing Literature**: Start by examining current research to identify gaps and understand how similar challenges have been approached.
- **Consult Educational Standards/Policy**: Align the identified problem with curriculum requirements or national educational standards/policy to ensure relevance.
- **Observe Classroom Practices**: Direct observation in educational settings can provide firsthand insights into the practical challenges faced by educators and learners.

### Conducting a Comprehensive Needs Analysis

A robust needs analysis is essential for understanding the scope, underlying causes, and potential impact of the identified problem. This process ensures that researchers move beyond surface-level observations to uncover the deeper systemic and contextual factors that shape the challenge. A thorough needs analysis often combines multiple sources of evidence, such as classroom observations, interviews, surveys, and an analysis of the existing assessment data, to create a nuanced picture of the educational context.

Importantly, this stage also involves examining relevant policies, curriculum requirements, and institutional priorities to ensure that the intervention aligns with broader educational goals. In addition, it is crucial to identify the training and professional learning needs of educators who will implement the intervention. Even the most well-designed innovations risk failure if educators do not feel adequately prepared, confident, or supported in their use. Assessing educators’ knowledge, skill levels, and perceived challenges ensures that the intervention is accompanied by appropriate professional development and capacity-building strategies (Penuel & Gallagher, 2017).

By systematically investigating the nature of the problem, the conditions in which it occurs, and the readiness of educators to adopt new practices, researchers can formulate precise and actionable research questions that are firmly rooted in real-world challenges. Plomp (2013) highlights that this grounding phase is critical for producing interventions that are not only theoretically informed but also practical, feasible, and contextually responsive.

Components of a Needs Analysis:

- **Data Collection:** Use a combination of quantitative and qualitative methods, such as surveys, interviews, and focus groups, to gather insights from various stakeholders.
- **Partner Input:** Engage educators, learners, partners, and even parents to capture a wide range of perspectives. This ensures that the problem is well-defined and accurately reflects the experiences of those involved.
- **Assessment of Current Practices:** Evaluate current teaching strategies, learning materials, and assessment tools to identify the strengths and weaknesses related to the problem.

### Collaboration with Educators and Partners

Collaboration is at the heart of DBR, particularly during the problem identification and analysis phase. Because DBR is situated in authentic educational contexts, researchers must engage closely with educators, school leaders, and other partners to ensure that the work is not only theoretically grounded but also practically relevant. Such collaboration ensures that the design of interventions reflects classroom realities, institutional priorities, and the lived experiences of learners and educators.

Collaboration also strengthens the research process by establishing a reciprocal relationship between theory and practice. Rather than treating educators as passive implementers of external ideas, DBR positions them as co-researchers who actively shape the design, implementation, and refinement of interventions (Design-Based Research Collective, 2003). This shared ownership enhances the ecological validity of the research and increases the likelihood of sustainable adoption. Penuel and Gallagher (2017) further emphasise that partnership-based approaches build trust and legitimacy, enabling the co-construction of solutions that respond to systemic challenges rather than isolated problems.

Benefits of Collaboration:

- **Enhanced Understanding**: Educators, learners, and partners bring insider perspectives that help researchers to refine and contextualise their understanding of the issue. This leads to more precise research questions and more feasible designs.
- **Increased Buy-In**: When partners are meaningfully involved from the outset, they are more invested in the project, which fosters stronger implementation and the long-term sustainability of outcomes.
- **Capacity-Building**: Collaboration strengthens professional learning for educators, as their participation in design processes deepens their understanding of both pedagogy and research (Barab & Squire, 2004).
- **Relevance and Impact**: Jointly created interventions are more likely to address genuine needs, making them both more effective and scalable.

### PRACTICAL TIPS

#### STRATEGIES FOR EFFECTIVE COLLABORATION

- **Form and Engage Advisory Panels**: Include educators, school leaders, and other partners in regular discussions to validate research goals and proposed methods.
- **Host Workshops**: Conduct workshops with all partners to co-define the problem and develop shared goals for the intervention.
- **School Visits**: Regular visits to educational settings provide opportunities for researchers to build relationships with educators and gain deeper insights into the context of the problem.
