# Step 3: Making It Tangible

The final step in the DBR design phase, which can lead directly into the evaluation phase, is prototyping, making tangible the concept identified in the previous step. The goal of this step is to make something tangible enough to test quickly. The purpose of prototyping is not perfection. It is not to make a final product or process or system. It is not really even to make a viable version of it. The purpose of prototyping is feedback and learning. Prototype the minimal version of your idea that will allow you to get feedback through testing. Make your design idea tangible enough to test in order to learn what works and what doesn’t and whether the idea does actually address the identified need in the real world. Build just enough to be able to explore how people interact with your ideas and how it could operate within the research context. Failures and successes are equally valuable at the early stages, as both allow you to learn more about your research context, partners, and the problem you wish to solve. As at all stages in DBR, you can move forward to evaluation or backward to exploration, need finding, or design. DBR approaches solutions to real-world complex problems in an iterative way: we need to be open to revisiting the problem definition, but we can also accept that the first solution for evaluation may not be final.

Prototyping should be quick and cheap, especially in the early DBR cycles. As you progress through DBR cycles converging on a more optimal solution, you can move from low-fidelity to high-fidelity prototypes. Low-fidelity prototypes provide a version of your idea which may be incomplete or simplified but is sufficiently real to gather feedback from your research partners and others in the research context. As a prototype is a simplified version, you will need to identify what are the key aspects that must be included from the first prototype. For example, if you are building a tool to generate automated feedback, the user interface is relevant, but probably the most important feature you need to prototype is the automated feedback part.

There are many options for low-fidelity prototypes that will be real enough to get started. A simple sketch or diagram can outline the key features of an idea for discussion. A paper prototype might represent the key features of a physical object. Storyboards can be used to prototype new processes or to outline how someone might interact with the new idea.
