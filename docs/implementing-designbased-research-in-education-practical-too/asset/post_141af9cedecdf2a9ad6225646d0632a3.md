
## PRACTICAL TIP: CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS PLANNING TEMPLATE

Before implementing an intervention in a new setting, use the following template to guide your planning:

| Area of Analysis | Key Questions | Evidence to Gather | Implications for Design |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learner Profile | What are the language, cultural, and socio-economic backgrounds of the learners? | Enrolment data, interviews | Modify content for accessibility and relevance |
| Infrastructure | What resources are available (tech, time, space, etc.)? | Site audit, educator feedback | Adjust delivery format and pacing |
| Curriculum Alignment | How does the intervention map onto existing curricula? | Curriculum documents, educator planning records | Clarify purpose and reduce redundancy |
| Educator Capacity | What training or support will be needed? | Surveys, professional development logs | Provide targeted training or mentoring |

## PAUSE AND REFLECT ...

- What assumptions are you making about the transferability of your intervention?
- How does the new context differ in terms of culture, resources, or policy?
- Are you listening to local voices in shaping how the intervention might be adapted?

## KEY TAKEAWAY

Scaling is not about replication. It is about thoughtful, informed re-contextualisation that respects both the theory behind the intervention and the unique features of each new environment.

## Building a Coalition of Partners

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

As The Literacy Links Project prepared to expand, the team recognised the need to build a coalition of partners who could share ownership of the work. An initial Scaling Forum was held at the local Education Centre, bringing together principals, literacy leads, and special education teachers from the five participating schools. St Brigid’s educators shared short classroom videos and honest reflections from the pilot, helping to build trust and spark discussion.

In small groups, participants identified both opportunities and challenges, such as limited release time and supporting children with diverse language needs. By the end of the session, the group had agreed on shared priorities such as maintaining the focus on explicit phonemic instruction, embedding professional learning, and keeping communication open between schools. A shared online space and monthly check-ins were established to sustain collaboration and ensure scaling remained a collective effort rather than an imposed initiative.

Scaling depends on buy-in from those involved, and without a coalition of committed partners, projects can struggle to take off. In DBR, partners are not simply passive recipients of an intervention; instead, they are co-designers, implementers, and long-term advocates of the work. Consequently, building a strong coalition ensures that scaling efforts are grounded in trust, shared goals, and mutual accountability (Penuel et al., 2017). Partner engagement should begin during the earliest phases of planning for scale. Meaningful engagement extends beyond school leaders and classroom educators to include support staff, parents and, where appropriate, learners themselves. Each of these groups brings distinct perspectives that can inform how an intervention is understood, implemented, and sustained. For example, educators and support staff can highlight practical constraints and opportunities within daily routines, while parents can offer insights into how family contexts, language, or cultural practices may influence the success of the intervention. Involving learners helps researchers understand motivation, relevance, and accessibility from the learner’s perspective.

Building meaningful buy-in begins with trust, and one of the most effective ways to build trust is through evidence. Sharing data from the pilot phase, especially qualitative evidence such as educator reflections, learner work samples, or classroom observations, can illustrate the intervention’s value to partners. Other formats such as visual graphics, short case studies, and informal learning conversations often resonate better than formal reports. Overall, these tools help partners to understand not only what was done, but why it mattered.

Equally important is identifying and empowering local champions. These are often respected educators who are open to innovation and can bridge the research team’s work with the realities of school life. By modelling the intervention, sharing honest accounts of its implementation, and celebrating small wins, they help introduce new practices, sometimes even schoolwide. Their credibility stems from lived experience, and their support can significantly shape a school’s perception of an initiative or intervention. Coburn (2005) reminds us that scale is not only about reach but also about depth, sustainability, and ownership. Champions are central to cultivating these dimensions. Sustained communication supports this work, and project teams should establish regular channels for sharing updates and inviting feedback, whether through staff meetings, newsletter updates, or online communities. When educators feel that their contributions are recognised and their concerns addressed, they are more likely to see themselves as co-constructors rather than passive recipients.

## PAUSE AND REFLECT ...

- How will you support those who will implement the project day-to-day?
- What strategies are in place to maintain communication and trust?

## KEY TAKEAWAY

Scaling without partner investment is likely to fail. Sustained and inclusive engagement builds the trust, capacity, and shared ownership needed to grow and sustain an intervention.

## Planning with Flexibility in Mind

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

As the Literacy Links Project moved into new schools, the research team worked with participating educators to identify what aspects of the intervention were core to its success and what could be adapted. Together, they defined a small set of non-negotiables that represented the theoretical heart of the design: explicit phoneme-grapheme instruction, data-driven grouping based on regular assessment, and the use of educator reflection logs to capture insights from daily practice. These elements were viewed as essential for maintaining the integrity of the intervention across settings.

Other features were intentionally left open to adaptation. Schools could select texts that reflected their learners’ language backgrounds and interests, adjust the frequency of small-group sessions to fit their timetables, and choose grouping formats that worked within their staffing structures. One school integrated the routines into its existing literacy stations, while another scheduled a shared “intervention hour” across grade levels to allow flexible support. By making clear distinctions between core components and adaptable ones, the team encouraged local ownership and creativity while ensuring that the intervention’s key principles remained intact.

As interventions move into wider implementation, questions often arise about what should be preserved and what can be adapted. While the core principles of interventions should remain intact, flexibility is key to successful scaling. A common misconception in scaling up educational interventions is the belief that strict fidelity to the original design ensures effectiveness. While fidelity can be important in maintaining the integrity of an intervention’s core elements, DBR emphasises responsiveness to local contexts. Therefore, in reality, rigid replication can lead to a poor fit and resistance from implementers, ultimately undermining the intervention’s success.
