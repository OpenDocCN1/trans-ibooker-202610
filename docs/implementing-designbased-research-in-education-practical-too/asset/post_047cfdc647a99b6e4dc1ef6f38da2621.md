
In group settings:
- Don't let one voice dominate.
- Don't let voices be drowned out or not heard.
- Don't be afraid of disagreements in group interviews as these can provide real insights. But do support a respectful open discussion with group rules and careful facilitation.

## Observations

Observation is perhaps the most valuable method to understand a research context deeply. It requires you to embed yourself in the context, observing and recording the activities around you first-hand. It allows you to see what people really do as opposed to what they say or think they do. It can allow you to notice aspects that the research partners may not be consciously aware of. Observations are particularly valuable as they get you, the researcher, out into the real world in all its complexities and contradictions. They are naturalistic and allow you to draw together a range of information not available through other methods.

Observations are not without their drawbacks, though. You can only observe what is there in the moment, not what went before or will come after. You can only observe what is observable – not the unobservable motivations or other internal aspects associated with the observable events. It can be difficult to conduct observations as they require sustained focus in a real-world setting where there may be a multitude of events and activities happening simultaneously. The lens or bias the observer brings to the task can also be problematic. Possibly most important is the problem of reactivity whereby the observer, by their presence alone, can influence or change the observed context. Therefore, it can be very hard to observe “natural” activity unless you take steps to fade into the background or become a part of the real-world context through participation.

Many of these drawbacks can be addressed through careful preparation, planning, and triangulation with other methods. Planning and piloting the focus and recording of observations are essential for successful observations. It is necessary to consider carefully what aspects of a setting to observe and how best to record those. Your theoretical framework can help you identify your focus and may even provide useful observation rubrics that you can adopt or adapt. Once you know what you want to focus on, you need to consider how to maintain that focus over time. Observations can occur on a continuum from fully structured ones using explicit rubrics or coding schemes to completely unstructured jottings and notes that need to be written up after the event. In between there are semi-structured observations that may use a loose rubric or protocol, which allow for additional notes to be taken during the observations. In all cases, you should record the date, time, location, and any other key details you will need for analysis, such as persons present. It is also useful to sketch a map to provide context for the observations made after the fact: for example, a classroom layout and your position in that. With appropriate consent, photos are also invaluable in recording artefacts and objects and even the people in the setting. Structured observations can draw on existing coding schemes, rubrics, or schedules from the research literature (e.g., teacher-student interaction types) or can be designed by you to specify clearly what you need to record. The schedule should be very specific with clear instruction, so that it can be used consistently across time and by different people. Unstructured observations, however, do not have any structure. They allow for free text, using descriptive adjectives, comparatives, highly concrete or visualisable words to record key aspects of the setting carefully. It is useful to consider all the senses in this: how does the setting look, sound, smell, feel, and taste? It is important to be specific and answer all the WH questions relative to the observation: when, where, what, and who. Insofar as it can be observed through facial expressions and body language, try to record the emotions of the people in focus. Semi-structured observations can use basic protocols to direct the observers’ attention but are not too prescriptive or restrictive. For example, the POEMS (People, Objects, Environment, Messages, Services) protocol is often used in human-centred design. Cohen et al. (2017) provide a useful overview of a wide range of observation frameworks and guidelines that you can consider and adopt as appropriate. For semi-structured and unstructured observations, you may need to follow up the observations in the setting with field notes written very soon after the observation moment to develop a running record of interpretation and analysis as you go. Not all observations have to be documented in the moment, of course. Video recordings offer the opportunity for observation and analysis after the fact. Photos of the research setting, with or without people, can also offer invaluable data at the analysis stage. Screen capture can be hugely valuable in recording computer-based activities such as how students set about a writing task or how teachers provide feedback through a learning management system. Remember that the observations can be narrow or broad. They can focus on very specific activities: doing homework, marking assessments, turning on the classroom equipment, classroom interactions, etc.

Researchers often start with unstructured observations in order to learn more about an environment and, from this, develop a more structured rubric that they can use for further observations. In order to be sure that you are fully exploring the research context, you will need to move around and go to different areas within the setting. You can try the “zoom-in, zoom-out” technique to change your perspective and observe at different levels or with different focus, as you progress. For example, you might zoom-in on one individual in the lunchroom, or you might zoom-out to get a bird’s eye view from above. The important thing for this need-finding phase is to generate enough observational data from the situation to be able to identify issues and needs within the research context through analysis. In this need-finding phase, you are seeking out surprises or unusual behaviour or workarounds or contradictions, as these can point to underlying needs in the research context that are not being met. When do you know you have completed enough observations? When you are beginning to recognise or predict behaviour that is repeated, you may have reached “saturation.” When you have noted some surprises, or you can explain an unusual behaviour or contradiction, you may be homing in on the best problem to solve in the research context for now.

## PRACTICAL TIPS: DOS AND DON'TS

Dos
- 1. Plan your observation schedule carefully — even if it is to be quite unstructured.
- 2. Pilot and practice your observation schedule and process — it is not easy.
- 3. Record the key information on where and when the observation is taking place.
- 4. Make note of any critical incidents, surprising or unusual behaviour or any contradictions between what people say and what they do.
- 5. Make note of objects and artefacts that may act as clues to behaviour.
- 6. Use photo, video, screen capture, and other media to capture observational data for later analysis (if you have consent for these).
- 7. Be specific and descriptive.
- 8. Write up additional field notes as soon as you can after the observation.

Don'ts
- 1. Don't interfere in what is happening unless you are clearly a participant.
- 2. Don't make assumptions about what you cannot observe (e.g., the motivation behind a certain kind of behaviour). You could follow up and ask your research partners about these unobservable aspects in interviews.
- 3. Don't assume what you see is typical of the setting unless it is repeated on multiple observations.

## Self-Documentation

Another method to explore the research context is by asking your research partners to document their own experiences through a text, photo, video, or any other suitable artefact. This can be hugely valuable as it generates data on the research context, which is exclusively from the perspective of your research partners. No matter how carefully you observe a setting, you cannot see the world through someone else's eyes. With self-documentation, you are getting your research partners' view of the world directly. With observations, you have to work hard not to bring your biases to bear on what you see. With self-documentation, there is no issue where you exclude your research partners' unique lens and focus. Self-documentation allows your research partners to share what is meaningful for them in the research context. For example, the photos you take as an external observer in a school might be very different from those that teachers or students might take, as they will focus on what they feel is important or difficult or interesting in that school. This process does require discussion, and perhaps training, so that you and your partners share a clear understanding of what kinds of things are relevant and important to document.
