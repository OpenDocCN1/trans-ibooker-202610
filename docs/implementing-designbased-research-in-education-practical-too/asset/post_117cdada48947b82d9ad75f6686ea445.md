
At times, all members may be expected to contribute equally; for example, by keeping reflective diaries or capturing photographs or videos of practice. Larger studies may involve increasingly complex, interchangeable roles. The research proposal provides the guiding principles, and success depends on placing the right people in the right roles at the right stage.

Achieving meaningful change requires in-depth analysis of current practices, identifying areas for improvement and developing workable solutions. As Hall (2020) highlights, DBR is human-centred and solution-oriented, with researchers and partners collaborating to address educational challenges.

## The Educators’ Voice

Involving educators directly in the DBR process not only enhances their engagement with research but also improves classroom practices through the co-construction of research in practice, such as the design of professional development programmes (Getenet, 2019). However, it is important to understand that, when inviting educators to participate in a research capacity, you are adding to their workload.

An educator’s role as a classroom teacher is quite different from their role as a co-researcher in DBR. Participation often requires them to take on additional tasks alongside their usual workload. Consequently, if classroom routines and research responsibilities are not managed carefully, educators may feel overwhelmed or stressed. The following quote from an educator-participant illustrates this challenge:

> EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT
> 
> Educator’s Experience Participating in a DBR Study
> 
> When I was first invited to join a DBR study, I agreed without much thought. The project information was interesting, but I was unsure what to expect. More than once, I considered withdrawing because I was already so busy. Despite the challenges, I chose to continue and am grateful for the experience, though it was not always easy.

In fostering professional relationships, it is really important to acknowledge and thank research participants, including both educators and learners. Equally, educators need appropriate support as they take on the role of co-researchers. Some of these supports should be documented in the study information sheet and might include:

### PRACTICAL TIPS

- The researcher is available for support at (e.g., provide an email address) and encourages open communication regarding all aspects of the study.
- Dedicate time to responding to educators’ inquiries or feedback.
- Clarify boundaries (e.g., availability from 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. on weekdays).

## Professional Relationships and Power

Although collaborative, experiential, and problem-based learning are well established in education, educators do not always engage deeply with the theoretical foundations of interventions or understand how they translate into practice (Loughran, 2012). DBR addresses this gap by creating a space where theory and practice intersect, enabling educators to act as co-researchers and contribute their professional expertise to the research process.

Power dynamics inevitably shape these working relationships. As McDonagh et al. (2019) note, the way adults perceive their classroom role influences their sense of power and control. For DBR researchers, it is, therefore, essential to acknowledge and respect educators’ professional identities, treating them as co-researchers with unique insights (Bakker, 2018). Listening carefully to their experiences both validates their autonomy and generates knowledge that strengthens practice and research alike.

Challenges arise, however, when educators lack a strong sense of agency in their everyday practice. In such cases, questions about professional identity and achievement can surface, making it even more important for DBR to create inclusive and empowering spaces. The following example is drawn from a collaborative research meeting during the early stages of a classroom intervention in 2024. The vignette illustrates how dialogue can foster professional relationships and redistribute power.

### EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

### COLLABORATIVE RESEARCH MEETING

RESEARCHER: “Thank you both for meeting with me, I am really excited about working with you and I want to emphasise that this programme will be developed together. Your experiences as educators are invaluable and I see myself as a facilitator, I do not have all the answers and I have no intention of imposing my ideas on you without consideration to your ideas.”

EDUCATOR 1: “That is nice to hear, I did worry that you may not realise the challenges we deal with in the classroom any given day and if the normal routine might get in the way of your research. But to think it is our research, not your research, makes it easier for me to share my ideas.”

RESEARCHER: “Thank you for being so honest. Your ideas are crucial to the research and we can start by agreeing to take turns sharing ideas.”

EDUCATOR 2: “Yes, I like that idea, it also gives me a bit more confidence to speak up because the idea doesn’t have to be super complex, or does it?”

RESEARCHER: “Not at all. Practical and fun ideas are often the most useful.”

This dialogue shows how collaborative conversation can validate educators’ contributions and begin to rebalance power within DBR. By recognising professional identity and fostering openness, DBR not only strengthens relationships but also supports the creation of practical, context-sensitive solutions.

## The Learners’ Voice

In DBR, the student voice refers to engaging learners as active participants and co-creators in shaping educational practices, technologies, and interventions (Gros & López, 2016). While the concept is often highlighted, many DBR studies still prioritise educators and researchers, with limited student input. Yet the benefits of student participation are significant: enhancing relevance and inclusivity, generating fresh insights, and empowering learners by making education more meaningful (Barab & Squire, 2016).

Educators play a central role in supporting student engagement. Their established relationships with learners help create safe environments where students feel confident to share perspectives. Researchers can build on these relationships to incorporate student voices without disrupting classroom dynamics. A simple starting point may be the educator asking the class a question, such as, “How do you think I could improve how I teach this history lesson?”

### PRACTICAL TIPS

**Small Group Activity**: Facilitate small group discussions to foster community and shared responsibility. These encourage critical analysis, amplify student voices, and deepen understanding of key issues.

**Class Presentations**: Ask student groups to prepare short presentations of their solutions to classroom challenges. Presentations integrate student perspectives into the research design and spark dialogue that informs decision-making.

Critical thinking frameworks can further extend reflection. Learners might question whether current practices meet their expectations, examine assumptions about “good education,” or consider perspectives from peers, educators, and policy-makers (Alexander, 2020; Roche, 2014).

### PAUSE AND REFLECT

How might you support learners in co-creating educational solutions through dialogue? Possible questions include:

- Which subjects do you find most engaging or uninspiring?
- What teaching or learning styles work best for you?
- What factors in the classroom shape your learning?
- What would your ideal classroom look like?

By drawing on strategies like these and developing new ones collectively, educators and researchers can ensure student perspectives are genuinely integrated into DBR.

## Communication Is Key

Effective communication is the cornerstone of DBR. From the earliest stages, it enables researchers, educators, and learners to share knowledge, negotiate goals, and co-develop solutions. Clear communication ensures that all participants understand the aims, objectives, and practical steps of a project, reducing the risk of confusion or misunderstanding. Just as importantly, communication in DBR is not only about transmitting information; it also involves building trust, listening attentively, and creating a shared space where ideas can be expressed, challenged, and refined.

Steven Pinker (2014) argues that spoken conversation is innate, arising from the social nature of human interaction and our capacity for mutual understanding. Yet despite this, adults often find it difficult to articulate their thoughts clearly. Most of us have experienced leaving a conversation wondering, “What was that all about?” In professional contexts, such uncertainties can make effective communication elusive. For this reason, researchers and educators must actively work to overcome communication barriers in order to build clarity, avoid misunderstandings, and support genuine collaboration.

## Collaboration: From Dialogue to Action

Collaboration in DBR is not just a starting point but a process that deepens over time. Once a shared problem has been defined, collaboration evolves through cycles of design, implementation, and reflection. What begins as dialogue gradually becomes collective action, with researchers, educators, and learners contributing in complementary ways.

Effective collaboration in DBR rests on several interrelated features:

- **Shared Decision-Making**: Valuing all voices equally so that outcomes reflect the needs of both research and practice.
- **Iterative Dialogue**: Maintaining regular, open communication, whether through formal meetings or informal conversations.
