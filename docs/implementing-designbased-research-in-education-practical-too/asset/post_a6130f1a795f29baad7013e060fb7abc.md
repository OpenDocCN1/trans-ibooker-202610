## Ethics in DBR

Ethics in DBR extend beyond securing formal approval; they represent an ongoing, relational, and reflexive process. Researchers must continually attend to the people, contexts, and potential consequences involved in their work. Although ethical procedures differ across institutions and countries, the core values of care, transparency, and integrity remain central. This section explores key ethical considerations with particular attention to evaluation and reflection.

### *Returning to Ethical Foundations*

Throughout the lifecycle of a DBR study, the participants’ autonomy, dignity, and rights must be upheld. Informed consent is fundamental, yet genuine ethical practice also depends on cultivating trust, respect, and transparency in all interactions.

When ethical dilemmas arise, it is useful to revisit the original ethics application, which serves as a reference point for decision-making. The application remains valid as long as its stated conditions are met; if any aspect of the study changes, researchers are responsible for following the appropriate procedures to ensure ongoing compliance.

Researchers should also remain mindful of the additional demands that evaluation may place on participants, particularly educators with already demanding workloads. Ethical DBR involves designing evaluation processes that respect participants’ time and capacity. This might include simplifying instruments, embedding evaluation within existing practices, or co-developing tools that align with participants’ routines and professional preferences.

Finally, ethical evaluation requires an openness to learning from failure. Not all interventions succeed, and not all design choices yield the expected impact. Adopting an honest stance that acknowledges both achievements and shortcomings strengthens the ethical as well as the methodological integrity of DBR (Bakker, 2018).

## Power Dynamics and Participants as Co-Designers

Power dynamics in educational research refer to differences in authority, influence, and control that shape collaboration and communication. In DBR, teams often include researchers, educators, and sometimes students or community members, each contributing unique expertise and perspectives.

Evaluating research within such collaborative settings requires sensitivity to these dynamics (Weinbaum et al., 2019). This is particularly important in DBR, where participants are not merely subjects but co-designers of interventions. When collaborators collectively examine the validity of their assumptions about a problem or solution, existing hierarchies can affect how openly individuals express their views.

Power dynamics are equally significant in classroom-based research. Participants who are directly involved in shaping interventions may feel pressure to provide positive feedback or may hesitate to offer a critique. To address this, researchers should intentionally cultivate environments that support honest and constructive dialogue. Helpful strategies include building rapport through ongoing communication, using informal feedback tools, or anonymising evaluation data to reduce any perceived risk.

## Ethical Research with Children

Traditionally, research involving children has tended to position them as passive subjects rather than as active contributors (Ørngreen, 2015). In contrast, participatory approaches emphasise listening to children’s perspectives and recognising them as knowledgeable participants in the research process (Griffin, 2019). Ethical research with children, therefore, requires relational care, attentiveness, and a genuine recognition of their contributions (Abebe & Bessell, 2014).

Drawing on a DBR study involving children aged 3–5 and their educators, ethical practice was grounded in institutional approval, informed parental and educator consent, safeguarding procedures, and data protection protocols. This approach reflected Abebe and Bessell’s (2014) four key ethical principles:

- **Clearly articulate** the values underpinning the research.
- **Bridge** formal ethical procedures with local cultural and institutional norms.
- **Recognise** that research takes place within relationships and personal interactions.
- **Uphold integrity** through transparency, accountability, and honesty.

These principles informed a sociocultural, constructivist approach in which children were treated as co-constructors of knowledge. Ethical reflection extended beyond formal approval processes to include classroom interactions, the participant’s voice, and the researcher’s role in shaping those dynamics.

Ethical responsibility continues throughout the entire lifecycle of a DBR project. Across the iterative cycles of design, implementation, and refinement, researchers must regularly revisit critical questions such as:

- Are our practices still ethical?
- Are participants’ voices genuinely represented?
- Have new risks emerged, and how should we respond?

By integrating ethical reflection into evaluation, DBR not only ensures methodological soundness but also demonstrates social responsibility. This alignment enhances the credibility of findings and strengthens relationships with participants.

## REFLECTION

Ethics in DBR is not a one-off approval or fixed protocol. It is an ongoing, context-sensitive practice that shapes every phase of research. Particularly in evaluation and reflection, ethical vigilance ensures that projects remain grounded in care, justice, and integrity. By embedding ethical awareness in DBR’s reflective cycles, researchers enhance the relational, methodological, and transformative potential of their work.

## Reflection in DBR

As the example above illustrates, reflection in DBR is not something reserved for the end of a project. It is an integral part of the research process. Reflection allows us to make sense of the unfolding design, assess participant engagement, and monitor how the intervention and theoretical framework converge. As new data emerges, it is important to pause and ask:

- What aspects are working well?
- What is not working effectively?
- What valuable insights can we draw from this analysis?

These reflective moments guide decision-making and help ensure that the research remains grounded in practical application while contributing to theoretical understanding. As McKenney and Reeves (2019) emphasise, reflection is the key to learning from design, rather than merely observing it.

## Reflection as Integration of Theory and Practice

DBR seeks to enhance practice while simultaneously contributing to theory. Reflection enables this integration. By analysing events and questioning the reasons behind them, we uncover the patterns and deeper significance within the data. In many DBR projects, theory co-evolves with the intervention.

**EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT**

Komatsu et al. (2025) conducted a design-based study to improve students’ understanding of mathematical proof. Their intervention was initially grounded in established theories of mathematical reasoning. However, classroom observations revealed unexpected reasoning strategies that diverged from those theories.

Instead of dismissing these findings, the researchers used them as prompts for reflection. They refined both the design and the theoretical framework, incorporating students’ informal reasoning into subsequent cycles. Over time, their theory evolved to reflect more accurately how students engage with mathematical proof in authentic classroom settings.

This study shows how reflection can lead not only to design improvements but also to richer, more grounded theories — one of DBR’s core objectives.

Reflection also supports ethical and responsible research. In DBR, researchers often juggle multiple roles: facilitator, designer, observer, advocate. These overlapping roles demand continual self-awareness. As Barab and Squire (2016) remind us, DBR researchers are not neutral observers but active participants in the system under study. Reflecting on our assumptions, intentions, and positionality safeguards the integrity of our work and our relationships.

In essence, reflection is a key component of DBR. It shapes design adaptations, decision-making, and our understanding of complex educational contexts. Whether it is embarking on your first DBR project or leading a team, cultivating reflective habits, being mindful of power dynamics, and practising honest self-assessment, each will strengthen your research and build your confidence.
