Evaluation, therefore, examines whether the anticipated mechanisms have occurred; for example, whether a story-based learning environment has fostered the predicted forms of critical thinking. Such assessments carry important implications not only for classroom practice but also for broader theories of learning.

DBR researchers evaluate both the practical success of an intervention and the validity of the theory that underpins it. For instance, if a design is guided by the principle that collaborative projects deepen comprehension, the evaluation should not only measure student outcomes (e.g., project performance) but also gather evidence (e.g., interviews, concept maps) to explore whether collaboration truly enhances understanding.

Findings from each evaluation cycle inform the refinement of both the intervention and its theoretical framework. Effective approaches can be retained and generalised, while less effective ones prompt redesign or theoretical rethinking (Bakker, 2018; Reeves & McKenney, 2012). Over time, this iterative process results in interventions that are validated in practice and in design principles that are empirically and theoretically grounded.

The following example illustrates how this evaluation process can inform design decisions and enhance educational practice.

# EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

# NURTURING CRITICAL THINKING IN PRESCHOOL

# Phase 1

+   - Finding: A child selected a familiar story and answered all of the educator’s questions without demonstrating critical thinking.
+   - Design Principle: To foster critical thinking, children need novel or open-ended content that encourages reflection and reasoning.

# Phase 2

+   - Finding: During storytelling, confident children responded readily to questions, while hesitant children **remained silent**.
+   - Design Principle: To foster critical thinking, educators must create equitable opportunities for participation so that all children can share their ideas.
