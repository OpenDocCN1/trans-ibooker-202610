## Locating Your Positionality

As a master's or PhD student or early career researcher, you may already have been asked to reflect on your positionality. If not, consider: what is your positionality, and how does it influence your research? These questions can feel challenging, but they are crucial, as your responses will shape the direction and quality of your work. Holmes (2020) suggests examining positionality through three areas:

- 1. The subject under investigation – Why is this topic of interest to you?
- 2. The research participants – Why did you select this group?
- 3. The research context and processes – What kind of knowledge do you aim to produce, and what answers are you seeking?

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

In my doctoral research on the development of critical thinking skills in young children, I recognised the significance of enquiry-based learning. I firmly believe that enquiry and debate are essential components of effective family communication. My background as a preschool educator guided the selection of research participants. My work experience, coupled with my research findings, influenced my decisions regarding the research context and processes and the subject under investigation.

Examining these interconnected elements can help clarify your evolving positionality. Importantly, positionality is not fixed; it develops as you broaden your perspectives on knowledge and its purposes, and on the diverse needs of learners. This journey of reflection is also a pathway to professional and personal growth, particularly in educational research, policy, and practice.

## Articulating Your Statements

Have you drafted a positionality statement? If so, did you write it yourself, slowly and critically, or use a template? While AI tools and online templates can produce passable text, outsourcing this work risks superficiality and undermines the ethical purpose of positionality: to examine how your values and experiences shape decisions across the study. Use such tools, if at all, only to spark thinking; your positionality statement must be authored by you. Because positionality is multifaceted and evolves over time, it is advisable to engage in reflexive self-reflection across the project.

## PAUSE AND REFLECT

- What assumptions or expectations do I bring to this study, and how might they shape my interactions and interpretations?
- How do my beliefs about the research context influence the choices I make?

These, or similar questions, may help you critically reflect on how assumptions and beliefs shape your positionality. For a fuller discussion of philosophical and interpretative frameworks, including the insider-outsider theory, see Creswell and Poth (2016).

In essence, positionality profoundly shapes the quality of research, reminding us that ethics extend beyond methodology to include how we represent ourselves and our work. Alongside this, researchers must also safeguard participants’ rights through careful attention to data protection. Both are central to building trust and conducting responsible research.

## Data Protection

When conducting DBR, it is crucial to address data protection concerns and develop effective strategies to safeguard participants’ privacy and adhere to ethical and legal regulations while conducting and documenting your study. Data protection policies vary by country and institution, but there are common principles across jurisdictions. The following checklist may be useful to ensure your study meets core data protection requirements and demonstrates ethical responsibility:

## PRACTICAL TIPS: YOUR DATA PROTECTION CHECKLIST

### Admin

- Submit a review form to your institution’s data protection committee (if required).
- Confirm requirements before submitting your ethics application to avoid delays.
- Contact your ethics committee or research board for guidance.
- Record details of any data protection training completed.

### Study Details

- Clearly outline ethical measures for working with human participants.
- Specify how participants’ personal data will be safeguarded.
- Provide written information for participants, including aims, data use, and storage procedures.
- Use clear, jargon-free language to support informed consent.
- Secure all data with restricted access to maintain confidentiality.

Adhering to these requirements and others outlined by your institution not only protects participants but also strengthens the ethical foundation of your research, reinforcing trust and accountability within the research team and community.

## PAUSE AND REFLECT

Before moving on, ask yourself:

- Am I clear on the ethical application requirements of my institution?
- Have I drafted a clear data protection protocol for handling sensitive data?
- Has my team received appropriate data protection training?
- Have I prepared and shared all necessary data protection forms?
