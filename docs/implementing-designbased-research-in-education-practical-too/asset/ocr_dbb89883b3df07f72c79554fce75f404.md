Another strategy is to build in anchors for comparability. For example, all educators might commit to implementing a single core activity, even if they structure the surrounding lessons differently. This creates a shared reference point across cases while still respecting contextual variation.

In the analysis phase, you can then employ techniques from comparative case studies, treating each classroom or iteration as a case in its own right. By systematically documenting adaptations (e.g., through lesson logs, debriefs, or adaptation trackers), you can later identify patterns: when does flexibility strengthen the design, and when does it undermine its core principles? This transforms what could appear as messy inconsistency into an opportunity for enquiry into how interventions interact with diverse contexts.

# PRACTICAL TIPS

+   - Clearly define core vs adaptable elements of the design.
+   - Track every adaptation and record the rationale behind it.
+   - Treat changes as valuable case data, not deviations.
+   - Use cross-case comparisons to identify patterns and generate deeper insights.

# Educator Buy-In and Role Conflicts

# The Issue

Some educators may be hesitant to adopt an experimental approach that feels disruptive to their established routines or that risks exposing their teaching practice to scrutiny. Even when they are open to innovation, they may struggle with the ambiguity of holding a dual role: simultaneously being a practitioner responsible for teaching and a co-researcher engaged in systematic enquiry. Without clarity and support, this can lead to anxiety, resistance, or the perception that DBR is an “extra burden” layered onto an already demanding workload.
