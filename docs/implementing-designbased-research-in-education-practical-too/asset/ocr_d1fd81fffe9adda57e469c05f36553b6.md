# PRACTICAL TIP

Develop a simple guide or matrix for participants outlining which parts of your intervention are core and which are flexible. This can be a powerful communication and training tool. By explicitly identifying these beforehand, you give participants guidance on where they have leeway to improvise.

# Collaboration between Researchers and Practitioners

Effective DBR hinges on genuine collaboration between researchers and the educators who enact the intervention. This partnership is not peripheral; it is the engine that drives the entire process. Without strong relational trust and shared ownership, even the most carefully designed intervention risks faltering when it meets the realities of classrooms or lecture halls.

Crucially, DBR is not research done on schools or educators but research done with them. Educators are engaged as co-designers, co-implementers, and co-learners, bringing contextual expertise that is every bit as valuable as the methodological expertise of the researcher (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012). This distinguishes DBR from related approaches such as action research: while both value practitioner voice, DBR positions collaboration at the heart of rigorous, design-driven enquiry, ensuring interventions are both theoretically grounded and practically viable. As Bakker (2018) emphasises, DBR is research conducted “with educators, for educators.” This principle becomes especially salient during implementation, when designs are stress-tested in authentic contexts.
