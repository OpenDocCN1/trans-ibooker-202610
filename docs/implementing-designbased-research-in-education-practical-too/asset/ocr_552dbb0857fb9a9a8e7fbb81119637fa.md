This requires us, as researchers, to focus on all of the people in the research context in a holistic way, acknowledging and exploring the many roles these people may inhabit in their own lives (parents, teachers, learners, friends, peers, mentors) and their human dimensions beyond mere demographics. As educational researchers, this mindset draws us away from a narrow focus on specific educational roles and demographic characteristics in order to situate each person firmly at the centre of the multiple systems that interact in their lives.

# Be Holistic

This mindset follows on from being human-centred. If we are to be genuinely human-centred, this entails that we consider the whole person when it comes to our research partners, not just their particular role or actions within our specific research context. Furthermore, we must consider the research context itself, holistically, as intersecting systems of connected and interacting individuals. Complexity is a given, and context matters. For example, a classroom context is its own complex system of individuals interacting, and it is situated within school and community systems that interact with the classroom system and so on. Indeed, Don Norman, one of the pioneers of Human-Centred Design and Design Thinking, represents this complexity using the term Humanity-Centred Design (2023). This phrase acknowledges that we exist in interconnected networks of individuals within systems with a wide range of factors that influence our behaviours and needs and that it is only by working within these full ecological systems that we can design successfully.

In real terms, it may not be possible to account for, track or measure, all variables within a given environment. However, within DBR, we intentionally take the real world with all of its complexities, ambiguities, and messiness as the research context, rather than a laboratory simplification:

> We have to accept human behaviour the way it is, not the way we would wish it to be.
> 
> (Norman, 2013, p. 7)
