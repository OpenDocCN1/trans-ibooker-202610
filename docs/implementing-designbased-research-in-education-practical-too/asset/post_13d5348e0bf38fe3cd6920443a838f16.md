
## Reeves’ Model of Design Research (2000, 2006)

The key contribution of Reeves’ Model of Design Research (2000, 2006) lies in operationalising DBR as a systematic yet flexible methodology, applicable across curriculum design, educational technology, and professional learning. Compared with earlier conceptual models, Reeves gives DBR practical research scaffolding, ensuring transparency, replicability, and cumulative knowledge-building.

The model is organised into four key phases: (1) analysis of practical problems, where real-world issues are identified and thoroughly examined to ensure the research is grounded in genuine needs; (2) development of solutions, which involves creating evidence-based interventions or tools tailored to the identified problems; (3) iterative testing and refinement, where solutions are tested in real-world settings, and data-driven adjustments are made to improve their effectiveness; and (4) reflection to refine theories and practice, allowing researchers to evaluate the broader implications of the findings and contribute to the theoretical landscape while offering actionable insights for educators.

A further hallmark of the Reeves’ model is its focus on collaborative partnerships, ensuring that interventions are co-designed with educators to align with real-world educational contexts. By integrating theoretical rigour with practical relevance, the model bridges the gap between research and application, fostering innovations that are sustainable and scalable. This model has been instrumental in advancing the integration of DBR into educational technology, curriculum development, and the training of educators.

## McKenney and Reeves’ EDR Framework (2012)

The EDR Framework developed by McKenney and Reeves (2012) is widely regarded as one of the most influential models in the field of educational research. Drawing on the analysis of earlier models, this framework presents a generic and adaptable structure that encapsulates the essence of the EDR process. It highlights three key phases: analysis/exploration, design/construction, and evaluation/reflection. These phases interact seamlessly with real-world practice. The framework’s flexibility allows it to adapt to various educational contexts while maintaining its core focus of generating dual outputs of practical interventions and theoretical knowledge. This dual-purpose approach ensures that the findings not only address immediate challenges in education but also contribute to the broader body of knowledge in the field.

The McKenney and Reeves’ EDR Framework (2012) has significant implications for educational research and practice. It advances the field by explicitly articulating dual outcomes – usable educational interventions and refined theoretical insight – and embedding continuous practitioner collaboration throughout. Unlike earlier DBR forms, EDR is less a specific design cycle and more a meta-methodology, adaptable across contexts and levels of scale. It also stresses capacity-building for teachers, positioning educators as co-designers who learn through participation in design. By focusing on producing interventions that are both scalable and evidence-based, the framework supports the development of sustainable educational innovations. Additionally, the generation of theoretical insights enriches the academic understanding of the teaching and learning process, fostering continuous improvement and innovation. This dual focus makes the EDR Framework an invaluable tool for advancing both practice and research in education.

## Plomp’s EDR Phases (2013)

Plomp’s EDR phases (2013) provide a structured framework for addressing complex educational challenges through a systematic and iterative process. This model comprises three core phases: Preliminary Research, where problems are identified and contextual analysis is conducted; Prototyping, which involves designing and refining interventions through iterative testing; and Assessment, which evaluates the intervention’s effectiveness and theoretical contributions. What distinguishes Plomp’s approach is its balance between structure and flexibility: each phase explicitly links empirical research with iterative design. Compared with McKenney and Reeves, Plomp places a slightly greater emphasis on evaluation and formal assessment of effectiveness, offering a more linear but still iterative process. His model is widely used for applied educational development projects where researchers and practitioners collaborate to create validated, scalable solutions.

Finally, in the Assessment Phase, the programme was implemented in multiple schools, where data on learner performance and engagement highlighted its effectiveness and informed further refinements.

## The DBIR Model (2017)

DBIR, as articulated by Fishman et al. (2013), represents an important evolution of DBR, extending its focus from classroom-level innovation to systemic educational change. While traditional DBR and EDR centre on developing and refining interventions, DBIR foregrounds implementation as a design process in itself. It recognises that effective, lasting innovation depends not only on well-designed pedagogical solutions but also on the organisational, social, and policy infrastructures that support their adoption and sustainability.

DBIR is built on four guiding principles: (1) a focus on persistent problems of practice identified collaboratively with educators; (2) iterative, co-design processes that engage educators as equal partners; (3) a commitment to developing the capacity for sustained change; and (4) the generation of theory about both learning and implementation. Implementation is not an endpoint but an ongoing, adaptive process that evolves through cycles of design, enactment, and reflection across multiple contexts.

Unlike the classroom-bounded orientation of early DBR, DBIR focuses on scalability, contextual diversity, and systemic improvement. It studies not just what works, but what works, for whom, and under what conditions. By examining the infrastructures and capacities that enable sustainable innovation such as leadership structures, data systems, and professional learning networks, DBIR bridges the gap between design research and implementation science. It repositions design not merely as the creation of interventions, but as the co-construction of the conditions that allow educational change to take root and endure.
