Key features of collaboration in DBR include:

- **Shared Decision-Making**: All voices are heard and valued, and decisions made are balanced to reflect research and practice needs.
- **Iterative Dialogue**: Regular conversations keep ideas evolving.
- **Shared Learning**: In the spirit of continuous professional development, everyone learns from each other, bridging theory and practice.
- **Ongoing Reflection**: Continuous reflection ensures clarity, relevance, and improvement.

## Shared Decision-Making

Shared decision-making can be an outcome of well-organised collaborative research meetings. These meetings can take place among some or all team members during the various phases of the study, providing an opportunity for discussion and the coordination of data as they become available.

Research meetings serve as a collaborative platform, primarily focused on generating diverse insights from team members while also fostering trustworthy relationships. This collaborative approach facilitates shared decision-making, enabling the co-defining of problems and solutions. These meetings should be conducted professionally and reciprocally, ensuring that all participants have an opportunity to express their perspectives openly and without judgement. In this way, collaborative meetings foster trust in team members and a deeper mutual understanding of the different stages of the study as it develops. Consequently, collaboration across the various DBR phases is beneficial in identifying and resolving issues such as data inconsistencies, methodological challenges, and, at a human level, any interpersonal conflicts that may arise when working with individuals.

## Beyond Speaking: The Art of Listening

Dialogue is an active process of communication that compels us to articulate our thoughts into words (Fisher, 2013). The art of listening transforms dialogue from a passive exchange into an active process of communication; however, effective dialogue requires expert listening skills, where individuals focus on the speaker’s message rather than rushing to express their own thoughts. In practice, this may prove more challenging than one might anticipate.

> 
> Can anyone communicate effectively without first understanding the fundamental need to listen?

The better we listen, the more information we absorb, which equips us to contribute meaningfully to the conversation. By attentively listening to what others think, feel, and experience, we can use their insights to inform and challenge our own assumptions. In his work with students, Robert Fisher (2013) cautions that, while we may hear, we do not always truly listen. This serves as a reminder to practice active listening in our interactions with both colleagues and learners.

An educational researcher plays a pivotal role in creating a supportive environment where all voices are heard and valued. One way to demonstrate and practice active listening is by summarising key points from what has been said, thereby clarifying whether the intended message was accurately conveyed (Elder and Paul, 2019). For instance, you may respond, “Am I correct in saying that you believe the education system can be improved?” You then pause to allow the speaker to clarify if you understood and interpreted their words as intended. This approach not only enhances communication but also conveys genuine interest in the contributions of others.

As a DBR researcher, you have a unique opportunity to ensure the voice of the participants is heard throughout the study duration, ensuring that every team member has an opportunity to express themselves and receive appropriate responses.

## Iterative Dialogue as Pedagogical Practice

Robin Alexander, a prominent figure in the field of dialogic teaching, underscores the significance of dialogue in stimulating and captivating learners’ attention during classroom discussions (Alexander, 2018). His principles of dialogue are derived from his research on dialogic, and elaboration, rather than being derived from a single definitive answer. The next scenario illustrates this process in practice:

EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT
When a preschool educator asks, “What do you think happens to a puddle after it rains?” children offer a range of possibilities, such as “Evaporation, soaking into the ground, the wind takes it away.”
Each response sparks another question, guiding learners to refine and expand their thinking together.

This glimpse into classroom practice shows how knowledge may be co-constructed by listening to others and sharing ideas, even with very young children. Instead of aiming for immediate closure, the educator encourages a process where children’s ideas accumulate and interconnect. Iterative dialogue, then, is not just a teaching strategy but also a stance: it frames learning as open-ended and provisional, always capable of being extended through further questioning.

Within DBR, this pedagogical stance has methodological implications. Dialogue takes place not only between teachers and learners but also between researchers and participants, or among partners working collaboratively. It ensures that insights are revisited and re-examined as studies progress, making the research process adaptive and responsive both to conceptual and human needs.

This early discussion introduces iterative dialogue as a foundation for learning and classroom enquiry. ## Collaboration and Feedback

Effective collaboration and constructive feedback are vital in educational practice as they allow educators to co-construct knowledge and improve outcomes together. At the heart of collaboration and feedback lies purposeful dialogue where questioning becomes a tool for reflection and deeper understanding. In this sense, purposeful questioning is central to collaboration, as it stimulates critical thinking and invites deeper reflection within a team.

When framed effectively, questions act as catalysts for generating constructive feedback and can guide colleagues towards new ways of investigating the educational challenges at hand. Embedding questioning strategies into dialogue not only drives the iterative process but also helps refine emerging ideas, ensuring that potential solutions are both theoretically grounded and practically viable. In this way, thoughtful questioning becomes the foundation of a collaborative environment, one in which diverse perspectives are valued, and knowledge is co-constructed to strengthen design outcomes.
