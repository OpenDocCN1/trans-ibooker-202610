# Power Dynamics and Participants as Co-Designers

Power dynamics in educational research refer to differences in authority, influence, and control that shape collaboration and communication. In DBR, teams often include researchers, educators, and sometimes students or community members, each contributing unique expertise and perspectives.

Evaluating research within such collaborative settings requires sensitivity to these dynamics (Weinbaum et al., 2019). This is particularly important in DBR, where participants are not merely subjects but co-designers of interventions. When collaborators collectively examine the validity of their assumptions about a problem or solution, existing hierarchies can affect how openly individuals express their views.

Power dynamics are equally significant in classroom-based research. Participants who are directly involved in shaping interventions may feel pressure to provide positive feedback or may hesitate to offer a critique. To address this, researchers should intentionally cultivate environments that support honest and constructive dialogue. Helpful strategies include building rapport through ongoing communication, using informal feedback tools, or anonymising evaluation data to reduce any perceived risk.

# Ethical Research with Children

Traditionally, research involving children has tended to position them as passive subjects rather than as active contributors (Ørngreen, 2015). In contrast, participatory approaches emphasise listening to children’s perspectives and recognising them as knowledgeable participants in the research process (Griffin, 2019). Ethical research with children, therefore, requires relational care, attentiveness, and a genuine recognition of their contributions (Abebe & Bessell, 2014).
