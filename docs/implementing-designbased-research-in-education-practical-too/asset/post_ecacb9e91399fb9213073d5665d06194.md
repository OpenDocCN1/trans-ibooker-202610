
## Focus on Theory

## Decision 6: Is There a Focus on Theory and Theory Building?

DBR aims to contribute to knowledge and theory, not just design solutions. If only the solution matters, then you are implementing a design project rather than a research project. If you wish to develop an understanding of the theory underpinning your solution, then you are conducting a DBR project. In this sense, theory is both input and output at all stages of the DBR process. It is critical to the initial exploration of the context of the study through the literature review, and in conjunction with the need-finding activities, theory generates design principles that inform the initial question formulation and intervention design. It provides the theoretical framework for the exploration and evaluation of the research context and the intervention phase – guiding the researcher(s) in deciding what to focus on and how. It provides the framework for data analysis from the needs analysis to the intervention evaluation. The goal of a DBR project is to solve a complex problem in the real world using, extending, and testing theoretical understandings of learning in order to achieve this goal (Barab & Squire, 2004). Hoadley and Campos (2022) emphasise the tension in DBR between the complementary identities of designer and researcher. DBR offers “a way to study designed interventions that challenge the traditional relationship between research and design” (Hoadley and Campos, 2022, p. 9). The focus on theory maintains and sustains the researcher identity throughout the design process – grounded in and contributing to theory.

## Focus on Social Justice

## Decision 7: Is the Main Focus Social Justice or Theory Building?

The final decision point relates to the commitment to social justice. A key distinction between DBR and other community-based participatory approaches is the underpinning commitment to pay close attention to elements of social justice. DBR, while it might aim to solve problems, does not have an explicitly transformative or emancipatory agenda. While from a surface observation, these approaches may seem to share many characteristics in how researchers work with participants and stakeholders, in how they seek to build trust and work to understand the context, in practice, or in underpinning philosophy and intention, they may differ. Within DBR, the goal is to address the problem identified with research partners and while this may transform the partners’ experiences or even their world view, that is not the planned intention of the research. DBR aims to act in a culturally responsive way that acknowledges possible differentials of power but does not seek to dismantle or change those power structures. Another surface similarity, that of engaging deeply with research partners at all stages of the research process, is also implemented in DBR to different ends and somewhat by different means. In participatory approaches, such as participatory action research, the goal is to develop partners’ understanding of their own context and empower them to transform it. In DBR, there is a nuanced difference in that participants are design partners, experts within the context of study about the validity and usefulness of a chosen solution within the frame of their own experience. Social Design-Based Experiments (SDBEs) do take an explicitly transformative approach within the parameters of DBR (Gutiérrez et al., 2020), but standard DBR research does not necessarily incorporate this focus.

## KEY TAKEAWAY

The initial needs-finding phase of DBR would greatly benefit from the kind of embedded engagement of an ethnographic approach — to observe and interrogate the lived experiences within the context of study. Similarly, where iterative cycles of investigation have identified a possible effective formulation of an intervention, this might be interrogated in an experimental or quasi-experimental study of a scale appropriate to the phase of the research. Overall, DBR might best be presented as a structure within which to develop a long-term research agenda that is responsive to the context and findings and adopts appropriate methodological approaches for particular phases of the work.

## Chapter Summary

This chapter outlined how DBR emerged over the last 40 years in response to drivers both in educational research and design. DBR offers a pragmatic and flexible approach to addressing complex problems in the field of education and learning. It takes a holistic perspective on the human experience, placing the human at the centre of the research and design process. It emphasises the unique capacity of DBR as a dynamic framework for long-term, context-sensitive research agendas. This chapter identifies the key mindsets of DBR and sets out explicitly how it is differentiated from other research approaches. In fact, DBR can be considered as an overarching approach within which other approaches or methods are deployed, as needed, at different stages and for different scales. As Barab and Squire note, DBR is “not so much an approach as it is a series of approaches” (2004, p. 2). The other chapters in this book provide a guide for researchers through the phases of a DBR project, focusing on how to use the mindsets and methodologies of DBR to generate usable theory and practical solutions in real-world educational settings.
