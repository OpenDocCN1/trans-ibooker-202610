# Time Constraints and Scheduling Conflicts

# The Issue

Time is one of the scarcest resources in schools, and class timetables are often already overloaded with curricular requirements, extracurricular activities, and school-wide events. Introducing a new intervention can feel like one demand too many. Educators may worry about “losing time” on the prescribed curriculum or disrupting established routines, while researchers may experience frustration when carefully planned sessions are cut short by unexpected interruptions. These interruptions can be as mundane as a change in home collection time or as unpredictable as fire drills, testing days, outdoor play, or spontaneous art sessions. Over the course of a DBR project, such scheduling pressures can accumulate, threatening the integrity and continuity of the intervention.

# What Can Help

The key to navigating time-related challenges lies in proactive collaboration, flexibility, and creative integration. Begin by engaging with school management and educators to clarify the project's aims and practical requirements. This early dialogue helps secure an agreed, dedicated time slot; even for a small pilot study, this might be as modest as a 30-minute session twice per week.

Another strategy is to integrate the intervention with existing curricular goals, so it is not perceived as an “add-on” but as a complement to what teachers already need to cover. DBR also benefits from a buffered, flexible timeline. Expect and plan for disruptions by scheduling additional weeks or spare sessions and design cycles that are short and manageable in length. A tightly scoped three-week cycle is far more likely to be completed successfully than an ambitious eight-week programme that risks derailment. When time is squeezed, prioritise the core activities that are most critical to testing the design assumptions. This ensures that, even with disruptions, the essential elements of the intervention are implemented.

Finally, seek opportunities to streamline instruction and data collection. For instance, everyday classroom practices, such as short reflective writing tasks, exit tickets, or partner discussions, can serve dual purposes: advancing learning while also generating data for the research team.
