We can integrate the tools of Design Thinking, Systems Thinking (Arnold & Wade, 2015), and even Futures Thinking (Canina et al., 2020) to put this mindset into practice. Furthermore, as educational researchers, theory can support us in adopting and operationalising a holistic perspective. For example, bioecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006) highlights the interplay of systems inhabited by a person of interest in their development, or sociocultural theory (Vygotsky, 1978) examines the dynamic processes in the interactions of a learner with others and their environment that support learning and development. The theoretical framework can help us as researchers to maintain this focus on the holistic view of the research context and our research partners as humans who are dynamic agents within this context.

# Iterate, Iterate, Iterate

In DBR, there is no single optimal solution that is envisioned from the outset. Solutions are designed through an iterative process of exploration, prototyping, and evaluation that is ongoing. This iterative process is fluid and non-linear. At each stage, change is possible: changes to the problem definition, changes to the solution, changes to the context, changes to the underpinning framework. The iterative process is documented rigorously to articulate the rationale and the impact of the changes made along the way.

# Bias to Action

DBR always seeks to make changes in the world. It is interventionist in nature. The focus on multiple iterations pushes us as researchers to make our ideas tangible through rapid prototyping and to evaluate them in the real world. In DBR, the goal of evaluation is to learn more in order to move forward and improve.
