DBR researchers would argue strongly that DBR is the ideal approach to develop theory as it can be conceptualised, developed, and validated in real-world, iterative research cycles; thus, rendering the theoretical and ontological constructs explored more robust and more detailed.

Theory is and should be constantly evolving. A theory represents the current best approximation of knowledge in an area, and it is the goal of science to extend, supersede, integrate, and otherwise modify theory to develop further our knowledge to the next best approximation. The constructs of a theory can support us to observe, analyse, and develop our understanding in a given area.

Halliday and Martin (1993) discuss how this tension is represented in scientific writing – while scientists acknowledge that reality is dynamic, they construct knowledge and write about it in a way that is static. In DBR, researchers acknowledge and harness the dynamic nature of theory in order to design and evaluate interventions and deepen understanding.

The emergence of mixed methods research (MMR) as a mechanism to integrate both broad and deep perspectives on phenomena of interest in productive ways (Onwuegbuzie et al., 2023) was also a driver and enabler of the DBR approach in educational research. MMR has become standard in educational research since the paradigm wars of the 1970s and 1980s. Through earlier decades, two great traditions of social science research – positivist approaches using quantitative methods and interpretivist approaches adopting more qualitative methods – were pitted against each other as oppositional positions in examining and understanding the world. Pragmatism offered a philosophical underpinning that could bridge this paradigmatic divide. Ponce (2021) outlines how MMR developed during this time period as a research approach grounded in a pragmatist philosophy. In pragmatism in general and DBR specifically, it is not a question of “anything goes,” but rather the aim is to enlist and leverage available resources, using the breadth of available methods and approaches to generate useful understandings and solutions to real-world issues. In this way, DBR could be considered as an overarching approach that can encompass specific approaches at specific stages of a research journey, from taking an interpretivist qualitative approach at the start to explore the research context, to developing a full-scale experimental study at a later stage to evaluate a design that has been developed through iterative stages of exploration. The diffusion of MMR across the educational research community provided a context for the development of a new methodology that could leverage the rich affordances of MMR in a novel way.

These waves of change in educational research drove and supported the development of DBR as an educational research methodology grounded in theory and embedded in context to tackle the complex problems of today. Through the late 1980s and early 1990s, educational researchers began to test and generate theory in naturalistic settings through design-based iterations. In the USA, the early work of Ann Brown (1992), Allan Collins (1992), and Paul Cobb et al. (2003) in this area was seminal in distinguishing the emerging characteristics of the domain. The early terminology they used was “Design Experiment,” positioning this approach in contrast to experimental studies, which focused on more controlled contexts. In the Netherlands, in parallel, researchers were developing a similar approach, which they termed “developmental research” with similar guiding principles (Gravemeijer, 1994). From the outset, researchers emphasised that the purpose of these approaches was to test and generate theory in naturalistic settings as well as to address complex educational problems. Bakker (2018) provides an excellent historical overview of the development of the field as it emerged globally. This organic development has generated some differences in approach and terminology across the wide community of researchers.

The Design Thinking revolution, which developed from the 1970s, aligned with the drivers of change in educational research in order to generate Educational DBR, a research methodological approach to tackle complex problems in education, generate and test usable theory, and design theoretically grounded solutions that are useful in the real world. The next section outlines the imperatives in the field of Design which drove the Design Thinking revolution.

## The Emergence of DBR in Design

The drivers of change in educational research that led to the emergence of DBR in the early 1990s are paralleled by imperatives in Design during the same time period. These imperatives led to the emergence of Design Thinking, which is at the core of DBR. The term Design Thinking was coined in the 1960s within the design community. Since the 1990s, it has been popularised through the work of David Kelley with the design company IDEO and the d-school in Stanford, among others. It began as an exploration of how designers developed new and innovative products, services, and processes. Don Norman, in his seminal book, the Design of Everyday Things, highlighted how poor design could result in products that did not meet users’ needs and wants. Good design, however, is harder to identify because it fits our needs so well that it becomes invisible.

High-profile product design failures demonstrated the need for designers to consider how they could conceive, design, and develop products and processes that are needed and useful in the context for which they are designed. “New Coke” is a classic example of such a design failure.

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

## NEW COKE: A CAUTIONARY TALE OF POOR DESIGN

During the early 1980s, Coca-Cola was losing its share of the soft drinks market in the USA. Responding to this, company executives decided that changing the Coke taste would boost sales. They conducted taste tests and focus groups and surveys with the new flavour and got relatively positive results but a small proportion of very negative results, which they chose to ignore. New Coke was launched in April 1985 to great fanfare, and initially, the consumer response was good enough. However, very quickly, there was a huge public backlash that played out nationally and internationally across the media and even into government. Only 79 days after they had launched New Coke in July 1985, they withdrew it and reintroduced the “classic” recipe. Despite all of the time and money they had committed to testing the new product, the company had failed to appreciate just what the classic Coke taste meant to people.

This example demonstrates a number of characteristics of poor design:

- 1. The designers ran the standard consumer tests with people, but they failed to really listen to the people they were designing for. The consumer tests addressed the product taste but not what the product meant to the consumers and to consumers’ sense of identity. They placed only a narrow focus on one dimension of their customers and the new product – taste.
- 2. The design did not address an acknowledged need in the world. The project was instigated to address a company problem about decreasing sales but did not fully explore what consumers were actually looking for.
- 3. The design was fully finalised and released into the world without sufficient iteration. The road to good design is full of failure, cycles of iteration, and testing that inevitably have some failures and some successes. This design failure might not have been on such a large scale if they had iterated more over the design.

Design Thinking was developed to address exactly these kinds of considerations.

First, the need to acknowledge and explore holistic situations in their full ecological breadth was as critical in the field of Design as it was in education. In the New Coke example, the narrow focus on taste missed the whole cultural significance of Coke to the American psyche, a gap which became increasingly evident in the backlash against the new flavour. Throughout the 1980s, key figures in Design Thinking established a powerful focus on designing for the real world with Human-Centred Design and, more recently, Systems Thinking. Human-Centred Design is the beating heart of Design Thinking and, by extension, DBR. It situates people at the centre of all activity. The term “Human” rather than “User” is used very intentionally here. It emphasises the importance of:

- 1. The person in their fullest sense, not just as the user or consumer;
- 2. All the individuals in the context of use, not just those who might be users of a new product, service, or process;
- 3. The need to work with people rather than make decisions for them (“Nothing about us without us”).

Design Thinking offers a holistic approach to understanding what is influencing people’s behaviour and what needs are not being met in a particular context.
