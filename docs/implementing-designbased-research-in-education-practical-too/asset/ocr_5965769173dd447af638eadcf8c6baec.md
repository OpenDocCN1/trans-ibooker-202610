Once you know what you want to focus on, you need to consider how to maintain that focus over time. Observations can occur on a continuum from fully structured ones using explicit rubrics or coding schemes to completely unstructured jottings and notes that need to be written up after the event. In between there are semi-structured observations that may use a loose rubric or protocol, which allow for additional notes to be taken during the observations. In all cases, you should record the date, time, location, and any other key details you will need for analysis, such as persons present. It is also useful to sketch a map to provide context for the observations made after the fact: for example, a classroom layout and your position in that. With appropriate consent, photos are also invaluable in recording artefacts and objects and even the people in the setting. Structured observations can draw on existing coding schemes, rubrics, or schedules from the research literature (e.g., teacher-student interaction types) or can be designed by you to specify clearly what you need to record. The schedule should be very specific with clear instruction, so that it can be used consistently across time and by different people. Unstructured observations, however, do not have any structure. They allow for free text, using descriptive adjectives, comparatives, highly concrete or visualisable words to record key aspects of the setting carefully. It is useful to consider all the senses in this: how does the setting look, sound, smell, feel, and taste? It is important to be specific and answer all the WH questions relative to the observation: when, where, what, and who. Insofar as it can be observed through facial expressions and body language, try to record the emotions of the people in focus. Semi-structured observations can use basic protocols to direct the observers’ attention but are not too prescriptive or restrictive. For example, the POEMS (People, Objects, Environment, Messages, Services) protocol is often used in human-centred design. Cohen et al. (2017) provide a useful overview of a wide range of observation frameworks and guidelines that you can consider and adopt as appropriate. For semi-structured and unstructured observations, you may need to follow up the observations in the setting with field notes written very soon after the observation moment to develop a running record of interpretation and analysis as you go.
