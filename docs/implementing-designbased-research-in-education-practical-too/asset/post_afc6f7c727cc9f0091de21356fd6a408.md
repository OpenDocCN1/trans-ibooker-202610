## Chapter Summary

Chapter 4 has outlined the core phases of DBR and illustrated how they operate as part of an interconnected, iterative cycle. By moving repeatedly through the stages of problem analysis, design and construction, implementation, and evaluation and reflection, researchers avoid treating interventions as static products and instead develop solutions that evolve in response to evidence and context. Ultimately, this chapter highlights that DBR is not simply about solving isolated problems but about generating interventions that are effective, sustainable, and theoretically robust. Its strength lies in continuous refinement, collaborative engagement with partners, and sensitivity to the realities of classrooms and schools.

# THE HUMAN ELEMENT OF DESIGN-BASED RESEARCH

# EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Before the questions, before the data, before the methods, there is a person. A researcher, sitting at a kitchen table late at night, or walking a quiet trail, turning over an idea in their mind. All research, no matter how structured or rigorous, begins from a deeply human place: curiosity, concern, hope, or even frustration. We sometimes imagine research as neutral or clinical, as if it unfolds in a vacuum. But behind every research decision is a set of lived experiences, relationships, values, and identities. The way a question is framed, the methods we choose, and the voices we centre all reflect who we are and what matters to us. This chapter explores the human side of research: the emotions that arise, the ethical tensions we navigate, and the connections we forge along the way. It reminds us that research is not just about generating knowledge; rather, it is about how we relate to that knowledge, and to each other. To be a good researcher is not only to be systematic but also to be reflexive and compassionate. We bring ourselves into every part of the process. And that, far from being a weakness, is what gives research its power.

## Part 1: Communication and Collaboration in Design-Based Research

The spoken word is as old as our species; we observe this in children who naturally tend to speak from a very young age. Speech in itself is a tool that transcends borders and cultures, bringing us a mix of languages, dialects, and sounds that one person delivers to another. It is worth noting that speaking and communicating serve different purposes. Speaking involves the production of words spoken aloud, while communicating encompasses the exchange of meaning through various modalities, including speech, gestures, tone, and silence, ensuring mutual understanding.

As adults, the reality is that we often find it easy to speak, yet difficult to articulate our thoughts to communicate clearly and effectively. For example, have you ever had a conversation with someone and come away thinking, “What was that all about?” In a research context, we may struggle to communicate with clarity because we are not exactly sure what we want to say or even how to get our ideas into words. As professionals, we must overcome communication challenges to avoid confusion or misunderstandings. There is both a hard and an easier way to improve how we communicate. The hard way is to do nothing and hope we get better. The easier way is to develop communication skills and strategies that help to move your thinking from thoughts into clearly delivered pieces of information.

### The Role of Communication in Design-Based Research

In Design-Based Research (DBR), communication facilitates collaboration, knowledge generation, and the co-construction of innovative solutions throughout a research study’s timeline. In other words, from the beginning of an idea to the completion of a study, communication drives the DBR process.

From the initial conception of an idea to crafting a research proposal, the best way to communicate your ideas effectively to a diverse audience is to use clear and straightforward language. By articulating your ideas clearly, you ensure that your research objectives, purposes, and goals are effectively conveyed to all relevant partners, facilitating a seamless transition from a research proposal to the acceptance of a new research study.

Another consideration is that communication extends beyond mere clarity of words; it also conveys intention. For instance, as a member of a research team, you have a fundamental responsibility to communicate ethically and professionally. To achieve this, you must possess a comprehensive understanding of the research objectives you aim to accomplish in collaboration with your team members. Furthermore, understanding the intended outcomes for learners and communicating this is also a useful way to identify potential challenges.

In essence, by communicating clearly from the project’s outset, individuals can better understand the educational issue being investigated and identify potential challenges with the research design, negotiate goals, and establish overarching study priorities. In a pilot study exploring a play-based mathematics curriculum, researchers initially proposed video-recording every session. However, some participants expressed that they were uncomfortable being videoed. Through early communication, educators highlighted this concern and suggested using adapted learning journals instead. By negotiating this research method, the team addressed ethical challenges and enriched the study design with classroom-informed insights.

Through effective communication and the attentive consideration of the participants’ views, we can broaden and enrich our perspectives as individuals, professionals, and members of a team. While communication and research focus on the exchange of information and perspectives, collaboration goes further by turning that dialogue into shared action and collective ownership of the research process.

## Collaboration in DBR: A Multi-Partners’ Approach

Collaboration is at the core of DBR. It is a dynamic, participatory process where researchers, educators, and other partners work together to co-develop solutions to educational challenges and generate innovative ideas (Bourdeau, 2017).
