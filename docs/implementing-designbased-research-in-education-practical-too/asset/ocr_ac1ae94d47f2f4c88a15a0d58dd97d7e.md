She discusses some of the complex processes involved in empathy that “enable us to perceive the emotions of others, resonate with them emotionally and cognitively, to take in the perspective of others, and to distinguish between our own and others’ emotions” (p. 74). In the past, and even still in popular discourse, empathy was considered an innate trait, something you were born with or not, and indeed recent neurological evidence suggests there is a neurobiological basis for empathy (Harris, 2007). To researchers who are experienced in participatory research, this is a central principle of their work already, and they may feel it is a core part of their identity. However, empathy is not just an innate part of us. Research has clearly demonstrated that the neurobiological pathways of empathy can be strengthened through action; that is, empathy can be learned (Phillips et al., 2013).

In DBR, this focus on empathy is not just a theoretical consideration but is very much a practical one which drives our actions as researchers. Chapter 5 emphasised the importance of relationships with partners in DBR and how we can actively seek to construct these within our DBR projects. In this chapter, we focus on phase 1 of the DBR cycle, where we actively seek to empathise with our partners in the research in order to develop a deep understanding of their context and their needs in order to negotiate correctly the right problem to solve in the research. This involves active engagement to understand how they do things and why, how they think about the world, and what is important and meaningful to them.

Batson et al. (2007) identify some of the essential precursors to empathy. This work highlights that empathy increases when we value others and attempt to take on their perspective. This is important in DBR research, as it identifies dispositions we should cultivate as researchers but also activities that we should take in order to understand our partners in the research process better. First, we actively set out to value our partners – articulate and acknowledge their value in the research process. Second, we should try to consider what it feels like to walk in their shoes, to take their perspective, and to live in their world. In this phase of the DBR research process, the methodologies focus on how to engage in empathetic enquiry. This mindset helps you to avoid the egocentric trap of designing for yourselves rather than for your research partners.
