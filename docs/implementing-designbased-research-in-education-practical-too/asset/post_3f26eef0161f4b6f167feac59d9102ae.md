## PHASE 3 IMPLEMENTATION OF A DESIGN-BASED RESEARCH INTERVENTION

## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT: SETTING THE SCENE

In the first semester of a large university’s teacher education programme, a group of lecturers introduced a new approach to developing pre-service educators’ skills in maths lesson design. Rather than delivering content through traditional lectures, they trial a series of small, practice-based workshops where students collaboratively plan, micro-teach, and reflect on short maths lessons. The sessions are co-designed by faculty and school-based mentors to ensure alignment with classroom realities. As implementation begins, some students thrive in the active, hands-on format, while others struggle with the ambiguity of open-ended tasks. These early tensions mark the start of an iterative process that will shape the intervention’s refinement over time.

## Introduction

The implementation phase of Design-Based Research (DBR) is where theoretical insights and designed interventions are tested and refined in real educational contexts. Rather than being a linear deployment, implementation in DBR is iterative and marked by cycles of testing, feedback, and revision (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012). In this crucial stage, researchers and educators collaborate to introduce innovations in authentic settings such as classrooms, schools, or communities. Through this process, interventions are evaluated for their feasibility, effectiveness, and sustainability.

This chapter provides both conceptual frameworks and practical strategies to support the implementation phase of DBR. It draws from diverse examples to illustrate how iterative design, contextual responsiveness, and close researcher-educator partnerships are central to effective educational innovation. Additionally, it includes reflective prompts, checklists, and tools to help researchers and educators navigate common implementation challenges.

## Implementation in DBR: Bridging Design and Practice

The implementation phase is central to DBR because it moves enquiry from theoretical development into authentic practice, generating empirical data that inform ongoing cycles of design and improvement (Barab & Squire, 2004). By situating interventions in real educational contexts, implementation allows researchers and educators to uncover context-specific challenges and opportunities and to adapt designs to serve diverse learners better (Reeves, 2000). Crucially, implementation is not a one-time test but an iterative process: each cycle provides feedback that guides refinement, ensuring that interventions remain both robust and relevant over time (Wang & Hannafin, 2005).

In this way, implementation acts as a practical test bed where theoretical assumptions are examined, innovations are stress-tested, and ideas are progressively honed. It bridges research and practice by ensuring that designs are not only grounded in evidence but also viable in the complexity of real-world settings (Fixsen et al., 2005). DBR embeds implementation within a broader cycle of design, enactment, analysis, and redesign. Rather than producing a final verdict after a single trial, implementation generates successive rounds of evidence that both refine practice and revisit theory. Each enactment is thus treated not as a judgement of success or failure but as an opportunity to deepen understanding. This perspective is reflected in models such as McKenney and Reeves’ (2012), where implementation spans analysis and exploration, design and construction, and evaluation and reflection. Depicted as a continuous, sliding-scale process, it begins with small-scale piloting and expands in complexity and scope as the designs mature, reinforcing its integrative role across all phases of DBR.

Because implementation occurs in dynamic contexts, flexibility is essential. DBR anticipates and embraces unexpected events, such as technical glitches, fluctuating learner engagement, or unanticipated interpretations, as valuable sources of insight. These moments are not treated as disruptions but as data that drive refinement. Each round of implementation, therefore, becomes both a practical trial and a site of theoretical reflection, simultaneously strengthening the design and expanding our understanding of how learning unfolds in context.

This chapter examines the implementation phase in greater depth, focusing on key dimensions that shape its success. These include:

- The role of piloting as an entry point,
- The importance of understanding implementation as an iterative enquiry,
- The importance of balancing fidelity with flexibility,
- Strengthening researcher and educator relationships through collaboration,
- Identifying best practices for implementing educational interventions,
- Identifying and addressing challenges associated with the implementation phase.

A natural starting point for implementation is piloting, where small-scale trials provide a low-risk opportunity to test feasibility, identify practical obstacles, and generate early insights before moving toward larger-scale enactments.

## Piloting: Starting Small

Before embarking on a full-scale implementation, conducting a pilot study is essential in DBR. Piloting allows the research team to test interventions on a smaller, manageable scale to identify potential issues, assess feasibility, and refine the intervention’s design based on initial findings (Collins, 1992; McKenney & Reeves, 2019). It serves as a preliminary, low-risk phase enabling researchers to identify or anticipate challenges, thereby optimising interventions before committing significant resources.

### Why Pilot an Intervention?

Piloting educational interventions has multiple advantages:

- **Early Identification of Issues**: Small-scale trials reveal technical, logistical, and instructional challenges that could disrupt larger-scale implementation (Damschroder et al., 2009).
- **Feasibility Assessment**: Pilot studies expose whether the intervention fits within existing educational structures and whether it can realistically achieve the intended outcomes.
- **Refinement Opportunity**: Data from pilot studies provide immediate insights for improvement, enhancing the overall quality and relevance (Sandoval, 2014).
- **Stakeholder Buy-In**: A successful pilot can build trust among partners by demonstrating an intervention’s potential effectiveness, thereby easing resistance during subsequent scaling (Coburn & Penuel, 2016).

The pilot revealed several issues: some students thrived in the collaborative planning tasks, while others struggled with the open-ended structure; lesson plans often showed creativity but lacked alignment with curricular objectives; and time management within sessions proved difficult.
