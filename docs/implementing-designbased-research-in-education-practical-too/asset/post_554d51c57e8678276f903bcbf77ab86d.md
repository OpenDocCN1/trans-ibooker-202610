She discusses some of the complex processes involved in empathy that “enable us to perceive the emotions of others, resonate with them emotionally and cognitively, to take in the perspective of others, and to distinguish between our own and others’ emotions” (p. 74). In the past, and even still in popular discourse, empathy was considered an innate trait, something you were born with or not, and indeed recent neurological evidence suggests there is a neurobiological basis for empathy (Harris, 2007). To researchers who are experienced in participatory research, this is a central principle of their work already, and they may feel it is a core part of their identity. However, empathy is not just an innate part of us. Research has clearly demonstrated that the neurobiological pathways of empathy can be strengthened through action; that is, empathy can be learned (Phillips et al., 2013).

In DBR, this focus on empathy is not just a theoretical consideration but is very much a practical one which drives our actions as researchers. Chapter 5 emphasised the importance of relationships with partners in DBR and how we can actively seek to construct these within our DBR projects. In this chapter, we focus on phase 1 of the DBR cycle, where we actively seek to empathise with our partners in the research in order to develop a deep understanding of their context and their needs in order to negotiate correctly the right problem to solve in the research. This involves active engagement to understand how they do things and why, how they think about the world, and what is important and meaningful to them.

Batson et al. (2007) identify some of the essential precursors to empathy. This work highlights that empathy increases when we value others and attempt to take on their perspective. This is important in DBR research, as it identifies dispositions we should cultivate as researchers but also activities that we should take in order to understand our partners in the research process better. First, we actively set out to value our partners – articulate and acknowledge their value in the research process. Second, we should try to consider what it feels like to walk in their shoes, to take their perspective, and to live in their world. In this phase of the DBR research process, the methodologies focus on how to engage in empathetic enquiry. This mindset helps you to avoid the egocentric trap of designing for yourselves rather than for your research partners.

## Be Curious

As with empathy, this mindset is central to this phase of the research and flows through into the methodologies. Leading from curiosity, not judgement, allows us to achieve all of the goals set out at the start of the chapter: it allows us to build relationships of trust, as it demonstrates a clear interest in what partners have to say, and it allows us to engage in the exploration necessary to develop a deep understanding of the research context and the lived experiences of the people in it.

The dictionary definition of curiosity is “an eager wish to know or learn about something” (2025). More precisely, Wagstaff et al. (2021, p. 363) define it as “the desire for knowledge and sensory experiences that sparks exploratory behaviour.” This orientation to investigation and exploration just for the sake of learning is crucial to a curious mindset in DBR.

As with empathy, this is a mindset that drives action. At its simplest, it is just to add the phrase “I’m curious” as a preface to questions. It clearly states to your research partner that you just want to learn; you are not looking to judge or to criticise or to evaluate; you just are really curious to find things out about their world, their experiences, and their needs.

## Be Aware of Your Own Lens

All of us have our own lenses and biases which influence how we see the world. No one is free of this, not the Dalai Lama or the smallest child. We are all influenced by the accumulation of all our past experiences. This mindset acknowledges this and seeks to address its impact on our research activity. This is about bias, which can be negative or positive but is never neutral. Both empathy and curiosity are tools to tackle our biases, helping us to reach out to our research partners without judgement and with an openness to learning about their worlds.

It requires conscious effort to surface and address biases. If you are working alone, you can do this by making those biases explicit to yourself by answering questions like “what are my life experiences in this context, with these people, in this job, etc.?”. It is also something that can be done with others, where you can invite your research partners and fellow researchers to act as your bias checkers, to call you up when you might need to remember to acknowledge and try to put aside your own biases. And you can offer to be that same gentle bias checker for them.

## Be Open to Imperfection and Ambiguity

As in our other chapters, we want to emphasise from the outset that, although we present DBR in discrete linear phases, the reality is not linear. It is fluid and iterative. The process and outcome of each stage guide the researcher on whether they need to go backwards or forwards to get to a better question or a better solution. The time committed to each phase can also vary wildly from iteration to iteration. This first phase of identifying the right problem to solve is typically quite long in the initial DBR cycle with extensive work to understand and empathise with the research partners and their needs and experiences. Subsequent iterations might be shorter, working with a smaller number of partners, or could again extend to involve further partners and other contexts. As a researcher, you need to be open to toggling back and forth between phases depending on the needs of the project in the given moment. You need to embrace the imperfections and ambiguities of a question that is “good enough” for now and still keep going.

### PRACTICAL TIP

These mindsets can all be summarised as pay attention and stay attentive. While you are with them, talking to them or observing, they are the centre of your universe, not you and your life but your research partners and their lived experiences and their needs. Even if you think you know the answers and you think you have seen it all, just pay all of your attention to your research partners, and this will bring insights that are enough to advance.

## KEY TAKEAWAY

In this DBR phase, suspend your judgement and analysis and immerse yourself in the lived experiences of your research partners. Actively engage your curiosity and empathy and openness with prompts – these mindsets can be learnt.

## Methodologies: Getting a Question That Is Good Enough to Start

**Why?** The goal here is not to arrive at the perfect question. That is impossible. The goal is to formulate a question that is good enough to start or continue with. All aspects of the DBR process, including this guiding question, require constant iteration and reframing throughout a project. Refining, broadening, narrowing, or otherwise changing the question is as much a part of the process as iterating over the solution. So don’t get too attached to your question, but rather be open to how it can change to open new perspectives and unlock ideas.

**How?** So how do we get to the question that is good enough to start with? In this phase of a DBR project, there are a number of methods available to us as researchers that we are going to explore below: we can ask our research partners directly about their experiences in surveys, interviews and focus groups, we can *observe* the research environment and our partners going about their activities within that, we can ask our research partners to *document* the research environment or particular activities for us through journaling, photo-voice studies or think-aloud protocols, or we get out into that research environment and try to *experience* it for ourselves. It is important during this stage to generate rich data that draws on different perspectives and experiences. Often researchers will use a number of these methods in combination as appropriate to the research context.

If some of these methods feel like only slightly different versions of the same method, you are right; they are. They are variations: different ways of getting to the same end goal of developing a deep understanding of your research partners within the research context.
