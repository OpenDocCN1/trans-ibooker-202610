
# THE ORIGINS OF DESIGN-BASED RESEARCH

# EDUCATIONAL RESEARCH MEETS DESIGN THINKING

# EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Ada and Leo are working as researchers in the area of emergent literacy in the UK. Over the course of their work, they have visited many early years education (EYE) settings and have observed that some of the evidence-based early literacy practices are not in use within these settings. The research evidence to support the effectiveness of these early literacy activities for the long-term literacy outcomes of three- and four-year-old children is very strong. They want to focus their research on improving the literacy outcomes of three- and four-year-olds in early years settings. This could seem like a problem with a simple solution: roll out the most effective early literacy practice in a sample of settings and evaluate its effectiveness. However, from their extensive experience in the field, Ada and Leo are aware that most of the research evidence is from children of middle-class parents in the USA. They are also conscious of how different each EYE setting is and of the many and varied challenges they face every day, such as staff retention and limited resources. It turns out that this is really a wicked problem: it is not evident which practice is most appropriate to which children in which settings as each child and each setting is unique; it is not obvious how to ensure that practice is deployed appropriately in each setting, and it is not even clear whether literacy is the question of greatest relevance to address in these contexts. Consequently, despite the wealth of research evidence to support particular early literacy practices, the researchers are unsure of how best to proceed to meet the needs of the children and those around them, parents and educators in particular.

**What should they do? Design-Based Research has emerged over the last 30 years to address just this kind of wicked problem.**

## Introduction

Education globally has many problems to solve, ranging from the simple to the complex. Often, even seemingly simple, technical problems turn out to be truly wicked. Design-Based Research (DBR) is a human-centred, theoretically grounded, holistic approach to tackling educational problems through intervention in the complex systems that are real-world educational settings. It incorporates both research to advance theory and design to produce usable educational products, processes, programmes, or policies. DBR is the beautiful offspring of Design Thinking and Educational Research, combining the core characteristics of both to create a coherent and powerful approach to undertaking research that aims to solve complex problems in education. Since its emergence in the 1990s, DBR has positioned itself clearly within the tradition of educational research as a methodological approach grounded in empirical research and theory to examine educational research questions ethically and with rigour. Specifically, DBR aims to solve educational problems through the exploration and implementation of contextually relevant and theoretically grounded solutions.

At the same time, DBR is situated within Design, and specifically Design Thinking, which emphasises a human-centred iterative process for identifying problems and designing solutions that are useful and usable in the contexts for which they are designed. DBR integrates the mindsets and methodologies of Design Thinking within the traditions and processes of educational research. The motivation for this has been to be true to the nature of rich and complex contexts within which learning and development happens.

This chapter sets out the rationale for the emergence of DBR as an educational research methodological approach over the last 40 years. It explores the drivers for this approach in educational research. It goes on to explore the conceptual origins of DBR in the field of Design and Design Thinking and considers the mindsets and processes of that field and what they can afford educational researchers today. Finally, it positions DBR within the broader landscape of educational research methods, identifying criteria for selecting DBR or alternative approaches suitable for specific research contexts and enquiries.

## The Emergence of DBR in Educational Research

Educational research aims to describe, compare, evaluate, explain, and/or develop educational practices, processes, programmes, and policies in ethically sound, methodologically rigorous, and theoretically grounded ways. Research methods textbooks set out ranges of research approaches to address these aims, drawing on different philosophical and theoretical underpinnings, as in Cohen et al. (2017), for example. These methodological approaches are in continuous flux in response to shifts in philosophy, culture, priorities, theory, and technology within disciplines and wider society over time. This is a fruitful generative process of revision and development that can expand the horizons of research in the field. In this vein, DBR emerged as a novel research approach in educational research through the early 1990s. It sets out to formulate solutions to complex problems in education using best practice in design. In the early 1990s, groups of educational researchers across different countries and institutions began independently to consider alternatives to existing research methodologies for a variety of reasons. The characteristics and motivations for DBR were convergent with a number of strengthening positions in educational research and theory over that time.

First and foremost, the evolution of research and theory in a number of fields, including the development of complexity theory, situated learning theory, and bioecological systems theory, emphasised the role of context in a range of situations, particularly in the realm of teaching and learning. It became clear for some researchers that the simplifications required to construct experiments or interventions in laboratory settings made it problematic, if not impossible, to relate or extrapolate research findings to real-life educational settings. Ann Brown (1992), one of the pioneers of DBR, in her discussion of her evolution as a researcher taking a design approach, described this evolution during her own career as “an increasing trade-off between experimental control and richness and reality” (Brown, 1992, p. 152) and highlighted the need to consider context more holistically and comprehensively in her research. There was a greater acknowledgement of the situated nature of educational experiences and that context did not constitute a set of factors to control but rather was critical to the enactment and experience of educational practices, policies, and programmes in the real world. Leo Van Lier (2010), writing in the context of language learning, captures this imperative for ecological validity as follows:

> Research often isolates particular pieces of the complex puzzle in order to study them in detail. However useful this may be, it obscures the dynamism of the actual teaching and learning work that goes on, and cannot show the emergent and contingent nature of that work.
> (Van Lier, 2010, p. 5)

This emphasis on taking a holistic and ecological approach to real-world contexts was very much the focus of some of the first examples of DBR in the literature, for example, the Design Experiments of Brown, Collins, and Cobb in the early 1990s (Brown, 1992; Cobb et al., 2003; Collins, 1992).

A second consideration in the development of DBR in the early 1990s, drawing on the pragmatist philosophy of John Dewey, for example, was a greater consideration of the need to develop and validate useful theory for practice and policy. This was by no means new in the social sciences and can be summarised by Kurt Lewin’s phrase: “there is nothing as practical as a good theory” (Lewin, 1943, p. 8). This resonates with the need for a more holistic perspective of research contexts: not only should research acknowledge the full context of the educational situation deeply, but it should also generate knowledge and theory that are useful in that context.
