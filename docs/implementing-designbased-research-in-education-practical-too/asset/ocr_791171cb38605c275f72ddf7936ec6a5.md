# Scaling as a Continuation of the Design Process

# EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

St Brigid’s educators began mentoring colleagues in the new schools but quickly discovered a number of unanticipated challenges. The daily timetables in other schools were organised quite differently, and some classrooms had as many as 30 children compared with the smaller groups that had participated in the original pilot. Staffing patterns also varied, with some schools sharing special education teachers across multiple classes. Rather than attempting to replicate the exact routines from the pilot site, the team treated these differences as opportunities for new learning. Using feedback logs and short reflective meetings, educators experimented with alternative grouping structures, such as rotating literacy stations and shared intervention blocks. These adaptations allowed each school to preserve the core features of the intervention while tailoring delivery to their own local realities. In this way, scaling became a process of redesign rather than simple replication.

In DBR, scaling should not be seen as the final step in a linear sequence but rather as an extension of the design process itself. Rather than seeking to “finish” a design once it proves effective in one setting, DBR emphasises the importance of continued enquiry, reflection, and refinement as the intervention moves into new contexts. Viewing scale-up as an extension of the design process reframes it as a dynamic, iterative endeavour, prompting researchers and teachers to treat each new implementation site as a living laboratory for continued learning and refinement. As interventions enter new schools, they interact with local structures, norms, and challenges. These encounters reveal both expected and unforeseen effects, offering new opportunities for learning and improvement (Barab & Squire, 2004).
