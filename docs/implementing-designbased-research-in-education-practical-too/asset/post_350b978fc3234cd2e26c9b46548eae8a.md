## Challenges in Scaling DBR Interventions

While scaling up DBR interventions holds great promise, it is not without its challenges. Preparing for and anticipating these challenges is critical to ensuring a smooth and effective scale-up process. These challenges often arise from the complex, variable nature of educational settings and the dynamic relationships between partners, resources, and policy environments.

## Contextual Variability

One of the primary challenges in scaling DBR interventions is the sheer diversity of educational contexts. Differences in school culture, infrastructure, educator readiness, and learner demographics can significantly affect how an intervention is received and implemented. What proves effective in one setting may not align with the values or capacities of another, necessitating continuous adaptation.

## Resource Limitations

Scaling often exposes resource constraints, whether in time, funding, staffing, or technology. Schools may struggle to allocate the necessary resources to maintain the integrity of an intervention, particularly if it requires additional planning time, materials, or professional development. Without adequate support, even well-designed interventions may falter during the process.

## Maintaining Core Integrity

As interventions adapt to fit new settings, there is a risk of diluting the core principles that made them successful in the first place. Balancing fidelity with the foundational concepts with the flexibility which is necessary is a persistent tension in the scale-up process. Without a clear articulation of the “non-negotiables,” essential elements may be lost or misinterpreted.

## Partner Turnover

Changes in leadership, staffing, or policy direction can disrupt continuity. When key champions move on, interventions may lose momentum. Institutional knowledge can quickly dissipate unless robust structures are in place to support knowledge transfer and leadership succession.

## Evidence of Impact across Contexts

Gathering robust evidence of impact across diverse settings can be challenging, particularly when the measurement tools or evaluation criteria vary. Inconsistent data collection practices, or a lack of alignment between the evaluation goals and local priorities, may undermine confidence in the intervention’s effectiveness.

## Change Fatigue

Educators are frequently presented with a steady stream of new initiatives in schools, which can result in resistance or disengagement, especially when past efforts have failed to bring about lasting improvement. Establishing trust and demonstrating early signs of impact are important steps in addressing this scepticism.

## Generating Design Principles through Scale-Up

### EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

After two years of expansion, the *Literacy Links Project* had grown into a vibrant professional learning network spanning six schools. What began as a small co-designed project in one classroom had evolved into a collaborative ecosystem where educators, literacy leads, and researchers continuously learned from one another. Through analysis of implementation notes, data dashboards, and educator reflections, the team began to identify recurring patterns that pointed to deeper, transferable insights. These findings were not simply about what worked in each school, but about *why* it worked and under what conditions it could thrive.

From this process, three design principles emerged that captured the essence of sustainable scaling. First, professional learning was most effective when led by *internal mentors* who modelled and coached within their own schools, rather than relying on external facilitators. Second, *data reflection* had the greatest impact when integrated into existing staff meeting structures, reinforcing shared responsibility for literacy outcomes. Third, *contextual flexibility* proved crucial: schools that adapted materials and schedules while holding firm to the instructional core achieved the strongest results. These principles became the project’s theoretical contribution, showing how scaling, when treated as an iterative design, deepens both understanding and practice across contexts.

Scaling provides a rich opportunity to extend not only the reach of a DBR intervention but also to refine and articulate the underlying design principles that support it. As interventions are adapted and tested across multiple settings, patterns emerge that help distinguish between core features and contextual adaptations. These insights can be translated into generalisable design principles, which guide future implementation and contribute to the broader field of educational innovation.

Design principles in DBR serve as theoretically grounded heuristics that inform how an intervention works, for whom, and under what conditions (Anderson & Shattuck, 2012). They are not rigid rules but flexible guides that help shape design decisions. As an intervention scale, real-world variability reveals which elements are essential for success and which can be modified.

This process strengthens the theoretical robustness of the intervention and enhances its adaptability. For example, a project that involves scaling a collaborative writing strategy might find that, across all contexts, structured peer feedback is critical to success regardless of whether it is delivered digitally or on paper. As these principles emerge, it is important to document them clearly, with examples and rationales grounded in data. This supports both future implementation and theoretical refinement.
