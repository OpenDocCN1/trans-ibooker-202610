From a practical point of view, self-documentation offers advantages over observation: the research context is where your research partners spend time in their normal lives, and so they can generate data over a longer time period than it might be feasible for you to observe in person. Furthermore, they will be involved in the normal activities of the setting and can document those from the inside, without any observer effects. However, self-documentation is hard work for your research partners. They would need to be very committed to the project goals to sustain this workload over an extended period. For this reason, it is very important to develop and pilot your self-documentation methods with your research partners so that it is as easy as possible for them to integrate into their normal processes or workflows. For example, teachers probably do not have time to type out observations on their computer during the day, but maybe they could jot down a few lines in a small notebook that fits in their pocket or bag. Or students might not want to write about what is important to them in their school, but they don’t mind snapping some pictures of their favourite places in the school and sharing those with you.

# PRACTICAL TIPS: DOS AND DON'TS

Dos

+   1. Think through the ethics and consent issues associated with self-documentation, in particular if you are working with children and young people.
+   2. Work with your research partners on what is the focus and mechanism for self-documentation.
+   3. Based on this collaboration, provide very clear instructions.
+   4. Have modest expectations – self-documentation is hard work, and people may not have the time or energy for it.

Don’ts

+   1. Don’t forget about data protection constraints, in particular for an image or video.
+   2. Don’t ask for too much.
+   3. Don’t forget to run a pilot to make sure what you are asking people to do is clear, feasible, and useful.
