Research partners might like or need to use other media, such as written output, drawings, visuals, or signs and gestures, as an alternative or additional means of expression. Partners may also have different languages, including sign language, that they wish to use to express themselves. These alternative methods of expressing meaning can be very rich and insightful and allow individuals to participate who might otherwise be unable or unwilling to do so.

Rather than a spoken interview, an email or text message exchange can take place as a written interview. This can be particularly helpful where the interviewee might need some time to consider and craft their answers. This can also allow for the use of more than one language for questions or answers. The use of translation tools could facilitate this and be more feasible than live interpretation. Interviewees can offer a visual representation rather than a spoken one in response to a question, such as a drawing. This can be particularly effective with younger children. In both face-to-face and online interviews, media can be combined to provide a rich communicative repertoire that can then be analysed together. For example, participants can be invited to write their thoughts on physical or virtual post-its or whiteboards. Online whiteboards offer participants the opportunity to contribute in a range of ways (drawing, post-its, graphics, images) in addition to speech, and all the spoken and written interaction can be recorded. For group interviews, this can be particularly useful as it can allow people to share their thoughts all at the same time, even if some of them do not get to speak. This kind of input can be unstructured as an option available or structured as part of a workshop format designed to elicit discussion. Some examples of structured formats include:

+   - **The “Bus-Stop” or “Carousel” Strategy**: This is a cooperative learning strategy applied to a research scenario. You decide on three to four key questions that you write on stations on a real or virtual wall. The group is divided into small groups of two to three, and each group is assigned a question. Each group discusses and writes a response to their question on the wall. Then the groups circulate to the next board and read the question and response and discuss and add something new to the discussion. By the end, everyone will have had the opportunity to respond to each of the questions and to engage in a discussion on it with all the group – either synchronously or asynchronously as they read previous responses.
