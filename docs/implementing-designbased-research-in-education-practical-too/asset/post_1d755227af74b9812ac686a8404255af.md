
## EXAMPLE IN CONTEXT

Student engagement is a huge field of study with some very powerful theories grounded in an extensive empirical evidence base. There is also a wide range of interventions and tools that have been shown to have a positive impact on student engagement outcomes across a range of contexts. Imagine your research context is a non-denominational school in a rural setting in Northern Ireland, where there is a high rate of school absenteeism (often used as a proxy for student engagement). The social and historical complexities of this post-conflict environment, with the added complexities of a rural environment in a post-digital society, mean that some of the tried and tested methods of other contexts for developing student engagement might just not be viable here, and the needs of the individuals in this environment are not equivalent to or comparable with those of others. For this reason, it is imperative in this setting to find the right problem to solve when it comes to student engagement – it could relate to school transport or the requirements of the agricultural calendar or quality of the school canteen food or staff dynamics or a wide range of other issues, but without talking to the people in the environment, a researcher would not know where to start.

As researchers, we know that in reality this process is very messy. Excellent research questions are derived through extensive research – both desk-based and in the field.

In Design-Based Research (DBR), defining the research question is explicitly not linear. It is an iterative process. The initial guiding question is refined and developed throughout the research process. While the overarching research objective may stay fixed, the specific researchable question may change as the right problem to solve becomes more apparent. In the above example, the research objective might be phrased as:

This study aims, in collaboration with stakeholders, to develop, implement and test an intervention to reduce school absenteeism in a rural school setting in Northern Ireland.

By conducting a thorough need-finding phase in DBR cycle 1, it might become apparent that students are mostly engaged and happy when in school, but the school calendar is at odds with the agricultural calendar in the area, and many students miss school to help on farms in the autumn, which results in a lot of conflict between teachers and students at the start of the school year, souring student-teacher relationships. This initial phase might address assumptions that the researchers may have had about post-conflict situations. As a result, the specific research question in DBR cycle 1 could become:

How do changes to the school calendar and timetable impact on school absenteeism and student-teacher relations in a rural school setting in Northern Ireland?

As the research progresses, the research question could develop further or even change completely if the initial assumptions are not validated in the research.

In this chapter, we outline how to find the right problem to solve in your DBR project. Bear in mind that this is only the best approximation of the right problem for now, and in order to progress, you will continually return to the question and iterate over this process to refine your “right problem to solve.”

## Goal for This Phase

The goal for this phase is to find the right problem to solve and to frame this as the best research question to address. What we need at the end of this phase is not the perfect question but a question that is good enough to start with. This has to be done with the project partners and in context, so you could break down this goal into a number of sub-goals:

- To develop and deepen relationships of trust with your partners in the research context;
- To develop an understanding of the context and the needs of your partners in the area of focus;
- To synthesise this information in order to find the most important problem to solve for now.

## Terminology

This phase of the process – identifying the problem to solve or research question to address – is fundamental to good design and, by extension, to good DBR. There are many names for this phase in Design Thinking and Human-Centred Design, and it can encompass one or more stages in the design process depending on the design model used:

- IDEO Design Framework: Inspiration;
- IDEO’s seven steps of Design Thinking Process: Frame a Question – Gather inspiration – Synthesise for Action;
- Stanford’s D-School Design Thinking Toolkit: Empathise and Define;
- Stanford’s D-School Process: Need finding;
- Enterprise Design Thinking Toolkit (IBM): Observe;
- Systems thinking: Gaining clarity, reframing.

In product design, it might be close to user research or market research or human factors research. However, the crucial difference between a Design Thinking approach and product design is that the former involves the people in the research context at the centre of the activity at all levels.

The terms might change, but the fundamental processes are the same across these different frameworks. This phase is about trying to understand deeply the needs and lived experiences of the people in your research context and distilling all of that into the best, workable question to answer or right problem to solve now.

This chapter sets out both the mindsets and the methodologies of the work, focusing on this particular phase of the DBR process.

## Mindsets for Finding the Right Question

We focus first on mindsets because they frame how we must approach our partners and our specific methodologies before we even begin. The key mindsets for this need-finding phase of DBR are:

- Be human-centred;
- Be empathetic;
- Be curious;
- Be aware of your own biases;
- Be open to imperfection and ambiguity.

### Be Human-Centred

Taking a human-centred approach is a baseline orientation for all DBR work. It is not just to be learner-centred or child-centred or teacher-centred; it is to be human-centred. This requires us, as researchers, to focus on the people in the research context in a holistic way, acknowledging and exploring the many roles they may inhabit in their own lives (parents, teachers, learners, friends, peers, mentors). As educational researchers, this mindset draws us away from a narrow focus on specific educational roles but situates each person at the centre of the multiple systems that interact in their lives. If we wish to approach this systematically and theorise the holistic perspective, the bioecological systems theory (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006) can provide a useful theoretical framework. It can help us, as researchers, to maintain this focus on the holistic view of the research contexts and our research partners as humans who are dynamic agents within this context.

### Be Empathetic

In human-centred design and Design Thinking, this phase of a project is often termed “Empathise” (D-School). Sam and Freddy’s scenario is very typical of product design and development over many years where product designers came up with great products, but it turned out nobody wanted them or nobody could use them or they would not work where they were deployed.

In Chapter 1, we gave the classic example of the New Coke design debacle in 1985. These kinds of epic failures of design at some level come back to the problem that the designers did not understand or empathise with the people who they intended to use their products. The designers thought they knew what the people wanted without even asking them. There is a long history of similar implementation failures in education where evidence-based interventions from one context fail when they are deployed in different contexts. This could be put down to the quality of the implementation, but very often it is attributed to a range of contextual factors (Domitrovich et al., 2008). Put simply, the intervention was not designed for its context of use. This phase of the DBR process involves not just asking your partners what they need but striving to develop a deep understanding of them so that you can help them identify what they need through discussion and collaboration.
