```
server:
    tomcat:
        threads:
            max: 200
resilience4j.bulkhead:
    instances:
        OrderService:
            maxConcurrentCalls: 150
            maxWaitDuration: 1000ms
```

In the code, you simply use the @Bulkhead decorator to specify the method that should be subject to the bulkhead behaviour. In the following example, a fall back method is also specified. This will be invoked when the bulkhead capacity is reached, and requests wait for more than one second.

```
@Bulkhead(name = "OrderService", fallbackMethod = "newOrderBusy")
    public OrderOutcome newOrder(OrderInfo inf) {// details omitted}
```

# IN THE WILD: SCALING MICROSERVICES

Amazon and Netflix were amongst the early pioneers, around 2009, of microservice based architectures at scale. Since then, much has been learned, and of course the scale of the systems built on microservices has grown incredibly. To deal with modern systems scale, Uber have evolved their microservice architecture to be based around collections of related services, known as domains, as described in this excellent technical blog post (https://eng.uber.com/microservice-architecture/).

Sam Newton’s book chapter ‘Microservices at scale’ (https://www.oreilly.com/radar/microservices-at-scale/) includes a case study that illustrates the importance of the fail fast, circuit breaker and bulkhead patterns. It is a forensic description of how cascading failures occur when slow service responses are encountered.

Finally it should be emphasized that sometimes microservices are not always the right approach. A case study describing how the benefits of microservices added unnecessary complexity at Istio (https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9520758) is well worth a read.
