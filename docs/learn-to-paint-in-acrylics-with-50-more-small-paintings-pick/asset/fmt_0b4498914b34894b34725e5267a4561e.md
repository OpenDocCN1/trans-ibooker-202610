
**1** Using the #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Unbleached Titanium mixed with a lot of glazing medium, keeping the texture as rough as possible. Allow to dry completely.
![Images](images/f0058-03.jpg)
**2** Mix a brick colour from Napthol Red, Yellow Ochre and a little Unbleached Titanium. Using a #4 bright brush, paint a brick pattern over the canvas, offsetting the rows and making each brick one brushstroke wide. The brick colour doesn’t have to be exact at this stage, as you’ll be layering more paint on top of it. As the paint on the brush runs out, the dry brushstrokes will help create the crumbling texture of the bricks. Allow to dry.
![Images](images/f0059-01.jpg)
**3** Mix a plaster or putty colour from Unbleached Titanium, a little Napthol Red and a little Mars Black. The colour doesn’t have to be totally true to life, but you need it to be thick and opaque. Using a #6 flat brush, apply the paint over parts of the brick pattern to create areas of broken plaster.
![Images](images/f0059-02.jpg)
**4** Now you can start to have some fun. Make up thin mixes of any colour you want (although it works best with colours closest to the brick colours, such as Yellow Ochre, Cadmium Yellow Medium and Mars Black). Using an almost dry #6 flat brush, brush each one in turn randomly over the canvas, almost to the point where you can no longer distinguish the bricks. Allow each colour to dry for 5 minutes or so.
![Images](images/f0059-03.jpg)
**5** Using Unbleached Titanium and a #1 round brush, repaint the mortar between the bricks. This re-establishes the original brick pattern while leaving the broken colours from step 4 visible on the bricks themselves.
![Images](images/f0059-04.jpg)
You can repeat the process of distressing and reclaiming as often as you want, using thin glazes for the brick colours and thicker paint for the mortar and crumbling plaster. This treatment would also work well for rusty metal and weathered, peeling paint.

### 13 Simple reflections in water: Hot-air balloon
Reflections in water are always a popular subject. Here are a few simple tricks to help you paint them convincingly. If the water is completely still, the reflection will be a perfect mirror image of the main subject. However, it’s often more interesting if there’s some movement in the water, as this breaks up the reflection and can create some intriguing shapes and patterns. Because the reflection is broken up, you don’t need to make it as detailed as the main subject – basic smudges of colour are sufficient – and you should avoid painting reflections with hard edges. Generally speaking, colours appear more muted in water reflections – even a white cloud or snow will need to be greyed down a little in a reflection. If you use a fairly dry brush for the reflection, patches of the underlying blue will be visible; this helps to convey the impression of ripples in the water.

Materials
![Images](images/f0060-02.jpg)
**1** Mix your sky colour from Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White. Using a #6 flat brush, paint the sky in the top third of the canvas and a strip of water at the bottom. Leave space in the middle for the clouds.
![Images](images/f0060-03.jpg)
**2** Mix a much paler blue (about 90% Titanium White) and fill in the cloud shape. Add more Ultramarine to the sky colour from step 1 and put in the shape of distant hills, leaving some white space between the hills and the clouds to represent snow. (This is a good opportunity to practise mixing tints.) Let the canvas dry completely, otherwise the Yellow Ochre in the next step will turn green.
![Images](images/f0061-01.jpg)
**3** Using the #4 filbert brush, block in the shape of the balloon in Yellow Ochre mixed with a little glazing medium, letting some of the blue show through in places to make it look slightly weathered. Let dry.
![Images](images/f0061-02.jpg)
**4** While you still have Yellow Ochre on your brush, begin painting the reflection; this is just a smudge of colour, not the entire balloon shape – the little patches of blue jutting into the reflection break up the shape and convey the effect of rippling water. Mix a pale yellow from Titanium White and a little Cadmium Yellow Medium. Paint the brightly lit side of the balloon and add the same colour to the reflection.
![Images](images/f0061-03.jpg)
**5** Add more Titanium White to the yellow mix from step 4 and paint the highlight on the upper left side of the balloon. Add a touch of this colour to the reflection. Switch to the #4 bright brush and use Mars Black to paint the balloon’s basket and the silhouettes of the people in it. Repeat in the reflection. Mix Napthol Red with a little Mars Black and paint the stripe on the balloon, then introduce that colour to the reflection. Using the #4 filbert brush, paint the brightest highlight on the balloon with pure Titanium White. Don’t add this to the more muted reflection.
![Images](images/f0061-04.jpg)
It might be tempting to include the whole of a reflection in your painting, but that would give it as much importance as the main subject. A hint is often all you need.

### 14 Rough and smooth surfaces: Egg on toast
This exercise gives you the chance to explore two very different textures in the same painting – the rough surface of a piece of toast and the smooth, slightly shiny surface of a fried egg. For rough surfaces, you can exploit the texture of the canvas itself, as well as the various textural techniques at your disposal, such as scrubbing the paint on unevenly and varying the direction of your brushstrokes. Smooth surfaces require very even applications of paint. The key difference, however, is the way light affects them: smooth surfaces have very sharp, clearly defined highlights as the light is reflected in them, whereas rough surfaces have very few or no highlights at all.

Materials
![Images](images/f0062-02.jpg)
**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with Unbleached Titanium. Let dry. Using the #4 filbert brush, outline the slice of toast in Burnt Sienna – it’s just a rectangle with an ‘M’ shape on top – and fill it in roughly, using Yellow Ochre for the lighter parts in the centre and adding Burnt Sienna to the mix towards the edges. Vary the direction of your brushstrokes and allow the texture of the canvas to show through. Let dry completely.
![Images](images/f0062-03.jpg)
**2** With the #6 flat brush and Titanium White, paint a doughnut shape one brushstroke wide for the egg white, leaving the centre open. Apply two or even three coats, depending on how clean and opaque you want it to be, allowing the paint to dry between coats. This is good practice for your brush control, too, as you have to keep restating the circle.
![Images](images/f0063-01.jpg)
**3** Using the #4 filbert brush and Burnt Sienna, strengthen the toasted edge of the bread and add a shadow to the left of the egg white. Fill in the egg yolk with Yellow Ochre, then add Cadmium Yellow Medium on top, leaving a little Yellow Ochre showing on the left-hand side for a shadow. This shows that the yolk is raised and rounded; if it was flat, there would be no shadow.
![Images](images/f0063-02.jpg)
**4** Switch to the #4 bright brush. Mix a very pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and a touch of Mars Black and use it to paint shadows around the left-hand sides of the yolk, egg white and toast. Apply one final layer of Titanium White to the egg white, this time adding a tiny bit of Mars Black to knock back the whiteness just enough for you to be able to add a highlight in the final step.
![Images](images/f0063-03.jpg)
**5** Add highlights to the top right of the yolk and egg white in pure Titanium White, and let dry thoroughly. Mix Mars Black with lots of glazing medium and load a clean brush (or even an old toothbrush). Gently flick the brush with your finger to spatter tiny specks of black pepper on to the egg. If it doesn’t work, just wipe it off with a paper towel and have another go.
