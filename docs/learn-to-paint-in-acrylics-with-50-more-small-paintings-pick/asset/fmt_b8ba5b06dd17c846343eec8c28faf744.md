
### 3
Mix a shadow colour for the barn from equal amounts of Napthol Red and Burnt Sienna. Using the #4 bright brush again, paint the shaded area on the underside of the roof (which you’ll add in step 4) and to the right of the building. The flat profile of the brush makes it perfect for these small, precisely shaped areas.

![Images](images/f0053-02.jpg)

### 4
Still with the bright brush, using Mars Black straight from the tube, put a roof on the barn and use the corner of the brush to create the windows. Switch to a #1 round brush and paint the fence posts in Mars Black, making them slightly irregular in shape and size to give them a more rustic appearance.

![Images](images/f0053-03.jpg)

### 5
Now build up the texture of the wheat with individual vertical brushstrokes, using various yellow mixes: Unbleached Titanium + Cadmium Yellow Medium in the distance; Cadmium Yellow Medium + Yellow Ochre in the middle ground; Yellow Ochre in the foreground. Vary the size of your brushstrokes, too, making them smaller nearer the barn and larger in the foreground. This helps to create a sense of scale and distance. Finally, indicate lines of wire between the fence posts with Unbleached Titanium.

![Images](images/f0053-04.jpg)

As the saying goes, ‘less is more’. The short, textural marks used for the wheat give the impression of slightly blurred grasses blowing in the wind without detracting from the main subject – the weathered old barn.

### 10 Simple composition: Tabletop still life
The objects in this still life are just a series of rectangles and a circle, and because they’re viewed from almost directly overhead, there’s no distortion in the shapes. This makes them very simple to draw, leaving you free to concentrate on developing opaque layers and controlling the brush. Although a still life such as this appears very simple, you still need to take your time setting it up. Do the objects you’ve chosen relate to each another in some way – by theme, colour or shape? How will you position the different elements within the picture space? Remember to look at the spaces between the objects (the negative spaces), as well as at the objects themselves.

#### Materials
![Images](images/f0054-02.jpg)

#### 1
Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with Cadmium Yellow Medium. Using a #1 round brush, draw the outlines of the objects and indicate the areas occupied by the cast shadow of the coffee mug in Yellow Ochre thinned with a little glazing medium.

![Images](images/f0054-03.jpg)

#### 2
Mix a bright blue from Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White. Using the #6 flat brush, paint the tabletop background, cutting out the shapes of the objects in your composition (and mug shadow) as you do so.

![Images](images/f0055-01.jpg)

#### 3
Mix a dark blue-black from Ultramarine Blue, Mars Black and a tiny bit of Unbleached Titanium and paint the cast shadows under the objects. Switch brush sizes as necessary: I used a #1 round for the thin pencil shadow, and a #6 flat for the much larger mug and book shadows.

![Images](images/f0055-02.jpg)

#### 4
Using the #1 round brush and Titanium White, paint the book label and the white rim of the coffee mug. Mix a medium grey from Mars Black and Unbleached Titanium and put in the shadows on the pages of the book, under the label, inside the mug and on the mug handle. Add a dab of grey to the end of the pencil for the metal ferrule. Let dry, then add a highlight on the ferrule in Titanium White.

![Images](images/f0055-03.jpg)

#### 5
Add a tiny touch more Mars Black to the grey from step 4 and paint the book label graphics. The mug casts a crescent-shaped shadow on the coffee; paint this in Mars Black. Use a very thin mix of Burnt Sienna and Ultramarine Blue for the coffee; using a lot of glazing medium will help you achieve the swirly pattern on the coffee. Paint the pencil tip in Yellow Ochre and the pencil eraser in Napthol Red and a little Cadmium Yellow Medium. Add a dab of Mars Black to the pointed tip for the pencil lead.

![Images](images/f0055-04.jpg)

The colour scheme for this painting is a primary triad (see page 26). Repeat the exercise, using the colour wheel on page 26 to identify other possible colour schemes.

### 11 Painting wood grain: Artist’s palette
Layering is the perfect technique for wood grain. First, lay down a very thin, broken layer to establish the base colour of the wood; you can then ‘draw’ the wood-grain pattern on top with your brush before covering the area with another thin layer of paint through which the wood grain remains visible. To make the wood grain really stand out, paint the background using a smooth, opaque colour, cutting out the shape of the palette as you do so; you may need two or more layers for this. Another benefit of this approach is that you don’t need to worry about keeping your wood-grain markings within the confines of the object: if they spill over slightly, you can just cover them up with the thick background colour, so you can be freer with your brushstrokes.

#### Materials
![Images](images/f0056-02.jpg)

#### 1
Mix Yellow Ochre with lots of glazing medium. Using a #6 flat brush, scumble it very loosely and roughly over the canvas. When touch-dry, paint the outline of the palette and the thumbhole in thicker Yellow Ochre using the #1 round brush. In the middle of the palette, indicate the general direction of the wood grain with a few well-placed lines. Let dry completely.

![Images](images/f0056-03.jpg)

#### 2
Mix a very thin wash of Yellow Ochre and a little Unbleached Titanium, with lots of glazing medium. Using the #6 flat brush, wash it over the palette; the underlying lines that you applied in step 1 will show through.

![Images](images/f0057-01.jpg)

#### 3
Mix a dark blue from Ultramarine Blue, Unbleached Titanium and Mars Black. Using the #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the palette and thumbhole as you do so. The wood grain stands out really well against the smooth, opaque background.

![Images](images/f0057-02.jpg)

#### 4
For the paint daubs on the palette, use any colours you want. Apply each colour in turn with a #4 filbert brush and let dry, then apply more layers of colour to create a thick, impasto texture. ‘Impasto’ is a technical term that just means applying the paint in layers so thick that they’re raised up from the surface.

![Images](images/f0057-03.jpg)

#### 5
Mix a dark blue-black from Ultramarine Blue and Mars Black and put in the cast shadows around the palette and thumbhole. Paint the brush handle and the outline of the ferrule in Mars Black. Use Titanium White for the bristles and to add a line of highlight along the ferrule and handle. Paint some colour on the end of the brush.

![Images](images/f0057-04.jpg)

You don’t need to put in every single detail of the wood grain, so long as you capture the general direction and width of the lines. Woods differ from each other not only in colour, but also in their patterning, so try painting a sample square of some different types of wood.

### 12 Distressing the surface: Brick wall
This is a really fun, experimental project that gives you the chance to play around with both thin and opaque layers to create somewhat unpredictable, organic-looking textures. The whole point is that it’s messy and imprecise: you degrade, or distress, one part of the painting and then go over it again to refine it and bring it partway back. In the process, the different layers add both colour and texture that become an integral part of the finished painting. Once you’ve got your head around the concept, experiment with other ways of distressing parts of the painting – for example, scraping off wet paint in places to reveal underlying colours, scratching into dry paint with a knife tip, rubbing the canvas with sandpaper and throwing on random drips and spatters.
