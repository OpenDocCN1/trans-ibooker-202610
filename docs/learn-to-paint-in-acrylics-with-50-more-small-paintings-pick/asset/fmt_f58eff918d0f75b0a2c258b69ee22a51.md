
### Cube

#### 1
Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Unbleached Titanium mixed with a little glazing medium. Mix Yellow Ochre with a tiny bit of Burnt Sienna and, using a #1 round brush, draw the edges of the cube. Remember that the vertical lines will be parallel to each other, as will the slanting lines of the top and bottom edges. Put in a single line to indicate the cocktail stick at the top.

![Images](images/f0080-03.jpg)

#### 2
Using a #4 filbert, apply stripes of mid-toned colour for the fillings, matching the angle of the top and bottom edges: Cadmium Yellow Medium + Ultramarine Blue (lettuce); Napthol Red + Unbleached Titanium (salami); Cadmium Yellow Medium (cheese). Mix Unbleached Titanium with a little Mars Black and paint the front (darkest) plane, using a #6 flat brush. Use this grey for the cocktail stick shadow; this tells us which direction the light is coming from. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0081-01.jpg)

#### 3
Now make shades (darker values) of the lettuce and salami colours by adding a tiny bit of Mars Black; use Yellow Ochre for the cheese. Apply them to the front plane with a #1 round brush. Make your lines a bit wobbly, so it looks as if the filling is oozing out.

![Images](images/f0081-02.jpg)

#### 4
Mix a bright orange from Cadmium Yellow Medium, a small amount of Napthol Red and a little Unbleached Titanium. Using a #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the sandwich as you do so. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0081-03.jpg)

#### 5
Using a #4 bright brush, paint the brightly lit top of the sandwich in Titanium White with some glazing medium. Add a little Unbleached Titanium to this mix for the left-hand side: this plane needs to look slightly darker than the top in order to look convincingly three-dimensional. Using the #1 round brush and the grey mix from step 2, add shadows under the mid-toned fillings on the left plane. Using the #6 flat brush, put in the cast shadow with a mix of Napthol Red, Mars Black and Burnt Sienna.

![Images](images/f0081-04.jpg)

A cube is a great shape for your first foray into painting three-dimensional subjects, as you can concentrate on getting the values right without having to worry about painting a complicated shape. Other cube shapes that you could try are a pile of books or a plate of chocolate brownies.

### 23 Reflective surfaces: Pawn

Surfaces that are highly reflective, such as polished wood, hard plastic, glass and metal, will have reflections that are very crisp and distinct. Highlights, too, will be sharp-edged and bright: look at the left-hand side of the pawn, for example, where light hits the object directly. In this project, the tabletop on which the pawn sits is also reflective: light also bounces from it on to the right-hand side of the pawn. It’s a subtle detail, but one that helps to convey the reflective nature of the materials. This project also uses a classical technique known as grisaille painting, which involves applying layers of thin, transparent grey paint over one another until subtle gradations in value emerge.

#### Materials

![Images](images/f0082-02.jpg)

#### 1
Using a #4 filbert brush and Mars Black, paint the shape of the pawn, keeping it as symmetrical as possible. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0082-03.jpg)

#### 2
Mix a thin glaze of Unbleached Titanium, a tiny bit of Yellow Ochre and plenty of glazing medium. Using a #6 flat brush, loosely brush it over the whole canvas. This glaze changes the value of the black to a mid-grey.

![Images](images/f0083-01.jpg)

#### 3
Now begin to build up the form of the pawn. With the #4 filbert brush and Mars Black, go over the facets of the pawn that are farthest away from the light. Leave a sliver of mid-grey on the right-hand edge of the pawn, where light is reflected back on to the surface.

![Images](images/f0083-02.jpg)

#### 4
Thin the Mars Black with glazing medium and carefully feather it over the left side of the pawn, where the light source hits the object directly, to make the transitions between the black surface of the pawn and the grey reflection more subtle. With the same mix, fill in the reflection on the table. The table surface is very reflective, so make the shape of the reflection crisp and sharp.

![Images](images/f0083-03.jpg)

#### 5
Mix Unbleached Titanium and Titanium White. Using the #6 flat brush, add brighter highlights on the left side of the pawn, taking care to keep the highlight shapes crisp and sharp-edged. Using the same mix, paint the wall behind the pawn, cutting out the shape of the pawn as you do so. Tighten up and refine the highlights with the edge of the #6 flat brush and Titanium White, then glaze the Unbleached Titanium and Titanium White mix over the table and the reflection to unify the piece.

![Images](images/f0083-04.jpg)

With a subject like this, pay careful attention to the shapes of the highlights, as well as to the values. Try painting the same subject again on a different surface, such as matt wood or fabric, to see what difference this makes to the way light is reflected.

### 24 Introduction to cylinders: Stack of pancakes

The next geometric shape we’re going to look at is the cylinder, which is, in effect, a series of ellipses stacked on top of each other. I’ve chosen a stack of pancakes to get you started: although they’re elliptical the edges are slightly irregular, so you have a bit more leeway when it comes to getting the shape right – they’re not as rigid as something like a bottle. When you’re painting cylinders, you can often only see the front arc of the ellipses (unless your subject is transparent, like a glass vase). But even though you’ll paint over the lines later, it helps to put in the whole of the ellipses at the start. If you’re thinking of your subject as a three-dimensional shape from the get-go, you’ll have a clearer sense of its roundness and where the light begins to fall away.

#### Materials

![Images](images/f0084-02.jpg)

#### 1
Mix Yellow Ochre with glazing medium. Using a #1 round brush, draw two ellipses, one on top of the other, more or less touching and as close in shape and size as possible. Connect them at the outside edges with straight vertical lines. Even though we can’t see the back arc of the bottom ellipse, it helps to put it in at the start so that you get a sense of the cylinder as a whole.

![Images](images/f0084-03.jpg)

#### 2
Mix the base colour of the pancakes from Unbleached Titanium, Yellow Ochre and a little Cadmium Yellow Medium. Using a #4 filbert brush, fill in the whole of the front shape. Again, you should be thinking about the volume of the cylinder as a whole, even though you’ll break it up later. The edges don’t have to be precise – they’re not that even and regular in real life.

![Images](images/f0085-01.jpg)

#### 3
Mix the brown ‘cooked’ colour of the pancakes from Yellow Ochre, Burnt Sienna, Unbleached Titanium and a tiny bit of Napthol Red. Using the #4 filbert, paint the top of the stack with the broad side of the brush; paint the contour lines that separate the pancakes with the brush turned on its side. Make sure the contour lines follow the arc of the original ellipse. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0085-02.jpg)

#### 4
Mix Unbleached Titanium with a little Cadmium Yellow Medium and paint the pat of butter with a #4 bright brush. For the shaded right-hand side of the stack, mix Unbleached Titanium, Mars Black, a little Burnt Sienna and Napthol Red, and a touch of Ultramarine Blue to cool it down, plus quite a lot of glazing medium to make it semi-transparent; you need to be able to see a hint of the contour lines underneath. Apply with the #4 filbert brush.

![Images](images/f0085-03.jpg)
