
#### 4
Mix violet from Napthol Red, Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White. Using a #1 round brush, apply short, directional brushstrokes for the fluffy-textured flower head, adding more Ultramarine to the mix for the cooler bottom half and more Napthol Red for the warmer top half. Again, leave some of the Yellow Ochre showing through, both to convey the individual petals and to increase the sense of luminosity.

![Images](images/f0135-03.jpg)

#### 5
Darken the base of the thistle flowers with a stronger purple mix. With a mix of Cadmium Yellow Medium and Unbleached Titanium, establish a few more thistle spikes pushing out into the background to add to the feeling of luminosity.

![Images](images/f0135-04.jpg)

Detail in the subject itself is subdued, but the halo effect of light shining around the edge of the subject creates a lovely atmospheric painting.

## 49 Creating a sense of scale: Canyon vista

We’re all familiar with the basic principle of linear perspective – the way that things appear smaller the farther away they are – but there are other ways of conveying a sense of scale and distance. The first is colour temperature: objects in the foreground appear warmer and more intense than those in the distance. In this scene, for example, the foreground oranges and blues are pumped up and highly saturated, while those in the background are cooler and paler. The second is that texture and detail become less pronounced with distance. This project is also about abstracting a complex scene into a much simpler pattern. Even though the subject is a vast panorama, you can reduce it to basic shapes and blocks of colour, even moving towards abstraction if you so choose.

### Materials

![Images](images/f0136-02.jpg)

#### 1
Mix a warm orange from Napthol Red, a little Cadmium Yellow Medium and tiny bit of Yellow Ochre. Using a #4 filbert brush, apply it over the land area, leaving the sky area untouched. Work from top to bottom, adding more Napthol Red as you move towards the foreground. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0136-03.jpg)

#### 2
Repeat the process, using blue for the shadow areas of the canyon walls. For the foreground shadow, add Burnt Sienna to Ultramarine Blue to neutralize and darken it. The shaded canyon walls in the left foreground are pure Ultramarine. As you work back add Unbleached Titanium, progressively lightening and cooling the colour. This has the effect of pushing the cliffs back in the scene. Add Titanium White for the sky area.

![Images](images/f0137-01.jpg)

#### 3
Mix a purple-pink from Napthol Red, a little Cadmium Yellow Medium and some Unbleached Titanium. Still using the #4 filbert brush, scumble this over part of the middle distance, varying the proportions of the colours in the mix.

![Images](images/f0137-02.jpg)

#### 4
For the trees, mix a dark green from Mars Black and Cadmium Yellow Medium, adding more black for the shadow sides. Glaze thinned down Burnt Sienna over the foreground shadow to warm it up a bit. Build up form on the foreground canyon with the orange mix from step 1, adding Unbleached Titanium for the most brightly lit parts.

![Images](images/f0137-03.jpg)

#### 5
Using a #1 round brush, put in some small trees in the middle distance to help create a sense of scale. However, it’s important not to get caught up in too much detail, especially on such a small canvas. Refine the shadow areas, using the same blue mixes as in step 2 and making the foreground shadows warmer, darker and more saturated.

![Images](images/f0137-04.jpg)

Practise painting perspective with other panoramic vistas, such as an Alpine mountain range, where the colour contrasts are likely to be less extreme than in this canyon scene. Then try the same approach with a flatter, but equally expansive, landscape such as an African plain.

## 50 Tonalism: Landscape with setting sun

Tonalism was an art movement that emerged in the US in the 1870s, and was characterized by muted and limited colour palettes. The tonalists were interested in capturing the effects of light – particularly moonlight or the diffused light of dawn or dusk. Large areas that are very close in value are set against small areas of higher contrast. Hazy, atmospheric landscapes were the most common subject matter. In this scene, the land forms a stark silhouette – although if you look closely you will see that it is not a solid black: instead, warm neutrals (browns, greys, green-tinged darks) intermingle. The Yellow Ochre underpainting imparts an overall glow and the small area of the setting sun and its reflection in the river below cut through the surrounding dark tones like a knife.

### Materials

![Images](images/f0138-02.jpg)

#### 1
Mix Yellow Ochre with a tiny bit of Burnt Sienna and glazing medium. Using a #6 flat brush, roughly brush this colour over the whole canvas and let dry. Now mix Burnt Sienna and a touch of Yellow Ochre with glazing medium. Using a #4 filbert brush, paint the silhouette of the horizon line and trees. Keep your brushstrokes very loose and textural.

![Images](images/f0138-03.jpg)

#### 2
Over the top two-thirds of the canvas, apply a mix of Yellow Ochre, Burnt Sienna and a little Mars Black with the #4 filbert, leaving space where the setting sun will be. Add the same colour to the reflection of the sky in the river, leaving space in the centre where the setting sun will be reflected. Also put a little bit of the colour into the trees.

![Images](images/f0139-01.jpg)

#### 3
Paint over the land in a mix of Mars Black with small amounts of Burnt Sienna and Cadmium Yellow Medium, to shift the colour towards green, using loose, vertical strokes to echo the direction in which the trees and grasses grow. Mix a bluish grey from Mars Black, Unbleached Titanium, Ultramarine Blue and Yellow Ochre and brush this over the top part of the sky and into the reflection. Note how this grey is very similar in value to the dark olive mix from step 2.

![Images](images/f0139-02.jpg)

#### 4
Continue to darken the land and refine the sky; note how the trees on the horizon line now appear backlit and contrast dramatically with the glow of the evening sky. In terms of value, the top two-thirds of the canvas form a unified, harmonious space that will contrast with the setting sun and its reflection.

![Images](images/f0139-03.jpg)

#### 5
Continue to develop the sky with the same colours as before, smoothing out the paint but still keeping some texture. For the sun and its reflection, mix Titanium White with tiny amounts of Cadmium Yellow Medium and Yellow Ochre, and apply with a #1 round brush.

![Images](images/f0139-04.jpg)

Experiment with the position of the horizon line, moving it up or down and seeing what effect that has on the mood. You could also try changing the colour temperature, perhaps by making the clouds warmer (by adding Napthol Red, for example), but avoid too much saturated colour.

## Glossary

### Acrylic
1. A polymer resin (type of plastic) derived from petrochemicals that takes many forms, including Perspex sheets and ‘woolly’ sweaters. 2. A type of water-soluble paint where pigments are suspended in an acrylic polymer resin binder that gives the paint workability and adhesion. It dries to a hard, durable, waterproof plastic film.

### Alla prima
A painting made all in one go, without underpainting.

### Canvas
1. A textile, which may be cotton, linen, hemp, sacking, calico or sailcloth, that is stretched over a frame to make a surface to paint on. 2. Any surface to which paint is applied: textile, wood, board. 3. The painting as a work in progress.

### Chroma
Chromatic colour, colourful, made of colour. Describes the intensity of a hue, and the saturation of pigment.

### Complementaries
Colours opposite each other on the colour wheel. Placed next to each other, each has maximum intensity; mixed together, they cancel each other out.
