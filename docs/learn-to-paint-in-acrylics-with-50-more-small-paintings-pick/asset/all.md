It’s been several years since the initial printing of my first book, *Learn to Paint in Acrylics with 50 Small Paintings*. Since then, I’ve received—almost daily—messages in my email inbox from readers who have praised the first book, criticized it, asked for clarification, wondered about materials, celebrated a return to painting after a long drought, and asked permission to use the book for various unexpected reasons (one reader used the book to help rehabilitate patients following hand surgeries, which made me very happy).

Each of these exercises is designed to be executed in under an hour and ready to share (whether in the virtual or physical world) instantly. The subjects are whimsical and intended to appeal to children, parents, grandparents, and anyone else interested in embarking on a fun-filled journey learning the difficult skill of painting in acrylics. My hope is that you will approach these 50 small paintings with an open mind and an eager spirit—one small painting at a time.

## Materials, tools and techniques

Before getting started on the 50 paintings, it is important to explore the options for tools and materials as well as to consider essential principles about colour and design. This chapter will introduce you to the basics of acrylic paint, brushes, surfaces and the equipment you will need to complete the projects illustrated in this book.

![Images](images/f0012-01.jpg)

### Acrylic paint

There are myriad formulations of acrylic paints available to the beginner and the advanced painter alike. All acrylic paints consist of a combination of pigment (which usually begins in powder form) and binder (which holds the pigment together).

The binding agent in acrylic paint is polymer-based (i.e. plastic, as opposed to the oil used to bind oil paints). The difference in price and quality from one brand to another is based on the ratio of pigment to binder in the paint. The advantage of acrylic paint over other mediums is its rapid drying time. One significant disadvantage to using acrylics, however, is that the acrylic binder cannot hold as much pigment as paint with oil-based binders. This results in more transparent paints, better suited to layering and glazing than to thick, opaque application.

![Images](images/f0014-01.jpg)

You can buy acrylic paint in tubes, pots, bottles, markers and spray cans – the packaging will often be dependent on the viscosity.

#### HEAVY BODY VS SOFT BODY ACRYLICS

Acrylic paint comes in a wide range of viscosities to suit different artist’s preferences and needs. Most acrylic paint brands offer ‘heavy body’ and ‘soft body’ (also referred to as ‘fluid’) versions of their paints.

It may seem a bit counterintuitive, but soft body paints are slightly more opaque due to the fact that less binder is used in the formulation. Soft body paint is for thin applications, whereas heavy body paint is for thick applications, such as impasto.

![Images](images/f0014-03.jpg)

Soft body acrylic

![Images](images/f0014-04.jpg)

Heavy body acrylic

![Images](images/f0014-05.jpg)

The top stripe of each colour was painted using soft body acrylics straight from the tube with no dilution. The bottom stripe of each colour was painted using heavy body acrylics with no dilution. Notice that while neither is fully opaque even at full strength, the soft body acrylics are slightly better at covering evenly.

#### ‘STUDENT’ VS ‘PROFESSIONAL’ PAINTS

Student-grade paints are often cheaper because they contain less pigment. These paints can be useful if you are just starting out in acrylic paint. Be aware that student- (or craft-) grade paints are rather transparent and that you will have a difficult time painting light colours over dark ones (yellow over black, for example). Higher grade paints are more expensive because they contain more pigment, making them considerably more opaque.

The difference in opacity between student-grade paints and the professional-grade versions can be seen particularly clearly with Cadmium Yellow Medium and Napthol Red. However, the effect is less pronounced with Mars Black, Ultramarine Blue and Unbleached Titanium.

#### MEDIUMS AND ADDITIVES

Acrylic paint can be mixed with a variety of mediums and additives to produce different effects and finishes. The market for acrylic mediums is vast and can be overwhelming to the novice. For the projects in this book, I used satin acrylic glazing medium to thin the paint to a usable consistency. It is recommended that you use a medium rather than water to thin your paint. This is because acrylic paint is pigment suspended in a binder (which is a medium), so adding water to the paint will not only thin the pigment, but will also thin the binder. This will compromise the paint’s ability to stick to initial layers, especially after they have dried. Thinning with an acrylic medium will work better than thinning with water because it is pure binder, which will adhere to the previous layers better than water.

![Images](images/f0017-01.jpg)

**Glazing mediums:** A thin, slippery medium ideal for glazing layers of pigment. This medium will reduce the pigment load considerably.

![Images](images/f0017-02.jpg)

**Thickeners:** Gel mediums make the paint very thick. If you like textured brushstrokes, try mixing a gel medium into your paint.

![Images](images/f0017-03.jpg)

**Open mediums/retarders:** If your paint is drying faster than you are comfortable with, retarding mediums will slow the drying time, allowing you to work at a slower pace.

### Brushes

Trying to choose the ‘right’ brush from the seemingly countless offerings available at the art supply shop (or online supplier) can overwhelm even the most seasoned painter.

For this book…

#### CHOOSING YOUR BRUSHES

The variety of material, shape, size and price can make choosing your brushes quite difficult. Even though there is a handful of objective characteristics to keep in mind when deciding on your brushes, in the end you should choose brushes that suit your personal taste. How the brush feels in your hand, the weight and balance of the brush, the length of the handle and even the price of the brush are issues to consider.

##### Stiffness vs. softness

Feel the bristles of the brush. If they are stiff, the brush will be more useful for applying thick, impasto strokes. A softer brush will absorb more paint and is ideal for applying layers of thin paint.

##### Natural vs synthetic bristles

Natural (fur taken from an animal) bristle brushes are more expensive than synthetic (manufactured) bristle brushes. Acrylic paint can damage brushes faster than other mediums due to the need to clean the brushes more often, so more durable synthetic bristles are generally the better option.

##### Brush size

Large brushes are useful when covering large areas; small brushes are better for fine-detail work. A good medium-sized brush will often replace the need for either large or small brushes.

##### Student vs professional grade

Most art supply shops carry different grades of paintbrush. ‘Student’ brushes are less expensive but also less durable. ‘Professional’ brushes will last many years with proper cleaning and care.

##### Price

It’s best to buy a small number of quality brushes and take good care of them rather than buy many low-quality brushes, which will only last a few projects each. If you are just starting out with painting, inexpensive materials are tempting, but you will end up replacing them more often, negating the savings.

![Images](images/f0018-01.jpg)

A brush roll enables you to carry your essential brushes without damaging them and easily view and access them when painting on the go.

![Images](images/f0019-01.jpg)

#### PROPER CARE OF BRUSHES

Use a water jar with a grate inside to clean your brush completely when you are waiting for a layer to dry or taking a break. Acrylic paint will dry very quickly on a brush that has not been cleaned and the brush will be ruined for future use. Also, do not leave the brush sitting in water for long periods of time as this will damage the brush shape and possibly loosen the ferrule from the shaft. When you have finished painting for the day, wash your brush with brush soap and remove any pigment left in the bristles. If the brush has lost its shape, apply a generous amount of brush restorer (available at art supply shops) and reshape the brush until the next painting session.

### Palettes

Perhaps the most useful feature of acrylic paint is its ability to dry to a hard, durable finish in a matter of minutes. This can be helpful in most cases, but the fast drying time also creates a problem – the paint you’ve squeezed out on your palette often dries before you can use it. One solution to this problem is to use a wet pad or sponge as a surface for mixing and storing your paint.

![Images](images/f0020-01.jpg)

My favourite palette is the Acryl-a-Miser palette system. It comes with many compartments for holding paint and the lid is very easy to open and close.

#### CHOOSING YOUR PALETTE

Palettes come in a wide variety of materials, shapes and sizes to suit every artist’s preference. You can buy one specifically manufactured for use with acrylic paint, or you can simply reuse an old dinner plate or recycle food packaging (such as plastic yoghurt lids) as a palette.

![Images](images/f0020-02.jpg)

Small circular palette

![Images](images/f0020-03.jpg)

Plastic palette with lid and sponge

![Images](images/f0020-04.jpg)

Palette paper

#### IMPROVISED PALETTE

To make your own palette, place 3–4 kitchen sponges underneath and on either side of an ice cube tray inside a small plastic container. Pour enough water over the sponges so that they are damp but there is no standing water at the bottom of the container. Fill the ice cube tray with your colours. Spray the palette periodically with water to keep the paint from drying out and cover the container with a lid when not in use. One drawback is that the sponges and paint can become mouldy if left closed for too long. A tablespoon of ammonia applied to one of the sponges will remedy this.

![Images](images/f0021-01.jpg)

This homemade palette is very inexpensive and with minimal attention the paint can be kept wet for weeks and even months at a time.

### Choosing a surface

The most popular choice for a painting surface when using acrylics is a pre-stretched, acrylic-primed canvas purchased from an art supply shop. There are, however, many alternatives to the traditional approach of painting on a prepared canvas. Acrylic paint manufacturers produce a wide array of products for use in preparing surfaces such as glass, metal and cloth.

For this book…

For the purposes of this book, I suggest preparing your own surfaces. This approach is much less expensive than buying a large number of canvases and it will allow you to experiment without fear of wasting money if something does not work out the way you had hoped.

If you decide to prepare your own surface for painting, there are many options available. The simplest involves applying acrylic gesso to your surface (there is also an oil-based gesso, so be sure the label says ‘acrylic gesso’). More expensive gesso will cover the surface area more quickly; less expensive gesso will require multiple layers.

While it is possible to paint directly on to paper, you will find that doing so leads to warping and buckling if the paper is not mounted to a stable surface. If you like the texture of paper, but want the rigidity of surface that a board provides, you can mount paper or canvas to a hard surface using ph-neutral glue. Mounting the paper will make your painting surface more durable but will also add weight, which may be a consideration if you need to transport the paintings when they are finished.

![Images](images/f0022-01.jpg)

**Mounting canvas or paper to a panel**  
**1** To mount canvas or paper on a panel, begin by applying ph-neutral glue to the entire surface of the panel using a small foam paint roller. This will adhere the paper or canvas to the panel as well as protect them from any acidity that may be present in the panel material.

![Images](images/f0022-02.jpg)

**2** Press the panel firmly on top of a piece of primed canvas or paper (the glue will stick to the unprimed side).

![Images](images/f0022-03.jpg)

**3** Trim the edges of the canvas or paper with a craft knife and allow to dry for an hour before painting.

**Priming with acrylic gesso**  
To prime a panel with acrylic gesso, simply spread the gesso evenly with a brush or foam paint pad. Wait about 10 minutes and add a second coat. The surface will be ready to paint when dry to the touch (after about 15 minutes).

![Images](images/f0023-02.jpg)

**Toning the canvas**  
In many of the projects, the initial step involves ‘toning’ the canvas/surface with a primary layer of acrylic paint. This helps to unify the colour and value of subsequent layers but, more importantly, it acts as a primer, sealing the absorbent surface to a degree and allowing the following brushstrokes to appear clear and direct.

### Transferring your drawing

If you are unaccustomed to sketching the initial outlines of your painting with a brush (a method used in nearly every project in this book), you may prefer to transfer the image directly to your canvas/panel/paper before starting with the brush. There are three ways of doing this: using graphite, using a grid system or using a projector.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Graphite || • Easy to do• Accurate || • May destroy original image• Leaves a slight indentation in the paper/panel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grid system || • Easy to get accurate image without damaging the canvas/panel || • Time consuming• Destroys original image |
| Projector || • Easy to achieve accuracy of line and placement of image on canvas || • Projection equipment can be expensive• It requires some expertise to operate |

![Images](images/f0024-01.jpg)

**Using graphite**  
**1** Apply graphite to the back of your pencil drawing. Alternatively, you can buy graphite paper from an art supply shop and sandwich it between your drawing and your support.

![Images](images/f0024-02.jpg)

**2** Place the drawing right-side up on your support and trace over the linework – the graphite will transfer on to your support, leaving an outline.

![Images](images/f0024-03.jpg)![Images](images/f0025-01.jpg)

**Using a grid system**  
**1** Lightly draw a grid over your source image, then draw a grid with the same number of squares on your support.

![Images](images/f0025-02.jpg)

**2** Alternatively, if you don’t want to draw on your original sketch or photograph, you can draw a grid with black marker on a piece of glass and place that over your source image. Carefully copy each square to correspond with the same square on the original image.

![Images](images/f0025-03.jpg)

Don’t be afraid to sketch the initial outline of your paintings freehand with your brush – it will add to the spontaneity of the finished painting.

![Images](images/f0025-04.jpg)

**Using a projector**  
Use an opaque, transparent or digital projector to project your drawing on to the canvas/panel/paper and lightly trace the linework. You can make copies or adaptations of your paintings in the same way, tracing the basic framework of the composition. Make sure your painting support is at a perfectly perpendicular 90-degree angle to avoid a skewed or distorted projection.

### Colour and value

The relationship between colour and value is perhaps the most important (and often the most elusive) concept for the painter to grasp. To put it simply, some colours are light, some are dark and some sit between light and dark.

#### COLOUR

The colour wheel is a device artists have used for hundreds of years to help better organize the colours they use in paintings. Although nature contains infinite variations of colour, it is helpful to limit the range of colours you use to keep your paintings unified and harmonious. The practice of using limited groups of colour is often referred to as a ‘colour scheme’. Here are a few of the most common colour schemes.

##### Colour wheel

The colour wheel is a helpful tool for choosing groups of colours as you work.

![Images](images/f0026-01.jpg)

##### Split-complementary

Similar to complementary except instead of using opposite colours, use two colours next to one of the opposing colours. Blue with red-orange and yellow-orange, for example.

![Images](images/f0026-02.jpg)

##### Analogous

Using colours close to one another on the colour wheel: limiting the colour range to include only red-orange, orange and yellow-orange, for example.

![Images](images/f0026-03.jpg)

##### Triad

A triad colour scheme utilizes three colours of equal distance from one another on the colour wheel. Red, blue and yellow would make up a ‘primary triad’. Green, orange and purple would make up a ‘secondary triad’.

![Images](images/f0026-04.jpg)

##### Complementary

Using colours at opposite sides of the colour wheel: yellow and purple, for example.

![Images](images/f0026-05.jpg)

##### Tetrad

A tetrad is a rather complicated colour scheme to manage. In it, you choose two colours from one side of the colour wheel and combine those colours with two from the opposite side, making a square or rectangle on the colour wheel. For example, red and orange with green and blue.

![Images](images/f0026-06.jpg)

#### VALUE

If you learned how to draw using only a pencil or pen, then you are accustomed to thinking in terms of value: light and dark. When speaking about colour, the term ‘value’ refers to the relative lightness or darkness of a colour. For example, yellow has a light value, purple has a dark value. A useful technique for comparing the values of different colours is to squint your eyes when looking at them. If the boundary between the colours disappears, they have the same value. Identifying the value of a colour is essential when mixing your paint, particularly when it is necessary to simplify a complicated scene. By reducing the number of values in a painting, you will make a painting that is more cohesive and harmonious.

![Images](images/f0027-01.jpg)

These colours are easily identified as violet, orange and blue.

![Images](images/f0027-02.jpg)

When the colour is removed from the shapes, it becomes clear that the orange and blue are nearly identical in value.

![Images](images/f0027-03.jpg)

Converting hues to black and white reveals their values (row 4). It’s often easier to judge the values of different hues against a mid-value ground (row 2) rather than a pale (row 1) or dark-value one (row 3).

### Basic design principles

When creating a painting, it is common for the artist to focus so much attention on rendering an object or a scene that the idea of designing a cohesive composition never enters his or her mind. In order to make pleasing, cohesive arrangements, it is important to pay attention to a few basic principles when designing your paintings.

#### UNITY

To create cohesive, coherent paintings, unify the elements with shared attributes. By using similar colours, shapes and even brushstrokes, all of the parts of your painting will work together to create a unified whole.

![Images](images/f0028-01.jpg)![Images](images/f0028-02.jpg)

The painting is unified by the limited colour palette of warm, muted neutrals (browns, greys, green-tinged darks) and the loose, vertical brushstrokes.

#### HARMONY

Harmony is a very similar concept to unity, with the exception that you are looking for elements that complement (rather than match) one another. Choose colours that enhance one another as a group.

![Images](images/f0028-03.jpg)![Images](images/f0028-04.jpg)

The yellow, ochre and greens of the lemon branch are unified; with the addition of violet the group is harmonious.

#### CONTRAST

If your goal is to create dramatic compositions, this can be achieved by exaggerating the contrast between either the values or the colours. For dramatic tonal contrast, use light against dark. For vibrant colour contrast, use colours from opposite sides of the colour wheel.

![Images](images/f0028-05.jpg)![Images](images/f0028-06.jpg)

The warm rusty orange of the padlock is a vibrant contrast to the cool blue of the door. The dramatic shadow adds tonal contrast.

#### FOCAL POINT

Perhaps the most important thing to consider when making a painting is to have a ‘focal point’, which refers to the most important piece of the painting. All other parts of the painting are arranged to be subordinate to the focal point.

![Images](images/f0029-01.jpg)![Images](images/f0029-02.jpg)

The lighthouse with its shining beacon is the focal point.

#### BALANCE

Use pleasing relationships and proportions to create a sense of calm balance. Balance in composition can be either symmetrical (equal) or asymmetrical (unequal). Symmetrical balance means each part is equally weighted with the centre of the canvas. Asymmetrical balance involves arranging the parts in a less rigid, more rhythmic manner.

![Images](images/f0029-03.jpg)![Images](images/f0029-04.jpg)

The sky occupies a third of the image. The wheat field is two-thirds of the painting.

#### DOMINANCE

One of the most common examples of using the principle of dominance is in choosing a placement for the horizon line. To place it exactly in the middle of your canvas divides the painting into equal parts, which usually creates an uncomfortable tension. Move the horizon line up to allow the land to dominate, or down to allow the sky to dominate. This principle also applies to colour (dominant hue), value (dominant key), shape (dominant object) and so on.

![Images](images/f0029-05.jpg)![Images](images/f0029-06.jpg)

With the horizon line near the bottom of the painting, the clouded sky becomes more dominant.

#### REPETITION

To move the viewer’s eye through the painting, simply repeat similar shapes, colours and so on. Since most people read from left to right, it is helpful to place the object you are repeating to the left of the composition and repeat it as you move to the right.

![Images](images/f0029-07.jpg)![Images](images/f0029-08.jpg)

The orange colour of the cliffs repeats throughout, as do the shadow shapes of the canyon walls.

### Displaying your work

As you progress through the projects in this book, you may notice that the finished paintings are designed to hang together in groups. By keeping the size of the paintings consistent, there is a built-in similarity that will coordinate the paintings when they are displayed. Spend some time exploring unconventional relationships between the paintings and arranging them in interesting and unexpected combinations.

#### FRAMING

If you choose to work on a surface (such as paper or unstretched canvas) that cannot be displayed without framing, you’ll need to consider using either a mount or a frame or some combination of the two.

##### Maths

Paintings on paper need to be separated from the glass by an airspace. The mount provides this, and also enlarges the clear space around the artwork. It’s common practice to make the bottom edge 1/2in (12mm) wider than the top and sides to correct an optical illusion when all are the same. Make sure to use acid-free boards. Choose pale, neutral shades; ivory is classic. Avoid unusual colours that may overpower the painting.

##### Frames

If you choose to execute your paintings on a three-dimensional surface (such as a stretched canvas or MDF panel), you may want to display the artwork in a conventional frame. Most art supply shops offer at least a limited selection of designs to choose from and some offer custom framing services. Generally speaking, small, simple paintings will look best in simple frames. Ornate and overly decorative frames are likely to distract from the painting and often cost more as well. If you do not live near an art supply shop or a speciality frame shop, many online suppliers offer a wide range of frame designs to choose from. Most suppliers will offer a substantial discount if you order multiple frames at the same time.

#### HANGING THEMES

While it is perfectly acceptable to hang your paintings in a random order, you may also want to arrange the paintings according to specific themes. A few suggestions – colour, shape and subject – are shown opposite.

![Images](images/f0030-01.jpg)

A thin mount will give your painting a sleek, modern look.

![Images](images/f0030-02.jpg)

A medium-sized mount will not distract from the painting.

![Images](images/f0030-03.jpg)

A large mount with offset centre will focus attention on your painting.

##### Colour

Look for similar colour schemes to get a unified group of paintings. You may want to arrange a group of yellow objects, or use the analogous blue, violet and red colour scheme shown here.

![Images](images/f0031-01.jpg)![Images](images/f0031-02.jpg)![Images](images/f0031-03.jpg)

##### Shape

Find repeating/similar shapes for unexpected groupings. Look for symmetrical objects with round shapes or perhaps a grouping of square shapes.

![Images](images/f0031-04.jpg)![Images](images/f0031-05.jpg)![Images](images/f0031-06.jpg)

##### Subject

Group the paintings by subject. Landscapes, birds, food, toys, art materials, objects and plants are a few of the repeating themes in this book.

![Images](images/f0031-07.jpg)![Images](images/f0031-08.jpg)![Images](images/f0031-09.jpg)

#### HANGING CONFIGURATIONS

Because the paintings are presented as a group, it is helpful to arrange the paintings according to a consistent pattern.

![Images](images/f0032-01.jpg)

Hanging your paintings in a plain frame will not detract from the artwork.

##### Line

Simply hang the paintings at the same height with equal distance between each painting. This has the benefit of showing the work in a progression. It can be difficult to get all of the paintings to align properly, so stand back to check this as you hang them.

![Images](images/f0032-02.jpg)

##### Grid

Hang the paintings in grids of 2 x 2, 3 x 3, 4 x 4 and so on. It is easy to show a large group of paintings in a small space if you hang them as a grid. Because the paintings from this book are quite small, it is possible to hang a large number of them in a small space by using a grid. Make accurate measurements for the hooks/nails to ensure the paintings line up vertically and horizontally.

![Images](images/f0032-03.jpg)

##### Random

Hanging the paintings in a haphazard, random arrangement will give the group a less formal and perhaps more ‘creative’ appearance. This will work especially well in a casual environment such as a coffee house or your own studio.

![Images](images/f0032-04.jpg)

#### HANGING METHODS

Once you have decided on your hanging theme and configuration, there are various hardware options for attaching your framed artwork, panel or canvas to the wall.

Hanging the work without frames will present your paintings in a more informal manner. And you don’t even have to hang them: simply prop the canvas against the wall on the floor or on a shelf.

##### Canvas and nail

A simple option for hanging is to paint all of your projects on small stretched canvases and hang the stretcher bar on a nail.

![Images](images/f0033-02.jpg)

## Putting on the paint

In this chapter, you will be introduced to the primary techniques needed to paint in acrylics through a series of progressively challenging projects. You will start with basic paint application exercises and slowly add new skills with each project. By the end of the chapter, you will have acquired the fundamentals necessary for painting in acrylics.

![Images](images/f0034-01.jpg)

### 1 Basic paint application:  
Door

This project is all about getting used to the feel of the paint and how long it takes to dry. I’ve chosen a simple scene of a coloured door on a plain-coloured wall, so you won’t need to worry about creating complicated shapes or colours running together. There isn’t even any colour mixing involved. Use this project to experiment with different ratios of pigment to glazing medium: if you want a thick coverage, add more pigment and/or apply more layers; if you want the paint to look thinner and more transparent, so that you can actually see the scratchy brushstrokes and create some texture in your painting, use more medium and apply only one layer. Varying the amount of pressure you exert on the brush will also help you see what textures you can create.

Materials

![Images](images/f0036-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with a layer of Unbleached Titanium. Allow to dry for 10 minutes or so, otherwise the red paint in the next step will merge with the damp white and you’ll end up with a pink door. Clean and dry your brush; you need to do this each time you use a brush with a new colour, unless you want to create a colour mixture with the old colour.

![Images](images/f0036-03.jpg)

**2** In the centre of the canvas paint a rectangle of Napthol Red straight from the tube, using the #6 flat brush. The shape of the brush makes it easy to paint straight edges and sharp corners. I applied two layers for good opacity; one layer would give a more transparent finish. Let the paint dry between layers. It doesn’t matter if the second layer isn’t dry before the next step, as you won’t be touching it with any other colour.

![Images](images/f0037-01.jpg)

**3** Thin Yellow Ochre paint with glazing medium and apply it to the wall, leaving the lintel around the door unpainted as a ‘negative shape’ – a technical term meaning the empty space around an object. I only used one layer of paint here, so that the brushstrokes would be visible and create some texture on the wall.

![Images](images/f0037-02.jpg)

**4** Mix Burnt Sienna with a little glazing medium and apply a long, thin line to indicate the ground under the door, again leaving a space.

![Images](images/f0037-03.jpg)

**5** Thin Burnt Sienna with glazing medium. Using a #1 round brush, paint the shadows cast by the lintel on the wall and around the top and left-hand side of the door. Use the same colour to paint the door knob. Congratulations on completing your first acrylic painting.

![Images](images/f0037-04.jpg)

With just three colours and two brushes, you can create a simple but graphic-looking painting. Try it again with a different colour combination – perhaps a Cadmium Yellow Medium door with Yellow Ochre shadows and an Ultramarine Blue wall.

### 2 Mixing tints and shades:  
Envelope

This exercise explores making lighter and darker versions of colours. The technical terms for these lighter and darker versions are ‘tints’ and ‘shades’. A tint is a colour that’s been lightened; in acrylics, that’s done by adding white or another light colour such as yellow. A shade is a colour that’s been darkened; in acrylics, that’s usually done by adding black, although blue or brown can also be used. We’ll come back to this later in the book, when we look at how light affects objects and how you can use tints and shades to make things look three-dimensional. For now, while you’re still getting used to handling acrylic paint and seeing what each brush can do, we’re mixing a tint and a shade of just one colour – Napthol Red – to paint a very flat subject made up almost entirely of straight edges.

Materials

![Images](images/f0038-02.jpg)

**1** Mix a salmon pink colour from equal amounts of Napthol Red and Unbleached Titanium. This is a tint – a lighter version of the Napthol Red. Using a #6 flat brush, paint a border about 11/2 brushstrokes wide around the edge of the canvas, leaving the centre white. Allow to dry, and remember to clean and dry your brush each time you use a new tint, shade or colour.

![Images](images/f0038-03.jpg)

**2** Continuing with the #6 flat brush, apply Napthol Red straight from the tube over the border, leaving a sliver of the pink around the inside showing through. This underlying pink helps harmonize the painting. You may need two coats to get a flat, opaque finish; apply just one coat if you want a more textured effect.

![Images](images/f0039-01.jpg)

**3** Go back to the original salmon pink colour from step 1. Go over the envelope, leaving a shape in the centre unpainted to define the interior. The curved shapes at the bottom of the white area indicate where the flap folds down. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0039-02.jpg)

**4** Using pure Napthol Red again, paint around the outside of the envelope to get crisp, clean edges and create the sloping sides of the envelope flap. Using the tip of the brush, paint the sharp line of the paperclip; I used the top edge of the brush for the straight lines and the corner tip for the curve.

![Images](images/f0039-03.jpg)

**5** Mix a very pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and a tiny amount of Mars Black. Using a #1 round brush, paint the small cast shadows on the white interior of the envelope. Now mix a dark red from Napthol Red with a little bit of Mars Black. This is a shade. Using the #1 round brush, paint the shadows on the pink part of the envelope at the top of the curved flaps and along the right-hand edges of the paperclip. Use the same colour for the cast shadows along the bottom and right-hand edges of the envelope.

![Images](images/f0039-04.jpg)

Hard to believe this is made up of simple mixes of Napthol Red and one other colour, isn’t it? Repeat this exercise with all the other colours in your basic palette, so you get used to how much white or black you need to add.

### 3 Opaque layering:  
T-shirt

One of the great things about acrylic paint is that, because of its short drying time, you can quickly apply one layer on top of another to create opaque areas of colour. Here, white is layered on top of a strong blue background. One layer might not be enough to cover the background completely, so wait until the first layer has dried and then decide whether you want to apply a second – or even a third – layer. There are no hard-and-fast rules: with some subjects, you might want to leave some of the background showing through, but once you’re familiar with the kind of coverage you get, you can decide how many layers to apply and how thick or thin the paint needs to be. Using a simple shape and a limited palette allows you to concentrate on the technique without being distracted by complicated shapes or colour mixes.

Materials

![Images](images/f0040-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with a strong blue mixed from equal amounts of Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White, with a little glazing medium. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0040-03.jpg)

**2** To paint the body of the T-shirt, paint a rectangle of Unbleached Titanium straight from the tube in the centre of the canvas, using the #6 flat brush. Here you can see the first layer being applied; once this had dried, I applied a second, leaving some brushstrokes visible to add variety and texture. At this stage, don’t worry if the coverage isn’t completely even – you can adjust things later.

![Images](images/f0041-01.jpg)

**3** Add rectangles of Unbleached Titanium for the sleeves. As each layer dries, decide whether or not you want to add another one. I applied two layers; you might decide you need three.

![Images](images/f0041-02.jpg)

**4** Using the background blue from step 1 and the #6 flat brush, paint around the T-shirt to tidy up the edges, scooping out a shallow curve for the neckline. Allow to dry. Mix a bright yellow from Cadmium Yellow Medium and a little Unbleached Titanium and paint a sun shape (or any motif you want). Switch to a #1 round brush and pure Ultramarine Blue and put in the cast shadows. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0041-03.jpg)

**5** Mix a very pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and a tiny amount of Mars Black and apply to the shaded areas of the T-shirt with the #4 filbert brush. These pale shadows show how the fabric falls into wrinkles and folds. Let dry. Add a touch of Mars Black to pure Ultramarine Blue and paint the face on the sun. Using the #1 round brush, paint the collar in Ultramarine Blue and the label in Titanium White with Ultramarine Blue details.

![Images](images/f0041-04.jpg)

This project demonstrates the covering power of acrylics: in watercolour, it would be impossible to paint a light colour over such a strong blue without the blue showing through. Experiment to see how many layers you need to achieve this result; it will vary depending on the pigments involved.

### 4 Expanding your colour palette:  
Child’s chalk drawing

Here’s a fun project to give you some practice in colour mixing and an idea of how many different colours you can make from the few paints that you have in your basic palette. You can either look at children’s drawings for your inspiration or just start scribbling and rediscover your inner child. Here, we’re painting sticks of chalk: the tops of the chalks are brightly lit, but the sides are not, so we need to mix shades (darker versions of the colours) to paint them. As soon as you add the shaded side stroke to each chalk, it changes from being just a flat shape to something that looks three-dimensional. We’re also adding cast shadows – again mixed from just two colours in your basic palette.

Materials

![Images](images/f0042-02.jpg)

**1** Using coloured pencils, draw a goofy, childish stick figure in the centre of your canvas. Using a #6 flat brush, paint a border of Unbleached Titanium around the stick figure, leaving a central rectangle unpainted so that it looks as if the drawing is on a separate piece of paper.

![Images](images/f0042-03.jpg)

**2** Now paint the chalks. In the upper left corner, using a #4 bright brush, paint a line of Cadmium Yellow Medium one brushstroke wide for the brightly lit top side of the chalk. Below it, apply a narrower stroke of Yellow Ochre; this is a shade – a darker version of the yellow. Repeat on the right of your painting, using Napthol Red for the light top and Napthol Red plus a little Mars Black for the shaded side.

![Images](images/f0043-01.jpg)

**3** Paint the blue chalk in Ultramarine Blue and the green chalk in a mix of Cadmium Yellow Medium and Ultramarine Blue.

![Images](images/f0043-02.jpg)

**4** Now paint the shaded sides of the blue and green chalks, adding a little Mars Black to the Ultramarine Blue, and adding Ultramarine Blue to the green mix.

![Images](images/f0043-03.jpg)

**5** Mix a pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and a tiny amount of Mars Black. Using a #1 round brush, put in the cast shadows under the chalks and along the bottom edge of the ‘paper’. With the round brush and the colours you used for the light tops of the chalks, add a few dots to look like specks of chalk dust.

![Images](images/f0043-04.jpg)

As well as mixing colours, this project gives you the chance to practise brush control. The shapes are simple – just a single stroke using the full width of the brush for the chalks – but you’ll get a feel for how hard to press and how quickly to move the brush over the canvas.

### 5 Mixing values of grey:  
Wax crayon

In the envelope project (see page 38), we looked at mixing tints and shades. This is a continuation of that process – but instead of mixing just one light and one dark version of a colour, here we’re creating a whole range from the very lightest highlight through medium light, medium, medium dark, to dark. The technical term for this is the ‘values’ of a colour. If you find it hard to work out how light or dark to go, try looking at your subject through half-closed eyes – or even take a photo on your phone and convert it to black and white. Concentrate on the different proportions of the pigments in your mix. You might find it helpful to keep a note of this, alongside swatches of your colour mixes.

Materials

![Images](images/f0044-02.jpg)

**1** Mix equal amounts of Unbleached Titanium and Mars Black. Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with a layer of this mixture. Let dry. This is the mid-value. If you start from a mid-value, it makes it easier to judge how much lighter or darker you need to go. If you start from the very lightest or darkest value, which is tempting, it’s harder to make that judgement call.

![Images](images/f0044-03.jpg)

**2** Add a little more Mars Black to the mix for a medium-dark value. Using the #6 flat brush, paint a line one brushstroke wide for the paper wrapper around the crayon. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0045-01.jpg)

**3** Now add the very darkest values to the painting – pure Mars Black. Paint the stripes on the wrapper and the base of the crayon below the wrapper, using the tip and side of the #6 flat brush. Then paint the top of the crayon as a cone shape with a flat top.

![Images](images/f0045-02.jpg)

**4** Switch to the #1 round brush. Use the toned ground colour from step 1 to paint the medium-light highlights on the grey parts of the paper wrapper. Add a tiny bit of Mars Black to that mix for the highlights on the black parts of the wrapper and the crayon.

![Images](images/f0045-03.jpg)

**5** The shadow cast by the crayon needs to be darker than the grey used for the wrapper, so mix a dark value of grey that’s roughly 80% Mars Black and 20% Titanium White. Paint the shadow with a #4 bright brush; the brush profile makes it easy to get clean, straight lines. With a #1 round brush, paint a sharp highlight on the wax parts of the crayon in Titanium White. Wax is shiny, so it has a sharper highlight than the paper wrapper.

![Images](images/f0045-04.jpg)

With just five values of the same colour, you can create a painting that looks three-dimensional. Try the same exercise using all the colours in your palette to get a feel for colour mixing and assessing values – then go on to paint lots of different-coloured crayons in the same painting.

### 6 Getting to know your brushes:  
Pastry with sprinkles

You don’t need many sizes or shapes of brushes to start painting with acrylics, and in this book I’ve limited myself to just four: #6 flat, #1 round, #4 filbert and #4 bright. However, each one has slightly different characteristics: the big flat brush allows you to cover large areas quickly with broad sweeps of colour and to create straight-edged shapes; the small round is great for sharp little marks and softer edges; the filbert is good for rounded objects and more organic brushstrokes; and the bright for making short rectangular strokes and small, precisely shaped areas. This exercise gives you the chance to practise different kinds of mark making and to get used to how much paint you can load on to your brush.

Materials

![Images](images/f0046-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Unbleached Titanium mixed with a little glazing medium. Using the #6 flat brush and Yellow Ochre, paint a straight-edged border one brushstroke wide for what will be the visible area of pastry. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0046-03.jpg)

**2** Switch to a #1 round brush and outline the inner and outer edges of the visible pastry area in Burnt Sienna; the brush shape enables you to keep your brushstrokes loose and slightly wobbly, so that you create a less mechanical, more organic look. The white area in the middle of the pastry border is where the icing will go.

![Images](images/f0047-01.jpg)

**3** Continuing with the #1 round brush, paint the little folds and crinkles around the edges of the pastry in Burnt Sienna. Switch to the #4 filbert brush for the large area of icing in the middle; this brush has a rounded tip, so it’s easier to vary the shape of your marks for the curved edges of the icing. Paint the icing in Titanium White with a very, very tiny touch of Mars Black.

![Images](images/f0047-02.jpg)

**4** The edges of the pastry are more baked than the middle, so create a gradation of colour from paler yellow-brown around the icing (Yellow Ochre + Unbleached Titanium + a touch of Cadmium Yellow Medium), then mid-brown (Yellow Ochre + Cadmium Yellow Medium) and finally a darker brown (Yellow Ochre + Burnt Sienna) at the outer edges. Use the #1 round brush and blend the colour transitions on the canvas. Apply as many layers as you need.

![Images](images/f0047-03.jpg)

**5** The final stages are all about small, controlled details, so use the #1 round brush for this. On the left side of the icing, paint highlights in pure Titanium White. Paint the cast shadow on the right and bottom edges in a pale grey (Unbleached Titanium + a tiny bit of Mars Black). Paint the sprinkles in whatever colours you wish, working one colour at a time. Finally, apply a little Napthol Red top and bottom for the jam oozing out of the edges of the pastry.

![Images](images/f0047-04.jpg)

This exercise exploits the main characteristics of each brush, but you might be surprised at how many different marks you can make with just one brush. Experiment by using the full width of the brush, then turn the brush on its side or use just the tip.

### 7 Cutting out a shape:  
Toy duck

When you first start painting, it can be difficult to paint very small, tight details, partly because it takes practice to acquire sufficient brush control. Although it might seem counterintuitive, one option is to start by painting the whole canvas in a colour that’s very close to that of the main subject and then define the shape of the subject by painting the background. Artists refer to this as ‘cutting out’ or ‘carving out’ the subject from the background, or painting the ‘negative space’. So instead of worrying about painting the outline of your subject (especially small, pointed shapes like the beak and tip of the tail), look at the shapes that the background makes – the U shape between the head and the tail, for example, or the semicircle between the beak and body – and paint those. The technique is also really useful for tidying up messy or wobbly edges to your subject.

Materials

![Images](images/f0048-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with an even coat of bright yellow mixed from Cadmium Yellow Medium, a tiny bit of Unbleached Titanium and some glazing medium. Allow to dry. It’s fine to leave some texture showing through for the wood grain on the toy. Using a #1 round brush and Yellow Ochre, paint a circle for the duck’s head and an elongated egg shape for its body. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0048-03.jpg)

**2** Mix an orange-red from Napthol Red and Cadmium Yellow Medium. Using the #1 round brush, block in the beak and indicate the wheels.

![Images](images/f0049-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a pale yellow from Unbleached Titanium and a hint of Cadmium Yellow Medium. Using the #1 round brush, put in the highlights – those parts where light hits the object most strongly – on the head, back and tops of the wheels.

![Images](images/f0049-02.jpg)

**4** Mix a reddish-brown from Napthol Red, Burnt Sienna and a tiny bit of Mars Black, adding plenty of medium to the mix to make it semi-transparent. Paint the background with the #6 flat brush, bringing the brush right up to the duck to ‘cut out’ and define the shape. Apply one layer if you want some of the paint strokes to remain visible, and multiple layers for a cleaner, more opaque finish. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0049-03.jpg)

**5** Add Mars Black to the reddish-brown mix and put in the cast shadow under the duck with a #4 filbert brush. Fill in the duck with a bright yellow mixed from Cadmium Yellow Medium and Unbleached Titanium, using the #4 filbert brush. For the wheels and the white of the eye, mix a very pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and a hint of Mars Black and apply with the #1 round brush. When this is dry, paint the wheel centre in Yellow Ochre straight from the tube and the pupil of the eye in Mars Black.

![Images](images/f0049-04.jpg)

All the really precise shapes, like the pointed beak and the tip of the tail, are made by painting the background rather than the duck itself. Have a look around your home and see what other everyday items might benefit from this treatment.

### 8 Introduction to massing:  
Daisy

When you first start painting, it can be tempting to put in every single detail you can see – every leaf on a branch, every petal on a flower, every brick in a wall. The trouble with this is that you get bogged down in detail; your work can become fussy and overcomplicated and difficult for viewers to read clearly. In this exercise, you’ll learn a technique called ‘massing’. What this means is that, instead of painting lots of small, individual shapes, you look for simpler, generalized shapes that can be treated as a single unit. It’s an easy way to make your paintings more cohesive.

Materials

![Images](images/f0050-02.jpg)

**1** Mix Cadmium Yellow Medium with a little glazing medium. In the centre of the canvas, using a #1 round brush, draw a circle for the flower centre, then an outer ‘guide circle’ and a stalk. You’ll paint over the guide circle later; it’s just to show you roughly where the extremities of the petals will be.

![Images](images/f0050-03.jpg)

**2** Switch to a #4 bright brush and fill in the flower centre in Cadmium Yellow Medium, then draw in shapes for the petals, keeping some separate and allowing others to overlap. As with the guide circle in step 1, you’ll paint over most of these shapes later. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0051-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a bright blue from Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White. Using a #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the petals as you do so. Allow to dry completely.

![Images](images/f0051-02.jpg)

**4** Some petals sit above others and cast shadows; paint these shadows in a pale grey (Unbleached Titanium + a tiny bit of Mars Black) using a #4 filbert brush. Now bring your painting together by ‘massing’ the petals and joining some of them together into an overall shape, using Titanium White; I joined all the petals on the right-hand side in one big mass, which makes a more graphically satisfying image. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0051-03.jpg)

**5** Where you feel it’s necessary, cut in with the background blue colour to delineate the tips of some of the petals and add more cast shadows with the pale grey mix. Still using the #4 filbert brush, add a little Yellow Ochre to the flower centre. Mix a mid-toned green from Ultramarine Blue, Cadmium Yellow Medium and Unbleached Titanium, and paint the stem.

![Images](images/f0051-04.jpg)

Because there’s still some separation between the petals on the left-hand side, our brain fills in the missing details and interprets the right-hand side as being made up of individual petals, too – but the result is graphic and unfussy.

### 9 Textured layering:  
Red barn

This exercise explores two different ways of layering paint to create interesting textures. The first method is to scrape off paint to allow some of the colour underneath to show through – perfect for capturing the weathered appearance of an old barn that may have been painted several times over the decades. The second method – used here for the field of wheat in front of the barn – is to apply a very loose, thin underpainting, in which the brushstrokes are clearly visible, and then build up a series of short textural marks on top. Take care not to overdo things, though – the texture should enhance the subject but not dominate it.

Materials

![Images](images/f0052-02.jpg)

**1** Mix a bright blue from equal amounts of Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White and paint the sky using a #6 flat brush. Leaving a little of the white canvas showing along the horizon (you don’t have to wait for the sky to dry since the sky and land don’t touch), paint the foreground in very thin Yellow Ochre (mixed with a lot of glazing medium), using vertical brushstrokes that mimic the stems of wheat. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0052-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a dull red from Napthol Red with a little Cadmium Yellow Medium and block out the shape of the barn using a #4 bright brush. While the paint is still wet, scrape some of the paint back off with the brush to allow some of the underlying blue to show through. This gives the barn a weathered appearance. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0053-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a shadow colour for the barn from equal amounts of Napthol Red and Burnt Sienna. Using the #4 bright brush again, paint the shaded area on the underside of the roof (which you’ll add in step 4) and to the right of the building. The flat profile of the brush makes it perfect for these small, precisely shaped areas.

![Images](images/f0053-02.jpg)

**4** Still with the bright brush, using Mars Black straight from the tube, put a roof on the barn and use the corner of the brush to create the windows. Switch to a #1 round brush and paint the fence posts in Mars Black, making them slightly irregular in shape and size to give them a more rustic appearance.

![Images](images/f0053-03.jpg)

**5** Now build up the texture of the wheat with individual vertical brushstrokes, using various yellow mixes: Unbleached Titanium + Cadmium Yellow Medium in the distance; Cadmium Yellow Medium + Yellow Ochre in the middle ground; Yellow Ochre in the foreground. Vary the size of your brushstrokes, too, making them smaller nearer the barn and larger in the foreground. This helps to create a sense of scale and distance. Finally, indicate lines of wire between the fence posts with Unbleached Titanium.

![Images](images/f0053-04.jpg)

As the saying goes, ‘less is more’. The short, textural marks used for the wheat give the impression of slightly blurred grasses blowing in the wind without detracting from the main subject – the weathered old barn.

### 10 Simple composition:  
Tabletop still life

The objects in this still life are just a series of rectangles and a circle, and because they’re viewed from almost directly overhead, there’s no distortion in the shapes. This makes them very simple to draw, leaving you free to concentrate on developing opaque layers and controlling the brush. Although a still life such as this appears very simple, you still need to take your time setting it up. Do the objects you’ve chosen relate to each another in some way – by theme, colour or shape? How will you position the different elements within the picture space? Remember to look at the spaces between the objects (the negative spaces), as well as at the objects themselves.

Materials

![Images](images/f0054-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with Cadmium Yellow Medium. Using a #1 round brush, draw the outlines of the objects and indicate the areas occupied by the cast shadow of the coffee mug in Yellow Ochre thinned with a little glazing medium.

![Images](images/f0054-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a bright blue from Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White. Using the #6 flat brush, paint the tabletop background, cutting out the shapes of the objects in your composition (and mug shadow) as you do so.

![Images](images/f0055-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a dark blue-black from Ultramarine Blue, Mars Black and a tiny bit of Unbleached Titanium and paint the cast shadows under the objects. Switch brush sizes as necessary: I used a #1 round for the thin pencil shadow, and a #6 flat for the much larger mug and book shadows.

![Images](images/f0055-02.jpg)

**4** Using the #1 round brush and Titanium White, paint the book label and the white rim of the coffee mug. Mix a medium grey from Mars Black and Unbleached Titanium and put in the shadows on the pages of the book, under the label, inside the mug and on the mug handle. Add a dab of grey to the end of the pencil for the metal ferrule. Let dry, then add a highlight on the ferrule in Titanium White.

![Images](images/f0055-03.jpg)

**5** Add a tiny touch more Mars Black to the grey from step 4 and paint the book label graphics. The mug casts a crescent-shaped shadow on the coffee; paint this in Mars Black. Use a very thin mix of Burnt Sienna and Ultramarine Blue for the coffee; using a lot of glazing medium will help you achieve the swirly pattern on the coffee. Paint the pencil tip in Yellow Ochre and the pencil eraser in Napthol Red and a little Cadmium Yellow Medium. Add a dab of Mars Black to the pointed tip for the pencil lead.

![Images](images/f0055-04.jpg)

The colour scheme for this painting is a primary triad (see page 26). Repeat the exercise, using the colour wheel on page 26 to identify other possible colour schemes.

### 11 Painting wood grain:  
Artist’s palette

Layering is the perfect technique for wood grain. First, lay down a very thin, broken layer to establish the base colour of the wood; you can then ‘draw’ the wood-grain pattern on top with your brush before covering the area with another thin layer of paint through which the wood grain remains visible. To make the wood grain really stand out, paint the background using a smooth, opaque colour, cutting out the shape of the palette as you do so; you may need two or more layers for this. Another benefit of this approach is that you don’t need to worry about keeping your wood-grain markings within the confines of the object: if they spill over slightly, you can just cover them up with the thick background colour, so you can be freer with your brushstrokes.

Materials

![Images](images/f0056-02.jpg)

**1** Mix Yellow Ochre with lots of glazing medium. Using a #6 flat brush, scumble it very loosely and roughly over the canvas. When touch-dry, paint the outline of the palette and the thumbhole in thicker Yellow Ochre using the #1 round brush. In the middle of the palette, indicate the general direction of the wood grain with a few well-placed lines. Let dry completely.

![Images](images/f0056-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a very thin wash of Yellow Ochre and a little Unbleached Titanium, with lots of glazing medium. Using the #6 flat brush, wash it over the palette; the underlying lines that you applied in step 1 will show through.

![Images](images/f0057-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a dark blue from Ultramarine Blue, Unbleached Titanium and Mars Black. Using the #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the palette and thumbhole as you do so. The wood grain stands out really well against the smooth, opaque background.

![Images](images/f0057-02.jpg)

**4** For the paint daubs on the palette, use any colours you want. Apply each colour in turn with a #4 filbert brush and let dry, then apply more layers of colour to create a thick, impasto texture. ‘Impasto’ is a technical term that just means applying the paint in layers so thick that they’re raised up from the surface.

![Images](images/f0057-03.jpg)

**5** Mix a dark blue-black from Ultramarine Blue and Mars Black and put in the cast shadows around the palette and thumbhole. Paint the brush handle and the outline of the ferrule in Mars Black. Use Titanium White for the bristles and to add a line of highlight along the ferrule and handle. Paint some colour on the end of the brush.

![Images](images/f0057-04.jpg)

You don’t need to put in every single detail of the wood grain, so long as you capture the general direction and width of the lines. Woods differ from each other not only in colour, but also in their patterning, so try painting a sample square of some different types of wood.

### 12 Distressing the surface:  
Brick wall

This is a really fun, experimental project that gives you the chance to play around with both thin and opaque layers to create somewhat unpredictable, organic-looking textures. The whole point is that it’s messy and imprecise: you degrade, or distress, one part of the painting and then go over it again to refine it and bring it partway back. In the process, the different layers add both colour and texture that become an integral part of the finished painting. Once you’ve got your head around the concept, experiment with other ways of distressing parts of the painting – for example, scraping off wet paint in places to reveal underlying colours, scratching into dry paint with a knife tip, rubbing the canvas with sandpaper and throwing on random drips and spatters.

Materials

![Images](images/f0058-02.jpg)

**1** Using the #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Unbleached Titanium mixed with a lot of glazing medium, keeping the texture as rough as possible. Allow to dry completely.

![Images](images/f0058-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a brick colour from Napthol Red, Yellow Ochre and a little Unbleached Titanium. Using a #4 bright brush, paint a brick pattern over the canvas, offsetting the rows and making each brick one brushstroke wide. The brick colour doesn’t have to be exact at this stage, as you’ll be layering more paint on top of it. As the paint on the brush runs out, the dry brushstrokes will help create the crumbling texture of the bricks. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0059-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a plaster or putty colour from Unbleached Titanium, a little Napthol Red and a little Mars Black. The colour doesn’t have to be totally true to life, but you need it to be thick and opaque. Using a #6 flat brush, apply the paint over parts of the brick pattern to create areas of broken plaster.

![Images](images/f0059-02.jpg)

**4** Now you can start to have some fun. Make up thin mixes of any colour you want (although it works best with colours closest to the brick colours, such as Yellow Ochre, Cadmium Yellow Medium and Mars Black). Using an almost dry #6 flat brush, brush each one in turn randomly over the canvas, almost to the point where you can no longer distinguish the bricks. Allow each colour to dry for 5 minutes or so.

![Images](images/f0059-03.jpg)

**5** Using Unbleached Titanium and a #1 round brush, repaint the mortar between the bricks. This re-establishes the original brick pattern while leaving the broken colours from step 4 visible on the bricks themselves.

![Images](images/f0059-04.jpg)

You can repeat the process of distressing and reclaiming as often as you want, using thin glazes for the brick colours and thicker paint for the mortar and crumbling plaster. This treatment would also work well for rusty metal and weathered, peeling paint.

### 13 Simple reflections in water:  
Hot-air balloon

Reflections in water are always a popular subject. Here are a few simple tricks to help you paint them convincingly. If the water is completely still, the reflection will be a perfect mirror image of the main subject. However, it’s often more interesting if there’s some movement in the water, as this breaks up the reflection and can create some intriguing shapes and patterns. Because the reflection is broken up, you don’t need to make it as detailed as the main subject – basic smudges of colour are sufficient – and you should avoid painting reflections with hard edges. Generally speaking, colours appear more muted in water reflections – even a white cloud or snow will need to be greyed down a little in a reflection. If you use a fairly dry brush for the reflection, patches of the underlying blue will be visible; this helps to convey the impression of ripples in the water.

Materials

![Images](images/f0060-02.jpg)

**1** Mix your sky colour from Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White. Using a #6 flat brush, paint the sky in the top third of the canvas and a strip of water at the bottom. Leave space in the middle for the clouds.

![Images](images/f0060-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a much paler blue (about 90% Titanium White) and fill in the cloud shape. Add more Ultramarine to the sky colour from step 1 and put in the shape of distant hills, leaving some white space between the hills and the clouds to represent snow. (This is a good opportunity to practise mixing tints.) Let the canvas dry completely, otherwise the Yellow Ochre in the next step will turn green.

![Images](images/f0061-01.jpg)

**3** Using the #4 filbert brush, block in the shape of the balloon in Yellow Ochre mixed with a little glazing medium, letting some of the blue show through in places to make it look slightly weathered. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0061-02.jpg)

**4** While you still have Yellow Ochre on your brush, begin painting the reflection; this is just a smudge of colour, not the entire balloon shape – the little patches of blue jutting into the reflection break up the shape and convey the effect of rippling water. Mix a pale yellow from Titanium White and a little Cadmium Yellow Medium. Paint the brightly lit side of the balloon and add the same colour to the reflection.

![Images](images/f0061-03.jpg)

**5** Add more Titanium White to the yellow mix from step 4 and paint the highlight on the upper left side of the balloon. Add a touch of this colour to the reflection. Switch to the #4 bright brush and use Mars Black to paint the balloon’s basket and the silhouettes of the people in it. Repeat in the reflection. Mix Napthol Red with a little Mars Black and paint the stripe on the balloon, then introduce that colour to the reflection. Using the #4 filbert brush, paint the brightest highlight on the balloon with pure Titanium White. Don’t add this to the more muted reflection.

![Images](images/f0061-04.jpg)

It might be tempting to include the whole of a reflection in your painting, but that would give it as much importance as the main subject. A hint is often all you need.

### 14 Rough and smooth surfaces:  
Egg on toast

This exercise gives you the chance to explore two very different textures in the same painting – the rough surface of a piece of toast and the smooth, slightly shiny surface of a fried egg. For rough surfaces, you can exploit the texture of the canvas itself, as well as the various textural techniques at your disposal, such as scrubbing the paint on unevenly and varying the direction of your brushstrokes. Smooth surfaces require very even applications of paint. The key difference, however, is the way light affects them: smooth surfaces have very sharp, clearly defined highlights as the light is reflected in them, whereas rough surfaces have very few or no highlights at all.

Materials

![Images](images/f0062-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with Unbleached Titanium. Let dry. Using the #4 filbert brush, outline the slice of toast in Burnt Sienna – it’s just a rectangle with an ‘M’ shape on top – and fill it in roughly, using Yellow Ochre for the lighter parts in the centre and adding Burnt Sienna to the mix towards the edges. Vary the direction of your brushstrokes and allow the texture of the canvas to show through. Let dry completely.

![Images](images/f0062-03.jpg)

**2** With the #6 flat brush and Titanium White, paint a doughnut shape one brushstroke wide for the egg white, leaving the centre open. Apply two or even three coats, depending on how clean and opaque you want it to be, allowing the paint to dry between coats. This is good practice for your brush control, too, as you have to keep restating the circle.

![Images](images/f0063-01.jpg)

**3** Using the #4 filbert brush and Burnt Sienna, strengthen the toasted edge of the bread and add a shadow to the left of the egg white. Fill in the egg yolk with Yellow Ochre, then add Cadmium Yellow Medium on top, leaving a little Yellow Ochre showing on the left-hand side for a shadow. This shows that the yolk is raised and rounded; if it was flat, there would be no shadow.

![Images](images/f0063-02.jpg)

**4** Switch to the #4 bright brush. Mix a very pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and a touch of Mars Black and use it to paint shadows around the left-hand sides of the yolk, egg white and toast. Apply one final layer of Titanium White to the egg white, this time adding a tiny bit of Mars Black to knock back the whiteness just enough for you to be able to add a highlight in the final step.

![Images](images/f0063-03.jpg)

**5** Add highlights to the top right of the yolk and egg white in pure Titanium White, and let dry thoroughly. Mix Mars Black with lots of glazing medium and load a clean brush (or even an old toothbrush). Gently flick the brush with your finger to spatter tiny specks of black pepper on to the egg. If it doesn’t work, just wipe it off with a paper towel and have another go.

![Images](images/f0063-04.jpg)

The rough, scratchy brushstrokes used to paint the toast contrast sharply with the very smooth layers of the egg. The highlight on the egg yolk is what really differentiates them from each other: it’s a tiny part of the whole painting, but it really brings it to life.

### 15 Understanding fabric:  
Theatre curtains

To paint fabric effectively, you need to understand how it drapes and hangs. There are several factors to take into account: the weight of the fabric itself, whether it’s stiff (like burlap) or soft (like velvet or silk) and what it’s draped over or hanging from (something draped over a hard-edged object such as a table will fall in sharp folds, for example). The curtains in this exercise are obviously made of a heavy fabric; you can tell from the way the curtains sag that they’re pulled down by their own weight and the force of gravity. A diaphanous chiffon, for example, would not fall into the same deep folds and creases. It’s important not to make the lines of the folds too straight and mechanical looking: the fabric is not rigid like metal or wood – the folds will vary in depth, so keep your lines slightly wobbly and vary the thickness.

Materials

![Images](images/f0064-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with a rich red mixed from Napthol Red and a little Yellow Ochre. Mix together Napthol Red and Burnt Sienna and, using a #1 round brush, map out the different sections of the curtains with bold, sweeping lines. You don’t need to wait for the underpainting to dry completely: you’re using the same basic colour, so it doesn’t matter if the lines blur a little.

![Images](images/f0064-03.jpg)

**2** Using the same mix and brush, put in the folds of the background curtain; this curtain hangs straight down from top to bottom, so the folds are vertical. Add a little bit of Mars Black to the mix here and there, to give some variety. Add the vertical folds to the side curtains, too.

![Images](images/f0065-01.jpg)

**3** Now establish the flow of the remaining sections. The top section stretches across the stage, but the weight of the fabric makes it sag in the middle so it forms a semicircle; use curved lines here. At the sides, the curtains are pulled back; they curve gently downwards to the point at which they’re tied and then fall in straight, vertical folds.

![Images](images/f0065-02.jpg)

**4** The background curtains are in deeper shadow (cast by the foreground curtains), so begin to distinguish between the curtains to give the scene some depth. Add Burnt Sienna to Napthol Red to make it darker. (This is a good opportunity to practise mixing shades.) Make up a thin wash and apply with the #6 flat brush; because the wash is so thin, the deep folds remain visible.

![Images](images/f0065-03.jpg)

**5** Continue developing the curtains as much as you want. Where you want folds, paint darker lines; where you want the feeling of soft drapes, smooth out the transitions between tones, keeping some patches lighter to show how light hits the material. Let dry. Use Mars Black for the gap between the background curtains and the dark stage area at the base; this helps to anchor the scene. Switch to the #1 round brush and paint a line of Unbleached Titanium for the metal curtain ties. Let dry, then add a line of Burnt Sienna on top.

![Images](images/f0065-04.jpg)

The sagging curves and deep folds in this painting convey the weight of the fabric beautifully. Search out different fabrics around your home and hang them over the back of a chair to see how they fall: a light silk scarf, a cotton bedsheet and a velvet curtain will all drape very differently.

### 16 Colour contrasts:  
Door and padlock

You can create really dynamic paintings by building them around strong colour contrasts. This project exploits contrasting colour temperatures. The term ‘temperature’ is used to describe a colour’s relative warmth or coolness: here, the warm orange tones of the rusty padlock are set against the cool blues of the door. This project is also an exercise in using complementary colours (see page 26). Blue is the complementary of orange, and when complementary colours are placed next to each other in a painting, they make each other appear more intense. The brushwork is also important: the weathered texture is built up gradually, in very thin layers, with the paint being applied roughly and loosely so that underlying colours show through and add to the patina.

Materials

![Images](images/f0066-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Yellow Ochre mixed with lots of glazing medium, applying pressure to the brush to create texture. Don’t smooth it out – you want the texture to show through as you progress. Let dry. Using a #1 round brush and Burnt Sienna mixed with plenty of glazing medium, draw the outline of the padlock. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0066-03.jpg)

**2** Using the #6 flat brush, apply very thin glazes of warm colour to begin developing the worn patina of the surface – Yellow Ochre mixed with Burnt Sienna, Yellow Ochre mixed with Napthol Red and a little Unbleached Titanium. Apply the paint roughly over both the lock and the door, using an almost dry brush and leaving plenty of the underlying colour showing through. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0067-01.jpg)

**3** Mix Mars Black with a little Burnt Sienna. Using the #1 round brush, paint the shaded interior of the loop, the keyhole, the vertical split where the door opens and the cast shadow to the right of the padlock. Paint in the shadow under the horizontal line that bisects the padlock, adding a small hump above the keyhole. Outline the two rivets and the wire loop at the top.

![Images](images/f0067-02.jpg)

**4** Mix a cool blue from Titanium White and Ultramarine Blue. Using the #6 flat brush, apply the colour roughly over the background, cutting out the shape of the padlock as you do so. Keep your paint application fairly random and very textured, leaving some of the original underpainting visible to give additional texture and a rough, weathered appearance.

![Images](images/f0067-03.jpg)

**5** With the #6 flat brush and a mix of Ultramarine Blue, Mars Black and lots of glazing medium, paint the dark blue shadowed areas on the door. Paint the highlights on the padlock with the #1 round brush, using Titanium White and Unbleached Titanium on the top left of the loop, the rivets and the keyhole, and Yellow Ochre on the right. Develop the rust colours with Yellow Ochre, adding other colours (Napthol Red, Cadmium Yellow Medium, Burnt Sienna) where you see fit. This is not precision painting: it’s intended to look rough.

![Images](images/f0067-04.jpg)

The warm, rusty oranges balance the cool blues, creating dynamic contrasts of colour, while the hints of orange on the door enliven this area and help unify the painting.

### 17 Intricate details:  
Butterfly

This is similar to the brick wall project (see page 58) in that you’re establishing the basic structure (the butterfly’s markings) first, and then glazing over it; you can then repeat the process until you have the effect and density of colour that you want. The difference here is that, because the markings are very precise and you want the same level of detail and coloration over the entire painting, you need to work in a much more controlled way, aiming for a very gradual gradation of colour over the butterfly’s wings. Think carefully about your colours, too: look for colours with a reasonable degree of transparency, so that they seem to glow. And don’t use pure black for the wing patterning, otherwise there’s a chance it will look like stained glass rather than a natural, living organism.

Materials

![Images](images/f0068-02.jpg)

**1** Mix a warm orange-yellow from Cadmium Yellow Medium and a tiny bit of Napthol Red to warm it up a little. Paint the background, using a #6 flat brush. Let dry. With a #1 round brush and Burnt Sienna, paint the outline of the wings, the body, and the dark areas at the top of the upper wings.

![Images](images/f0068-03.jpg)

**2** Using the same brush and colour, add more detail in the wing pattern and paint the antennae. Try to keep the patterning symmetrical.

![Images](images/f0069-01.jpg)

**3** Mix Mars Black with a touch of Burnt Sienna. Using the #1 round brush, apply this colour over the Burnt Sienna from the previous step, but keep the colour just inside the Burnt Sienna lines so that you begin to give the pattern some depth. Paint the segmented body as a series of overlapping circles. Let dry completely.

![Images](images/f0069-02.jpg)

**4** Mix a warm orange from Cadmium Yellow Medium and Napthol Red, with plenty of glazing medium. Glaze this over the patterned wings with a #4 filbert brush, adding more yellow as you move downwards. Note how the underlying Mars Black/Burnt Sienna markings remain visible. The three little dots on the top of each wing are just Cadmium Yellow Medium.

![Images](images/f0069-03.jpg)

**5** Using the #1 round brush and the Mars Black and Burnt Sienna mix from step 3, re-establish the patterning on the wings. Put in the white dot markings around the edges of the wings, using Unbleached Titanium. If you wish, you can go back in with the yellow-orange mix from step 4 to refine the colour transitions further.

![Images](images/f0069-04.jpg)

This is a great project to practise your brush control. Repeat it with different colours in your palette, so that you get a feel for how transparent they are.

## Light, shadow and form

Now that you have a better understanding of applying paint to represent flat, two-dimensional objects, it’s time to explore the ways light, shadow and colour function with three-dimensional forms. The projects in this chapter feature objects that can be represented using a small group of simplified forms: spheres, cubes, cones and cylinders.

### 18 Introduction to cubes:  
Block of butter

It often helps to think of your subject as a basic geometric shape, rather than attempt to draw a complete outline. That’s what we’re going to be looking at over the next few pages. Let’s start with the simplest of all – a cube, or box shape. With minor variations, this crops up in countless subjects: buildings, cars, pieces of furniture and so on. The most important thing to remember is that all vertical lines of a cube will be parallel to each other, as will all horizontal lines. So even if you’re looking at the top face of a cube from an angle and the two sides appear to slant, those two side edges will still be parallel to one another, as will the top and bottom edges. A bright brush is perfect for painting cubes, as its shape allows you to create really sharp lines and corners.

Materials

![Images](images/f0072-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Unbleached Titanium mixed with a little glazing medium. Mix a mid-toned blue from Titanium White and Ultramarine Blue. Using the #6 flat brush, paint the background, leaving an oval shape unpainted in the centre for the plate.

![Images](images/f0072-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a pale yellow from Titanium White and a little Cadmium Yellow Medium. Using a #4 bright brush, paint a rectangle a little above the centre of the plate for the block of butter. This is a chance to see how accurate you can be with the bright brush – it’s great for really crisp, squared-off corners. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0073-01.jpg)

**3** The left side of the butter is the most brightly lit; using the #4 bright brush, paint it a very pale yellow mixed from Titanium White and a tiny amount of Cadmium Yellow Medium, angling the top and bottom edges to form a parallelogram so that the butter is seen in perspective. Paint over half of the yellow from step 2 with Yellow Ochre to create the shaded front edge.

![Images](images/f0073-02.jpg)

**4** With a very thin, pale mix of Unbleached Titanium and a tiny bit of Yellow Ochre, paint the reflection of the butter block on the plate, applying it as a very thin glaze to give the illusion that the plate is shiny and reflective. Mix a pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and Mars Black. Switch to a #4 filbert brush and paint a crescent shape on the left to represent the dip in the plate, the cast shadow on the right of the plate and a diagonal line for the knife.

![Images](images/f0073-03.jpg)

**5** With Mars Black, indicate the silhouette of the butter knife. On the blue background, put in the shadow cast by the plate itself using a mix of Ultramarine Blue and a small amount of Mars Black. Switch to the #1 round brush and add highlights on the butter and plate with pure Titanium White. Use the grey mix from step 4 for the knife details.

![Images](images/f0073-04.jpg)

Just a few clean strokes of the bright brush are enough to create a convincing cube. Have a look around your home and see what other cubes you can find: a book, a plastic food container or a square tin can could all be painted in a similar way.

### 19 Introduction to ellipses:  
Pineapple slice

Here we’re looking at a circle in perspective. This isn’t as complicated as it sounds. If you hang a circular object such as a plate on the wall in front of you, you’ll be looking at it straight on and it will appear as a complete circle, because everything is the same distance from you. If you lay it down on the table in front of you, however, the back edge is farther away from you than the front, and so the shape appears to be slightly distorted: instead of being a complete circle, it appears more like an oval, or ellipse. The trick with drawing or painting ellipses is to be bold and confident. If you work too slowly, it’s more likely that you’ll overthink things and get the shape wrong. To get into the swing of things, stand back from your canvas and practise drawing the shape in the air, using your whole arm – a bit like the artist’s equivalent of playing air guitar.

Materials

![Images](images/f0074-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Cadmium Yellow Medium mixed with a little glazing medium. Using a #1 round brush and Yellow Ochre, draw an ellipse in the centre of the panel for the overall shape of the pineapple slice, keeping it as symmetrical as possible; it doesn’t have to be perfect, though, as you can tidy up the shape later.

![Images](images/f0074-03.jpg)

**2** For the hole in the middle of the slice, draw a smaller ellipse in the centre of the first one, then add a half ellipse (a line curving down at each end) across the middle to establish how deep the hole is. Going back to the ellipse from step 1, add a couple of straight lines down from the sides to link with another half ellipse (this time a line curving up at each end).

![Images](images/f0075-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a bright blue from Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White. Using a #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the pineapple slice as you do so.

![Images](images/f0075-02.jpg)

**4** Now you can begin to make the pineapple slice look three-dimensional. The light source is positioned top left, so there are shaded areas on the inside left of the pineapple ring and the right-hand outside edge. Put these in with the #1 round brush and Yellow Ochre. Then put in the lines that radiate out from the centre of the pineapple.

![Images](images/f0075-03.jpg)

**5** Continue painting the light and shaded areas with the #1 round brush to give a 3D effect. For the brightest highlights, use Titanium White on its own. For the mid-tones, use progressively darker mixes: Titanium White + Cadmium Yellow Medium; Cadmium Yellow Medium on its own; then Cadmium Yellow + a little Yellow Ochre. Finally, add a little Burnt Sienna for the darkest yellow tones and put in the cast shadows using Ultramarine Blue mixed with a tiny bit of Mars Black.

![Images](images/f0075-04.jpg)

Bold primary colours really make this painting sing. For a more subtle effect, repeat the exercise with a harmonious colour scheme, using a soft orange or orange-red for the background.

### 20 Introduction to spheres:  
Pincushion

The next geometric shape we’re going to look at is the sphere – a shape that’s found in a multitude of everyday objects, from fruit to footballs. So how do you make a sphere look as if it’s rounded and three-dimensional, rather than just a flat circle? The key is to exploit differences in value as the object curves away from the light: our eyes tell us that if part of the object is in shadow, then that part must be turned away from the light source – so we instinctively ‘read’ it as being rounded. You will be blending colours wet into wet on the canvas rather than creating sharp, clearly defined shifts in value. The gradation is so subtle it’s almost imperceptible, and it’s worth practising this because the fast drying time of acrylics can make it difficult to achieve. This exercise also introduces the idea of contour lines – lines that curve around the form of the object to give it depth and dimension.

Materials

![Images](images/f0076-02.jpg)

**1** Mix a dull red-orange from Napthol Red and a little Cadmium Yellow Medium. Using the #4 filbert brush, paint an oval shape for the pincushion, leaving a star shape unpainted at the top. The pincushion is not a pure circle; painting it as an oval helps to make it look as if it’s squished down a little under the weight of the filling.

![Images](images/f0076-03.jpg)

**2** While the first layer is still wet and without cleaning the brush, go over the shape again, adding a little bit of Mars Black and Burnt Sienna to the mix as you work towards the shaded areas on the left and bottom. Slowly ‘feather’ the colours together so that the wet paints blend on the canvas, giving a subtle gradation of tone. Don’t worry about a few irregularities – the pincushion is bound to have a few wrinkles in it.

![Images](images/f0077-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a dark olive green from Cadmium Yellow Medium, a little Unbleached Titanium and a tiny amount of Mars Black. Using a #4 bright brush, paint the star shape at the top. Switch to a #1 round brush for the stripes radiating down, curving the two outside stripes around the form. Add a little Ultramarine Blue near the base of the stripes, for the parts that are in shadow.

![Images](images/f0077-02.jpg)

**4** Mix a bright blue from Titanium White and Ultramarine Blue. Using a #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the pincushion as you do so and leaving gaps for the pins. As they’re the same colour as the primed canvas, it’s easier to leave spaces for them than to add them later – and the shape of the brush means it’s simple to make the pins absolutely straight.

![Images](images/f0077-03.jpg)

**5** Mix Titanium White with a little glazing medium and add a few pins to the front of the pincushion, using a #1 round brush. Paint the cast shadow under the pincushion in a mix of Ultramarine Blue, Unbleached Titanium and Mars Black. Put in the shadows cast by the pins with a mix of Napthol Red, Cadmium Yellow Medium and a little Burnt Sienna, making sure they follow the contour of the pincushion, just like the green stripes in step 3. Use the same mix for the shadows around the star. Finally, paint the pin heads in any colours you like.

![Images](images/f0077-04.jpg)

The light in my painting was directly above the pincushion. When you’ve finished this painting, try it again with the light to the left or right, to see how it affects the shadows.

### 21 Introduction to cones:  
Holiday hat

The cone is another simple shape, but it’s important to know how light and shade work on it. On the side nearest the light source, the highlight area follows the shape of the cone from top to bottom, parallel to the edge, and is roughly triangular. (Depending on the material the subject is made from, there may not be a highlight as such – just an area that’s lighter in value than the rest.) As you move closer to the middle of the cone, away from the light source, the highlight area is more of a straight vertical. On the side farthest from the light source, the shadow area is once again parallel to the edge and roughly triangular. Like the pincushion (see page 76), this is an exercise in creating smooth transitions of value. You can continue to clean up the gradation – repeated layering and refining give a smoother gradation – but it’s entirely up to you how far you take it.

Materials

![Images](images/f0078-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #1 round brush and Napthol Red, draw an ellipse for the opening of the hat, with lines leading up from it to form a triangle. It really helps to draw in the whole of the ellipse to start with, even though in the finished painting we’ll only see the front arc.

![Images](images/f0078-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a dull red-orange with Napthol Red and a tiny bit of Cadmium Yellow Medium, and fill in the hat with a #4 filbert brush.

![Images](images/f0079-01.jpg)

**3** While it’s drying, add a little Mars Black to the red-orange mix and paint the shaded right-hand side, blending the colours on the canvas. Add a little Unbleached Titanium to the red-orange mix and paint the lighter left-hand side, again blending the colours. These light and shaded areas make roughly triangular shapes. Mix Cadmium Yellow Medium and a tiny amount of Unbleached Titanium and use a #6 flat brush to paint the background, cutting out the shape of the hat as you do so. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0079-02.jpg)

**4** Using a #4 filbert brush and Titanium White mixed with a tiny bit of Unbleached Titanium, paint the pompom on top and the fluffy trim around the rim. It took two layers to make the white parts opaque. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0079-03.jpg)

**5** Mix Unbleached Titanium with a tiny bit of Mars Black and some glazing medium. Using the #4 filbert brush, paint the parts of the pompom and trim that are in shadow. Put in the cast shadow with a #4 bright brush, using Yellow Ochre mixed with plenty of glazing medium to keep it semi-transparent; again, you may need two layers here.

![Images](images/f0079-04.jpg)

The transition from light to shade is very subtle here. If you found this tricky, look for a cone shape with a more reflective surface, such as a metal kitchen funnel, as the highlight and shadow areas will be more obvious.

### 22 Light and shade on a cube:  
Multi-layered sandwich

We’ve already looked at how to draw a cube shape (see page 72); here, we’re concentrating on making that cube shape look three-dimensional. With any object, the side closest to the light source (whether that’s the sun or an artificial source such as a table lamp) is brightly lit and the opposite side is in shadow. With a hard-edged object, such as two sides of a building at right angles to each other or the sandwich in this exercise, there’ll be a sharp shift from light to dark. The first thing you need to do is determine where the light is coming from. Here, it’s above and to the left of the sandwich. The top plane receives the most light, so that’s the brightest; the left-hand plane is next, with the light just glancing onto it, so it’s a little bit darker than the top plane; the right-hand (front) plane receives no direct light, only ambient light, so it’s the darkest.

Materials

![Images](images/f0080-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Unbleached Titanium mixed with a little glazing medium. Mix Yellow Ochre with a tiny bit of Burnt Sienna and, using a #1 round brush, draw the edges of the cube. Remember that the vertical lines will be parallel to each other, as will the slanting lines of the top and bottom edges. Put in a single line to indicate the cocktail stick at the top.

![Images](images/f0080-03.jpg)

**2** Using a #4 filbert, apply stripes of mid-toned colour for the fillings, matching the angle of the top and bottom edges: Cadmium Yellow Medium + Ultramarine Blue (lettuce); Napthol Red + Unbleached Titanium (salami); Cadmium Yellow Medium (cheese). Mix Unbleached Titanium with a little Mars Black and paint the front (darkest) plane, using a #6 flat brush. Use this grey for the cocktail stick shadow; this tells us which direction the light is coming from. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0081-01.jpg)

**3** Now make shades (darker values) of the lettuce and salami colours by adding a tiny bit of Mars Black; use Yellow Ochre for the cheese. Apply them to the front plane with a #1 round brush. Make your lines a bit wobbly, so it looks as if the filling is oozing out.

![Images](images/f0081-02.jpg)

**4** Mix a bright orange from Cadmium Yellow Medium, a small amount of Napthol Red and a little Unbleached Titanium. Using a #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the sandwich as you do so. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0081-03.jpg)

**5** Using a #4 bright brush, paint the brightly lit top of the sandwich in Titanium White with some glazing medium. Add a little Unbleached Titanium to this mix for the left-hand side: this plane needs to look slightly darker than the top in order to look convincingly three-dimensional. Using the #1 round brush and the grey mix from step 2, add shadows under the mid-toned fillings on the left plane. Using the #6 flat brush, put in the cast shadow with a mix of Napthol Red, Mars Black and Burnt Sienna.

![Images](images/f0081-04.jpg)

A cube is a great shape for your first foray into painting three-dimensional subjects, as you can concentrate on getting the values right without having to worry about painting a complicated shape. Other cube shapes that you could try are a pile of books or a plate of chocolate brownies.

### 23 Reflective surfaces:  
Pawn

Surfaces that are highly reflective, such as polished wood, hard plastic, glass and metal, will have reflections that are very crisp and distinct. Highlights, too, will be sharp-edged and bright: look at the left-hand side of the pawn, for example, where light hits the object directly. In this project, the tabletop on which the pawn sits is also reflective: light also bounces from it on to the right-hand side of the pawn. It’s a subtle detail, but one that helps to convey the reflective nature of the materials. This project also uses a classical technique known as grisaille painting, which involves applying layers of thin, transparent grey paint over one another until subtle gradations in value emerge.

Materials

![Images](images/f0082-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #4 filbert brush and Mars Black, paint the shape of the pawn, keeping it as symmetrical as possible. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0082-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a thin glaze of Unbleached Titanium, a tiny bit of Yellow Ochre and plenty of glazing medium. Using a #6 flat brush, loosely brush it over the whole canvas. This glaze changes the value of the black to a mid-grey.

![Images](images/f0083-01.jpg)

**3** Now begin to build up the form of the pawn. With the #4 filbert brush and Mars Black, go over the facets of the pawn that are farthest away from the light. Leave a sliver of mid-grey on the right-hand edge of the pawn, where light is reflected back on to the surface.

![Images](images/f0083-02.jpg)

**4** Thin the Mars Black with glazing medium and carefully feather it over the left side of the pawn, where the light source hits the object directly, to make the transitions between the black surface of the pawn and the grey reflection more subtle. With the same mix, fill in the reflection on the table. The table surface is very reflective, so make the shape of the reflection crisp and sharp.

![Images](images/f0083-03.jpg)

**5** Mix Unbleached Titanium and Titanium White. Using the #6 flat brush, add brighter highlights on the left side of the pawn, taking care to keep the highlight shapes crisp and sharp-edged. Using the same mix, paint the wall behind the pawn, cutting out the shape of the pawn as you do so. Tighten up and refine the highlights with the edge of the #6 flat brush and Titanium White, then glaze the Unbleached Titanium and Titanium White mix over the table and the reflection to unify the piece.

![Images](images/f0083-04.jpg)

With a subject like this, pay careful attention to the shapes of the highlights, as well as to the values. Try painting the same subject again on a different surface, such as matt wood or fabric, to see what difference this makes to the way light is reflected.

### 24 Introduction to cylinders:  
Stack of pancakes

The next geometric shape we’re going to look at is the cylinder, which is, in effect, a series of ellipses stacked on top of each other. I’ve chosen a stack of pancakes to get you started: although they’re elliptical the edges are slightly irregular, so you have a bit more leeway when it comes to getting the shape right – they’re not as rigid as something like a bottle. When you’re painting cylinders, you can often only see the front arc of the ellipses (unless your subject is transparent, like a glass vase). But even though you’ll paint over the lines later, it helps to put in the whole of the ellipses at the start. If you’re thinking of your subject as a three-dimensional shape from the get-go, you’ll have a clearer sense of its roundness and where the light begins to fall away.

Materials

![Images](images/f0084-02.jpg)

**1** Mix Yellow Ochre with glazing medium. Using a #1 round brush, draw two ellipses, one on top of the other, more or less touching and as close in shape and size as possible. Connect them at the outside edges with straight vertical lines. Even though we can’t see the back arc of the bottom ellipse, it helps to put it in at the start so that you get a sense of the cylinder as a whole.

![Images](images/f0084-03.jpg)

**2** Mix the base colour of the pancakes from Unbleached Titanium, Yellow Ochre and a little Cadmium Yellow Medium. Using a #4 filbert brush, fill in the whole of the front shape. Again, you should be thinking about the volume of the cylinder as a whole, even though you’ll break it up later. The edges don’t have to be precise – they’re not that even and regular in real life.

![Images](images/f0085-01.jpg)

**3** Mix the brown ‘cooked’ colour of the pancakes from Yellow Ochre, Burnt Sienna, Unbleached Titanium and a tiny bit of Napthol Red. Using the #4 filbert, paint the top of the stack with the broad side of the brush; paint the contour lines that separate the pancakes with the brush turned on its side. Make sure the contour lines follow the arc of the original ellipse. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0085-02.jpg)

**4** Mix Unbleached Titanium with a little Cadmium Yellow Medium and paint the pat of butter with a #4 bright brush. For the shaded right-hand side of the stack, mix Unbleached Titanium, Mars Black, a little Burnt Sienna and Napthol Red, and a touch of Ultramarine Blue to cool it down, plus quite a lot of glazing medium to make it semi-transparent; you need to be able to see a hint of the contour lines underneath. Apply with the #4 filbert brush.

![Images](images/f0085-03.jpg)

**5** Using a #6 flat brush and an orange-red (Napthol Red + Cadmium Yellow Medium), paint the background, cutting out the shape of the pancake stack as you do so. Using a #1 round brush, refine the butter and pancakes: shadow side of butter (Yellow Ochre + Cadmium Yellow Medium); left side of butter (Titanium White); light colour on pancakes (butter colour from step 4); cast shadows on butter and right-hand side of stack (Burnt Sienna). Using a #4 bright brush, paint the shadow to the right of the pancakes with a mix of Napthol Red, Mars Black and lots of glazing medium.

![Images](images/f0085-04.jpg)

Repeat step 1, varying your viewpoint. Viewed from directly overhead, the ellipse will appear more like a circle; the more you lower your viewpoint, the narrower the ellipse will seem.

### 25 Creating a unified colour scheme:  
Cactus

This project uses a very limited palette to create an almost monochromatic image made up of green and various tints and shades of grey – an analogous colour scheme (see page 26). Using analogous colours is a classic way to create a calm, peaceful mood. But there’s a twist here: sometimes in painting, the things you can’t see are just as important as those that you can. Even though it’s not immediately obvious, the same colour – Cadmium Yellow Medium – is used in every single mix, which helps to unify the painting. This colour is also applied as a thin, transparent glaze over the whole image, subtly modifying both the colours beneath and those laid on top to add a soft, warm glow.

Materials

![Images](images/f0086-02.jpg)

**1** Using Yellow Ochre and a #1 round brush, draw the cylinder of the pot and put in a horizontal line for the tabletop. Mix a dark green colour from Mars Black, a little Cadmium Yellow Medium and glazing medium. Using a #4 filbert brush, block in the oval leaf shapes and add a stroke to the base of the pot. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0086-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a very thin, transparent yellow-green glaze from Cadmium Yellow Medium, Yellow Ochre, a little Mars Black and plenty of glazing medium. Using a #6 flat brush, apply this mix over the entire canvas. Note how the glaze affects the green of the cactus – it started out as quite an acidic green, but the glaze softens it. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0087-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a mid-toned grey from Unbleached Titanium, Mars Black and a tiny bit of Cadmium Yellow Medium and paint the pot with the #4 filbert brush. Add more Cadmium Yellow Medium to push the grey colour towards green. Using a #6 flat brush, apply this over the background, cutting out the shape of the pot and cactus as you do so. The warmth of this background colour is partly due to the yellow glaze underneath.

![Images](images/f0087-02.jpg)

**4** Paint the tabletop in a light grey mix of Unbleached Titanium, Cadmium Yellow Medium and a little bit of Mars Black. Note how well all the colours harmonize with each other: this is because the Cadmium Yellow Medium and Mars Black are common to all the mixes.

![Images](images/f0087-03.jpg)

**5** Darken the tabletop mix slightly and paint the light side of the pot. The shadow colours on the wall and pot are all mixed from the same colours in varying proportions – Mars Black, Unbleached Titanium and Cadmium Yellow Medium. Paint them using the #4 filbert brush. Using the #1 round brush and Titanium White, paint the spines on the cactus and the highlight on the pot.

![Images](images/f0087-04.jpg)

Greens and greys predominate in this simple still life – but note how the initial thin yellow glaze imparts a warm glow to the whole painting.

### 26 Multiple spheres:  
Toy panda

In this project, we’re building up a complex form from a series of interlocking spheres. Treat them in exactly the same way as a simple sphere (see page 76), but take the time to assess each one individually to see where the shifts from light to dark occur. For example, the panda’s protruding snout shields one side of its face from the light, so that side of the face is a little darker than the shaded side of the body. These are small, subtle differences, but they help to convey the variations in the round forms. Experiment with different brushstrokes, too, to see what textures you can create. Dabbing the paint on will create a slightly mottled effect, which is perfect for the fake fur this cuddly panda is made from, whereas a dry-brush treatment might be more appropriate for a toy made from a woven fabric such as linen.

Materials

![Images](images/f0088-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #4 filbert brush and Unbleached Titanium thinned with glazing medium, draw a series of circles and ellipses for the various parts of the panda. The proportions don’t have to be perfect; these are just initial guide lines.

![Images](images/f0088-03.jpg)

**2** Fill in the black parts of the panda, using the #4 filbert brush and Mars Black thinned with a little glazing medium.

![Images](images/f0089-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and a little Mars Black. Using a #1 round brush, fill in the shaded white areas on the left-hand side of the spheres. Add more Unbleached Titanium as you work across to the right-hand side, moving out of the shade and towards the light source.

![Images](images/f0089-02.jpg)

**4** Apply pure Titanium White for the lightest parts of the head and belly. Mix a dark grey from Mars Black and a little Unbleached Titanium. Indicate the eyes, draw the mouth and paint the lighter sections on the tops of the ears, nose, arms and legs (see final painting). Using a #6 flat brush and a mix of Ultramarine Blue and Unbleached Titanium, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the panda as you do so.

![Images](images/f0089-03.jpg)

**5** Mix the cast shadow colour from Ultramarine Blue, a little Mars Black and Unbleached Titanium and apply with a #4 bright brush. With the #1 round brush and Titanium White, paint the whites of the panda’s eyes. When dry, paint the pupils in Mars Black.

![Images](images/f0089-04.jpg)

At first glance, this might look like quite a complicated object to paint – but, like most things, it can easily be broken down into a series of simple geometric shapes.

### 27 Hatching brushstrokes:  
Bee

This project gives you the chance to practise using very short, controlled brushstrokes to create the soft, furry texture of this (literally) larger-than-life bee. These hatching strokes are built up on top of a mid-toned ground colour, allowing some of the underlying colour to remain visible so that you can gradually build light and dark tones on top and create a real sense of depth and form. The shapes are very simple – just circles and egg shapes for the different parts of the bee’s body. This, combined with the bold patterning of black stripes on a yellow-orange ground, allows you to focus on making textural brushstrokes without having to worry too much about your drawing skills.

Materials

![Images](images/f0090-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #1 round brush and Yellow Ochre, draw the outline of the insect as a series of very simple shapes – circles for the head and thorax, an elongated egg shape for the abdomen and wing, and lines for the legs and antennae.

![Images](images/f0090-03.jpg)

**2** Using a #4 filbert brush and leaving a space for the eye, block in the head and body with Yellow Ochre mixed with a tiny bit of Cadmium Yellow Medium. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0091-01.jpg)

**3** Now paint the legs, stripes and head details, using the #1 round brush and Mars Black. The round brush has a fine tip, so it’s perfect for painting small details.

![Images](images/f0091-02.jpg)

**4** Mix Cadmium Yellow Medium with Unbleached Titanium and paint hairs where the light catches the body, using the #1 round brush and short strokes. With the #6 flat brush, paint the background in a mix of Ultramarine Blue, Titanium White and a touch of Unbleached Titanium, cutting out the shape of the insect as you do so. Add more white and thin the colour with glazing medium when you paint the wing, so that it appears transparent.

![Images](images/f0091-03.jpg)

**5** Continue to build up texture and form, making your brushstrokes follow the direction in which the hairs grow. Make the colour darker near the edge of the body (Yellow Ochre) and lighter in the centre (Cadmium Yellow Medium + Unbleached Titanium). Add some Mars Black hairs on the head, and tiny highlights in Titanium White.

![Images](images/f0091-04.jpg)

The short, carefully controlled brushstrokes capture the fuzzy texture of the bee’s body perfectly – but do think carefully about their direction. Before you start painting, take time to analyze whether the tiny hairs stick up or run lengthways along the insect’s body.

### 28 Understanding colour values:  
Paper bag puppet

In the multi-layered sandwich project (see page 80), we used different values of white for the three planes of the sandwich ‘cube’ in order to make the subject look three-dimensional. This exercise takes things a step further, as we’re now bringing different colours into the equation. The same colours (brown, yellow and red) occur on both the light and dark sides of the paper bag. If the difference between the brown colour of the bag on the light and shaded sides is, say, three steps on a value scale (see page 27), then the difference in the yellow and red colours also needs to be three steps in order to create a believable sense of dimension and form. Colour can complicate things, so if you find it hard to judge the values, try looking at your subject through half-closed eyes – or even take a photo on your phone and convert it to black and white.

Materials

![Images](images/f0092-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Unbleached Titanium mixed with a little glazing medium. Using a #1 round brush and thinned Yellow Ochre, paint the outline of the bag. Effectively, you’re drawing a cube (like the sandwich project), so make sure that the lines of each side are parallel to one another – but instead of the top being a normal cube, here it’s at a 45-degree angle. Also draw in the shadow that the bag casts.

![Images](images/f0092-03.jpg)

**2** Block in the shadow under the top of the bag, which forms the puppet’s mouth. Draw the eyes and nose, then delineate the different sections of the bag, again taking care to keep the lines on each side parallel to one another.

![Images](images/f0093-01.jpg)

**3** Using a #4 bright brush, paint the left side of the bag, where the light hits it, using a mix of Unbleached Titanium and a tiny bit of Burnt Sienna for the brown parts and Cadmium Yellow Medium for the shirt. For the shaded side (and the shadows around the eyes and nose), use a mix of Unbleached Titanium and tiny amounts of Mars Black and Burnt Sienna for the brown parts, and Yellow Ochre for the yellow.

![Images](images/f0093-02.jpg)

**4** Using a #1 round brush, paint the pupils of the eyes and the moustache in Mars Black; the nose and bottom lip in a mix of Burnt Sienna and Cadmium Yellow Medium; and the tie and arms in Napthol Red. (Don’t worry about the value of the red for now; you’ll adjust it in the next step.) Mix a pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and Mars Black and paint the cast shadow, using a #6 flat brush.

![Images](images/f0093-03.jpg)

**5** Paint the strands of hair using the #1 round brush and whichever colours you want. Apply a layer of Burnt Sienna to the arm that’s in shadow so that, like the brown and yellow colours on this side of the bag, it’s three steps darker in value than the red on the light side. Paint the whites of the eyes in Titanium White.

![Images](images/f0093-04.jpg)

Being able to assess and mix different values of a colour is essential if you want to make things look three-dimensional. Try this exercise again, using different colours for the shirt and arm, so that you get used to mixing different shades.

### 29 Shadow colours:  
Building block giraffe

Shadow colours are rarely, if ever, pure black: if you look closely you will see that they often comprise (or at least contain) a dark version of a complementary colour. Here, the shadow is a dark blue shadow – and blue and orange are complementaries. This is also a great technique to use to simplify the overall design of a painting and give a clear sense of light and shade. The shadow colour on the orange/brown blocks blends in with the dark blue background; similarly, on the light side of the yellow blocks, you can see that the boundary between the blocks and the background almost disappears.

Materials

![Images](images/f0094-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with Unbleached Titanium. Mix a pale yellow from Cadmium Yellow Medium and Unbleached Titanium. Using a #4 bright brush, paint a strip one brushstroke wide in the centre of the canvas. Apply a second slightly wider stripe of Yellow Ochre to the right, so you create an L shape. You may want to apply two layers of each colour.

![Images](images/f0094-03.jpg)

**2** Referring to the photograph above for the shapes, paint the giraffe’s head, using a #6 flat brush and the same colours as in step 1 – the yellow plus white mix for the light side, and Yellow Ochre for the dark.

![Images](images/f0095-01.jpg)

### 30 Combining cones and spheres:  
Ice-cream cone

This is a slightly more complex arrangement than some of the previous projects, as it involves several shapes – spheres for the cherry and ice cream, an ellipse for the part of the ice cream pressed on to the cone, and the cone itself. Each one receives light in a slightly different way, so you can’t just treat all the shadow areas the same. With each area in turn, ask yourself whether there is a sharp transition from light to shade (as there would be on a cube, where the planes are at right angles to each other) or if the transition is more gradual as the object curves away from the light source. Think about textures, too. An uneven application of paint can look like ripples or undulations in the object’s surface, conveying the idea that it’s not totally smooth.

Materials

![Images](images/f0096-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #1 round brush and Yellow Ochre thinned with glazing medium, draw a sphere, ellipse and cone stacked on top of each other, trying to keep things symmetrical. I only put in part of the ellipse, but you could draw in the whole thing if you wish.

![Images](images/f0096-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a pale blue from Titanium White, Unbleached Titanium, Ultramarine Blue and a tiny hint of Cadmium Yellow Medium, and paint the ice cream, using a #6 flat brush. Then paint the cone in Yellow Ochre, using flat, slab-like strokes to create the texture of the wafer.

![Images](images/f0097-02.jpg)

### 31 Complex ellipses:  
Button flower

We’ve already looked at painting a single ellipse (see page 74). Your challenge here, with multiple ellipses in the same painting, is to make each one as symmetrical as possible. A really useful tip is to try to draw ellipses in one fluid movement, rather than in two halves – otherwise there’s a risk that you’ll make the ends of the ellipse pointed or blunt. This really is a case of practice makes perfect. The exact shape of an ellipse will depend on your viewpoint, so just use your eyes and draw what you see. You will notice that as your viewpoint gets lower, the ellipse will appear narrower until it disappears altogether and all you can see is a curve. Conversely, as your viewpoint gets higher, the ellipse will appear broader until, when you view your subject from directly overhead, it looks like a complete circle.

Materials

![Images](images/f0098-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #1 round brush and Yellow Ochre thinned with glazing medium, draw two ellipses, one inside the other, to represent the raised area of the centre button.

![Images](images/f0098-03.jpg)

**2** Now draw the ellipses of the button petals, making sure they’re evenly spaced and consistent in size. Although you won’t see the full ellipses in the finished painting, it really helps to put them in here so you get a feel for the overall shape. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0099-01.jpg)

**3** Using a #4 filbert, paint the flower centre in Cadmium Yellow Medium. While that’s drying, mix a pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and a small amount of Mars Black. Paint the petals, leaving a little of the white of the canvas showing through to represent highlights. Apply a second layer of Cadmium Yellow Medium to the centre to get a good, opaque coverage.

![Images](images/f0099-02.jpg)

**4** Add a little more Mars Black to the grey from step 3 and paint the shadows on the petals, using the #4 filbert brush turned on its side. (Note that the interiors of those buttons are also a series of ellipses.) For the stem, mix a mid-tone green from Cadmium Yellow Medium and a tiny bit of Mars Black. The shadows on the centre petal are Yellow Ochre, while the highlight is Titanium White plus a very tiny amount of Cadmium Yellow Medium.

![Images](images/f0099-03.jpg)

**5** Mix a bright blue from Ultramarine Blue, Titanium White and a tiny amount of Unbleached Titanium. Still using the #4 filbert, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the flower as you do so. Switch to the #1 round brush and mix some Ultramarine Blue, Unbleached Titanium and a little Mars Black for the cast shadows under the flower and stem. ![Images](images/f0099-04.jpg)

Getting the shape of the ellipses right is crucial to make this image look convincingly three-dimensional. Look around your home for another circular subject, such as a shallow round bowl, and try drawing it from different viewpoints to see how the shape changes.

### 33 Reflected light:  
Takeaway carton

When you’re painting a white object, only the very brightest highlights will ever be a pure white; any shaded areas will be a shade of white, and even the mid-tones will be white mixed with another colour. What that other colour is will depend on what colours are nearby. This is because light bounces off the surfaces that it hits and is reflected back on to objects nearby – so nearby objects then pick up some of the reflected colour. In addition, light (and hence colour) is reflected back at the same angle at which it first reached the surface; it can’t bounce around curves or over steep sides. Here, the reflected blue can’t reach the inside of the carton – so the interior leans towards an off-white tint.

Materials

![Images](images/f0102-02.jpg)

**1** Mix Yellow Ochre with a tiny bit of Unbleached Titanium and some glazing medium. Using a #1 round brush, draw the shape of the carton. It’s roughly a cube, but it’s slightly tapered at the base, so the vertical lines are not parallel to each other (although the horizontal ones are).

![Images](images/f0102-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a pale blue from Ultramarine Blue, Titanium White, a little Unbleached Titanium and glazing medium. Using a #6 flat brush, paint the shadow side of the carton.

![Images](images/f0103-01.jpg)

**3** Add more Titanium White to the mix and paint the light side of the carton. There’s no need to clean your brush completely: just wipe off excess paint from the brush on a paper towel, then add Titanium White to the mix on your palette. It doesn’t matter if you go over the Yellow Ochre lines, as you’ll be cutting out the shape from the background in the next step.

![Images](images/f0103-02.jpg)

**4** Mix a bright blue from Ultramarine Blue and a little Titanium White and glazing medium. Using the #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the carton as you do so. Try to keep the box edges really sharp.

![Images](images/f0103-03.jpg)

**5** Using the #1 round brush and Titanium White, add highlights on the edges and folds of the carton. Paint the handle in Mars Black and the interior in Unbleached Titanium. Put in the shadows on the light side of the carton using the same colour you used to paint the shaded side of the carton. For the cast shadow, mix Ultramarine Blue, Mars Black and a little bit of Burnt Sienna, and apply with a #4 bright brush.

![Images](images/f0103-04.jpg)

Our eyes automatically ‘read’ the carton as being white, even though there’s very little pure white on it. Once you’ve completed this exercise in blue, paint it again with a different background colour and see how that changes the colour of the reflected light.

### 34 Multiple textures:  
Paintbrush

Even the most mundane household objects can make an interesting painting. This is a fun project that brings together several of the textural techniques you’ve learned already – an almost dry brush and very short, thin brushstrokes for the coarse bristles; tints and shades to create the effect of light shining on the wet paint; fine highlights to make the metal ferrule appear reflective; and flat applications of colour for the matt wooden brush handle. Materials

Using a #4 filbert brush, paint a rectangle for the brush hairs, making the top slightly irregular, and narrowing into a triangle and thinner rectangle at the bottom. ![Images](images/f0105-01.jpg)

**3** With the #4 filbert and Burnt Sienna, indicate a slight shadow under the paint on the brush and paint the stripes on the ferrule. Paint the handle in Mars Black. Let dry.

**4** Mix Yellow Ochre and Unbleached Titanium and paint the bristles with a series of short, fine brushstrokes using the very edge of an almost dry #4 bright brush. Have a look around your home and see what other multi-textured objects you can find to paint. What about a fur-trimmed leather boot, a glossy lipstick in a matt black case or a silver corkscrew and cork? They’re all simple shapes with interesting contrasts in texture.

### 35 Complex cylinder:  
Pretzel

Even quite complex shapes can be broken down into simple geometric forms. Although a pretzel might look complicated, with all those twists and turns, it’s actually a variation on a basic cylinder. If that sounds a bit far-fetched, think about how a pretzel is made: it’s just a sausage – or cylinder – of dough with the ends crossed over and then looped under each other. So the same principles that apply to a simple cylinder, such as the stack of pancakes (see page 84), also apply here. There are no hard edges; instead, the cylinder curves away from the light, creating gentle transitions of value as it moves from light to shade. The trick is to look for where the transitions occur, rather than trying to paint the pretzel in a linear way, and to blend them so that they’re very smooth and gradual.

Materials

![Images](images/f0106-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush and Yellow Ochre straight from the tube, loosely draw the shape of the pretzel to establish the base colour; apply only one layer to keep some texture. You don’t need to wait for this to dry. Mix the background colour from Napthol Red, Cadmium Yellow Medium and a little Yellow Ochre. Using the #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the pretzel as you do so.

![Images](images/f0106-03.jpg)

**2** Now establish the lightest and darkest values. Using a #4 filbert brush, paint the lighter parts of the pretzel in a mix of Yellow Ochre, Unbleached Titanium and a tiny bit of Cadmium Yellow Medium. Paint the darker baked parts in a mix of Burnt Sienna, Napthol Red and Unbleached Titanium.

![Images](images/f0107-01.jpg)

**3** Continue to develop the sense of light and shade by putting in the mid-tones. Glaze Yellow Ochre over the lighter colour where the cylinder begins to curve away from the light, which is coming from the right. Glaze a mix of Burnt Sienna, Napthol Red and a little Yellow Ochre over the dark parts.

![Images](images/f0107-02.jpg)

**4** Continue to develop the values as you see fit, keeping the transitions soft and gradual: there are no sharp edges here. For the highlights, apply Titanium White with a #1 round brush.

![Images](images/f0107-03.jpg)

**5** To complete the painting, add sprinkles of salt crystals in Titanium White, and put in the cast shadows in a mix of Napthol Red and a little Mars Black. Remember that the light is coming from the right, so the shadows will fall towards the left.

![Images](images/f0107-04.jpg)

Assessing where light hits the subject and where the shadows fall is key with a subject like this, as all those twists and turns can seem a bit overwhelming to paint. A garden hose would be another good subject to experiment with.

### 36 Random edges:  
Cumulus clouds

It takes a bit of practice and brush control to achieve soft, apparently random edges. Clouds are a great subject to practise with, as they’re insubstantial, billowing shapes. They’re often so fleeting and fast moving that it’s almost impossible to pin them down to a solid, hard-edged shape. It helps if you keep your wrist loose and flexible, and work quickly; if you grip the brush too tight, you’re likely to end up with hard, sharp lines. Another tip is to allow your brush to trail off so that you get tiny drybrush wisps of white through which you can see the blue of the sky. This is also a good exercise for painting gradations of colour, both in the sky (which gets paler towards the horizon) and in the clouds. One of the joys of acrylic is that, while it’s still wet, you can easily move the paint around, creating smooth transitions from one value to the next.

Materials

![Images](images/f0110-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Unbleached Titanium. This off-white will stand for the mid-value of the white clouds. Mix a thin, pale grey from Unbleached Titanium and a little Mars Black. Using a #4 filbert brush, indicate the horizon and draw a series of different-sized ovals for the clouds; you may find it helps to hold the brush near the end of the handle to get more wobbly, less defined shapes. Roughly scribble in a shadow on the largest cloud. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0110-03.jpg)

**2** With pure Titanium White and the #4 filbert, paint the brightest parts of the clouds, where the sun is shining through the edges. Lightly indicate a few distant cloud shapes along the horizon. Note that some of the ground colour still shows through at this stage. Twist and turn your brush as you work, keeping your wrist loose so that you don’t tighten up and create hard edges.

![Images](images/f0111-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a bright blue from Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White. Using a #4 bright brush, paint the sky, cutting out the shapes of the clouds as you do so. Gradually add more Titanium White as you move down towards the horizon; try to keep the gradation smooth. To create the wispy cloud trails, drybrush blue over parts of the white; this also helps to soften the cloud edges.

![Images](images/f0111-02.jpg)

**4** Mix Ultramarine Blue, Unbleached Titanium and a tiny amount of Mars Black to establish the blue of the distant hills, using the #4 bright brush. With the #4 filbert and a mix of Yellow Ochre, Unbleached Titanium and a little Cadmium Yellow Medium, paint the fields in the foreground.

![Images](images/f0111-03.jpg)

**5** Using the #4 bright brush, block in the foreground hill in a mix of Mars Black, Burnt Sienna and a tiny bit of Unbleached Titanium, making short vertical strokes with just the edge of the brush for the trees. Fill in the interior of the large cloud in a pale grey mix of Unbleached Titanium and a small amount of Mars Black, blending it into the colours already laid down.

![Images](images/f0111-04.jpg)

The soft, billowing shapes and rapid brushstrokes capture the feeling of clouds scudding across the sky. Repeat this exercise, altering the size and position of the clouds and varying the amount of gradation in both clouds and sky.

### 37 Expressive brushstrokes:  
Banana peel

Up to now, we’ve looked at ways of conveying form through shading and by blending colours to create transitions in value from one plane to the next. In this project we’re emphasizing – or even exaggerating – these planes by using very angular, straight-edged brushstrokes. The same slab-like strokes are also used to layer the colours on the banana peel. Your choice of brush is important here: one with a flat or only slightly rounded profile, such as a filbert or a bright, will make the task much easier. ### 38 Luminous light effects:  
City at sunset

In this cityscape, the colours from the setting sun pour over the buildings, creating a misty haze through which only silhouettes can be seen. The colour radiates outwards, with the white of the canvas standing for the searingly bright orb of the sun itself and the colours gradually darkening and becoming warmer towards the edges of the canvas. This creates a kind of tunnel effect that channels our eye towards the centre of the painting. The hazy effect is achieved by using very thin glazes that contain lots of glazing medium; this makes the colours very transparent, so you see the solid, heavily textured buildings though them. The glazes combine optically on the canvas, each successive layer adding colour without obliterating the colours underneath. Keep your brushstrokes quite rough; the more smoothly you blend the colours, the less it will look like haze.

Materials

![Images](images/f0114-02.jpg)

**1** Using Mars Black and a #6 flat brush, paint the city skyline as a silhouette of rectangles and thin spires. Take care to maintain some texture in your brushstrokes: as you glaze over the top in subsequent steps, this will help to make the scene look extra hazy and more atmospheric. Let dry completely, as you’re going to be doing some heavy layering over the top and you don’t want to lose the crisp shapes of the buildings.

![Images](images/f0114-03.jpg)

**2** Leaving a circle unpainted for the sun, cover the canvas with a thin glaze of Cadmium Yellow Medium, using a very high ratio of glazing medium to pigment. Apply the paint as lines radiating outwards – circular brushstrokes around the sun, then diagonal lines over the buildings, going down on the left-hand side and up on the right-hand side, creating a dark ‘tunnel’ effect. Let dry for a few minutes.

![Images](images/f0115-01.jpg)

**3** Mix Napthol Red with lots of glazing medium and apply with the same radiating brushstrokes as in the previous step, staying away from the yellow area around the sun so that this area remains bright and vibrant. Note how the underlying colours remain visible through the thin glazes: the silhouettes are still bold and crisp, but now they’re overlaid with red and yellow, giving the impression of the sunset being reflected in the stone and glass.

![Images](images/f0115-02.jpg)

**4** Switch to a #4 bright brush and re-establish the shape of the sun by painting around it in Titanium White. Paint the windows and the smoke coming out of the top of the smoke stack white. Allow to dry completely before applying the final glaze.

![Images](images/f0115-03.jpg)

**5** Apply more glazes of colour (more Napthol Red close to the canvas edges, more Cadmium Yellow Medium closer to the sun) until you have the effect you want.

![Images](images/f0115-04.jpg)

Experiment with making your brushstrokes rougher or smoother to see what difference this makes to the haziness of the painting. Reinterpret the project with your own city skyline, or find images online of instantly recognizable skylines such as New York’s Chrysler Building or Florence’s Duomo.

### 39 Luminous glazes:  
Illuminated lightbulb

Making something look luminous is challenging: in oils, you can take your time smudging and blending the colours together, but acrylics dry so quickly that you have to adopt a different approach. The answer is to apply glazes, or very thin layers of colour. This allows some of the underlying colour to show through, creating a shimmering effect, and also makes it possible to blend the colours wet into wet to create very smooth transitions from one shade to the next. You also need to look at where the colour transitions occur. Needless to say, the lightest and brightest areas are closest to the source of the light, giving a kind of halo effect, and the colours gradually get darker as you move farther away. The colour palette, however, is limited, with Titanium White being reserved for the very brightest parts – those that are so bright it hurts your eyes to look directly at them.

Materials

![Images](images/f0116-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in a thin layer of Cadmium Yellow Medium mixed with plenty of glazing medium, allowing some texture to show through. Let dry. Using Mars Black and a #4 bright brush, paint the silhouette of the light fitting. Paint the very brightest parts of the interior of the bulb in Titanium White.

![Images](images/f0116-03.jpg)

**2** Let the black from step 1 dry; the white can still be wet, as you’re not painting over it here. Make a thin mix of orange-brown from Napthol Red and a little bit of Burnt Sienna. Using the #6 flat brush, glaze over the background, cutting out the shape of the bulb as you do so. Apply the glaze more thinly closer to the bulb so that more of the yellow base colour shows through.

![Images](images/f0117-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a brighter orange from Cadmium Yellow Medium and a little Napthol Red. Using the #4 filbert brush, paint the background area closest to the lightbulb. Use some of the same colour for the darker sections within the bulb, towards the top under the light fitting.

![Images](images/f0117-02.jpg)

**4** Using the #6 flat brush and Titanium White, paint the brightest areas inside the bulb.

![Images](images/f0117-03.jpg)

**5** Continue building up layers of glazes, using the same mixes as before, until you’ve got the effect you want, with smooth transitions from one shade to the next. It’s a gradual process of manipulating the colours to where you want them to be – brightest around the edges of the bulb and darkening towards the edges of the canvas.

![Images](images/f0117-04.jpg)

Note the halo effect – the light is brightest closest to the bulb and falls off as you move farther away.

### 40 Creating clean, fresh whites:  
Swan

When you paint a white object, it’s rarely – if ever – pure white. Some areas will be in shadow, others will have really bright highlights, and in between will be a range of mid-values. It’s a good idea to start from the mid-values, as this makes it easier to judge how much darker and lighter to go for the rest of the painting. Here, the canvas is toned with Unbleached Titanium, and it is this colour that stands for the mid-toned whites of the swan. You’ll also be cutting out the shape of the swan by painting the background. This gives a freshness that would be hard to achieve by painting the light values on top, as each value of white receives only one or, at most, two layers of paint. If you painted the background first and the swan on top, you would need several layers to achieve the right degree of opacity, and this could make your painting look heavy and laboured.

Materials

![Images](images/f0118-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas with Unbleached Titanium. Using a #1 round brush and Yellow Ochre, paint the outline of the swan as a series of simplified shapes – an egg shape for the body, a leaf for the tail, an oval for the head and an S shape for the neck. Allow to dry. The Unbleached Titanium will stand for the mid-values of white, so judge all the other values in relation to it.

![Images](images/f0118-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a mid-toned blue from Ultramarine Blue, Titanium White, a little Unbleached Titanium and plenty of glazing medium. Using a #4 filbert brush, loosely indicate the shadow areas, letting some of the brushmarks show to create texture. Don’t try to paint individual feathers – instead, look at the overall shape of the feather masses and make your brushstrokes follow the direction of the feathers. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0119-01.jpg)

**3** Mix Titanium White with very tiny amounts of Unbleached Titanium and Mars Black to knock the value down a bit; this is so that you can apply pure Titanium White highlights later. Using the #4 filbert brush, loosely indicate the lighter feather masses, allowing some of the Unbleached Titanium canvas to show through for the mid-toned whites. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0119-02.jpg)

**4** Mix a dark, warm background colour from Mars Black, Ultramarine Blue and a little Burnt Sienna. Using the #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the swan as you do so. Use the edge of the brush to get crisp shapes, and leave the reflection area unpainted for now. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0119-03.jpg)

**5** Using the #4 filbert, paint the most brightly lit parts (the front of the head, neck and chest) in pure Titanium White, smoothing the colour into the underlying mid-tones. Thin the background colour by adding glazing medium. Using the #6 flat brush, glaze this colour over the bottom half of the painting, dragging the brush across the surface to create the effect of ripples caused by the swan swimming through the water. Paint the swan’s eye and head markings in Mars Black, using the #1 round brush.

![Images](images/f0119-04.jpg)

Don’t get too caught up in detail: a hint of the feather texture is enough. Instead, concentrate on the different values of white, as these are what will make the swan look rounded and three-dimensional.

### 41 Simplifying the subject:  
Flamingo

When you first start painting, the temptation is often to make things overly complicated. In reality, even complex subjects can generally be reduced to a series of simple, interlocking shapes and approaching a subject in this way can really force you to think about what’s essential. Hint at detail by using expressive brushstrokes to create texture, rather than putting in every single feather or leaf. Simplify the colours, too, reducing your colour palette to a narrow range and creating variety through tints and shades. And make sure your colour choices are balanced: if the background and main subject have the same intensity (or saturation) of colour, they can compete with each other, fighting for the viewer’s attention. Here, the background is a pale blue; adding a little Unbleached Titanium to the mix desaturates and cools the colour slightly, so that the background appears to recede.

Materials

![Images](images/f0120-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in a blue mixed from Ultramarine Blue, Titanium White, plus a tiny bit of Unbleached Titanium to desaturate the background so that it appears to recede. Allow to dry. Using a #1 round brush and Napthol Red, paint the outline of the flamingo as a series of simple shapes – slightly curved triangles for the beak and tail, ovals for the body and head, and an S shape for the neck.

![Images](images/f0120-03.jpg)

**2** There’s no need to let the outline dry, as the colour will be incorporated into the next mix. Using a #4 filbert brush and a mid-toned pink mixed from Napthol Red, a little Cadmium Yellow Medium, Unbleached Titanium and a little glazing medium, roughly block in the flamingo. Let dry for a minute or two.

![Images](images/f0121-01.jpg)

**3** Add a tiny amount of Mars Black to Napthol Red. Using the #1 round brush, fill in the S-curve of the neck, a circular shape for the eye, the underside of the beak, and the shadow area of the tail feathers. Also put in zigzag lines for the reflections of the legs.

![Images](images/f0121-02.jpg)

**4** Using the #1 round brush, paint the shaded underbelly in a mix of Mars Black, Unbleached Titanium and a tiny bit of Napthol Red to push it towards pink. (Note that some light is reflected up onto the bird from the water, so some of the initial light pink is visible at the bottom of the body.) Mix a brighter pink from Napthol Red and Unbleached Titanium and paint the upper body with the #4 filbert brush. Paint the beak and eyeball in Mars Black.

![Images](images/f0121-03.jpg)

**5** For the shadow on the water, mix a dark purple from Ultramarine Blue and Napthol Red and apply it with the #1 round brush. Apply tints of pink on the upper part of the body by working Titanium White into the bright pink from step 4, blending the colours on the canvas. Paint the very brightest highlights in pure Titanium White.

![Images](images/f0121-04.jpg)

If you’re painting a subject like this from life, it can be hard to get the shapes just right. Start by making a series of ultra-quick pencil sketches, concentrating on the angle of the bird’s head in relation to its body and legs, in order to fix the rhythm of the bird’s movement in your mind.

### 42 Dramatic contrast:  
Light on road

Introducing contrast into your work is a great way to give a painting dramatic impact, even when the subject matter itself is fairly mundane. There are several kinds of contrast at work here: contrast in scale (the foreground takes up most of the image, while the mountains occupy only a small area); contrast in value (light sunlit patches are set against dark shadows); and contrast in colour temperature (the warm violet shadows are balanced by the relatively cool reds of the background mountains). The bold shapes, veering towards abstraction, also give the image impact. This painting also exploits complementary contrasts (see page 26): the golden yellow of the cornfields offsets the violet shadows on the road, while the green mountains and verges are counterbalanced by the red earth tones. These are all tools for making more dramatic images.

Materials

![Images](images/f0122-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, apply a thin layer of Yellow Ochre over most of the canvas, leaving some texture for the rough road. Paint the sky area in a mix of Yellow Ochre and Unbleached Titanium. Note the contrast in proportions – the foreground takes up about four-fifths of the canvas.

![Images](images/f0122-03.jpg)

**2** With a #4 filbert brush and Burnt Sienna thinned with glazing medium, roughly block in the composition, simplifying everything to a series of basic geometric shapes. Instead of gentle curves, I chose to make a series of angled lines for the road; it’s not an abstract approach, but nor is it strictly representational.

![Images](images/f0123-01.jpg)

**3** Using the #4 filbert brush, roughly block in the road in a mix of Unbleached Titanium, Napthol Red and a little Cadmium Yellow Medium. Mix a dark green from Mars Black and a tiny bit of Cadmium Yellow Medium, and block in the shadow areas, keeping the shapes very bold and stark. Paint the sky in a mix of Titanium White, Unbleached Titanium and a little Ultramarine Blue, allowing some of the original underpainting to show for the clouds.

![Images](images/f0123-02.jpg)

**4** Mix a violet colour from Ultramarine Blue, Unbleached Titanium and Napthol Red. Using the #4 filbert brush, block in the shadows on the road. Develop the grassy areas with a mix of Mars Black, Cadmium Yellow Medium and a little Unbleached Titanium to lighten the colour a little. These dark colours contrast starkly with the patches of bright sunlight.

![Images](images/f0123-03.jpg)

**5** Continue to work over the painting, developing the colours and values. Put in some light green grasses to balance the dark green ones, and warm up the golden yellow of the cornfields with a thin glaze of Napthol Red to offset the cool violet of the road.

![Images](images/f0123-04.jpg)

Look at other landscape scenes – either your own photos or images online – and think about how you might use the same principles to paint them. Simplify the shapes and details wherever possible, and remember that colours don’t always have to be true to life.

### 43 Broken brushstrokes:  
Tree

Broken brushstrokes are simply small dabs or short strokes of paint applied without being blended together; the colours combine optically, rather than physically on the palette or canvas, and some of the underlying canvas is allowed to show through. The technique creates a very lively, energetic feel and is perfect for conveying the sparkle of natural light – not surprising, therefore, that it is often seen in the works of the late 19th-century Impressionists. In this project, the background colours grade from red, through orange and yellow, to green. This is known as a prismatic or spectral gradation, the colours following the order in which they’re seen when sunlight passes through a prism. The fact that these colours are close together on the colour wheel helps to harmonize and unify the painting.

Materials

![Images](images/f0124-02.jpg)

**1** Working upwards from the bottom of the canvas, apply short vertical strokes of paint with a #4 filbert brush. Start with an orange mix of Cadmium Yellow Medium and Napthol Red at the base, gradually add more yellow as you get nearer the centre and finally add Ultramarine Blue to the yellow to create the greens. The shape of the filbert makes it ideal for this kind of mark making. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0124-03.jpg)

**2** Mix Burnt Sienna with glazing medium and paint the silhouette of the tree trunk and main branches, as well as the horizon line, again using the #4 filbert brush. If you don’t have any suitable reference material of your own, it’s easy to find tree silhouettes online.

![Images](images/f0125-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a slightly darker brown from Burnt Sienna and Ultramarine Blue. Using the #4 filbert again, fill in the interior of the tree using broken hatching marks, turning the brush on its side. These broken marks on the darker parts of the trunk also hint at the texture of the bark. Continue adding branches, switching to a #1 round brush for the smaller ones.

![Images](images/f0125-02.jpg)

**4** Mix a dark olive green from Cadmium Yellow Medium and Mars Black (this give a warmer, more natural-looking green than adding blue to the yellow). Dab the colour over the tree, looking for the overall shape of the foliage masses rather than trying to paint individual leaves. Dab in some shrubbery in the background and some grass around the base of the trunk.

![Images](images/f0125-03.jpg)

**5** Mix Titanium White with a tiny bit of Ultramarine Blue. Using a #1 round brush, randomly dab in ‘sky holes’ – places where the sky shows through the foliage – to give the impression of light shining through.

![Images](images/f0125-04.jpg)

The purpose of this exercise is to explore the creative possibilities of broken brushstrokes; although the technique works particularly well for landscapes, you can apply it to any of the projects done so far to make them more impressionistic and create a more energetic painting.

### 44 Gradation and depth:  
Misty mountains

One of the ways of creating the illusion of depth and distance in a painting, particularly in landscapes, is through gradating colours. Things appear smaller the farther away they are – but colours also appear cooler, paler and less bright with distance. In this project, you can see the gradation in the mountain ranges. Texture and details are also reduced with distance. Here, for example, we can see trees silhouetted against the skyline on the nearest range of mountains, but on the next range only the overall shapes of the mountains themselves can be discerned. This project also exploits the natural luminosity of red and yellow pigments by making use of thin glazes for the sunset. Because the colours are all in the same part of the colour wheel (red to yellow, combining optically to create orange), they intermingle happily, creating a harmonious colour scheme.

Materials

![Images](images/f0126-02.jpg)

**1** Mix a warm pink from Napthol Red, Unbleached Titanium and a tiny bit of Cadmium Yellow Medium to create more of a sunset pink than a salmon pink. Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas, keeping the texture rough and uneven. Allow to dry a little, but not completely.

![Images](images/f0126-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a fairly thick, warm purple from Napthol Red, Unbleached Titanium and Ultramarine Blue. Using a #6 flat brush, drybrush the colour across the middle of the canvas, applying very little pressure: you want a slightly broken coverage that allows some of the initial pink to show through.

![Images](images/f0127-01.jpg)

**3** While the paint is still wet, mix a rich orange from Cadmium Yellow Medium and Napthol Red and apply a thin glaze over the top third of the canvas. For the middle third, add more Napthol Red to the mix and drybrush it over the canvas. For the mountains at the base, mix a dark but warm purple from Ultramarine Blue, Unbleached Titanium and a little Mars Black. Apply this paint more thickly than in the sky so that the land has more solidity.

![Images](images/f0127-02.jpg)

**4** Switch to a #4 filbert brush and continue while the purple from step 3 is still wet. Add more Mars Black to the purple mix and paint a loose V shape at the base of the canvas; the darker value gives the impression that this area is closest to the viewer. Push the brush into the wet underlayer to mix the colours and establish the shape of the middle mountain range. Put in some short strokes with the brush tip for trees.

![Images](images/f0127-03.jpg)

**5** Paint the closest trees, along the bottom of the canvas, in Mars Black. To create the orange haze around the sun, mix Cadmium Yellow Medium, Napthol Red and Unbleached Titanium and apply a short stroke with the #4 filbert brush. Paint the sun itself in Cadmium Yellow Medium, using a #1 round brush.

![Images](images/f0127-04.jpg)

Although the mountains occupy only the bottom quarter of the painting, the three carefully gradated values of purple convey the scale of the scene. Don’t feel limited by the small size of the canvas: you can create an impressive picture even on such a small square of canvas.

### 45 Mixing colours using glazes:  
Lighthouse

A glaze is basically a very thin mix of colour – so thin that the colour of the canvas or any paint underneath shows through it. Each layer of paint is modified by the layer(s) underneath; the colours mix optically on the canvas – for example, a yellow glaze applied on top of blue will appear green – yet the colours remain distinct, so you get a much more lively, shimmering effect than you would by applying a ready-mixed green. This makes it the perfect technique for capturing the fleeting effects of light. Be sure to thin the paint for your glazes with acrylic glazing medium rather than water, as this not only increases the fluidity of the paint but also helps it to adhere to the canvas. You may be surprised at how little pigment you actually need. Also remember to let each glaze dry before you apply the next; with acrylics, this does not take long.

Materials

![Images](images/f0128-02.jpg)

**1** Using the #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in a soft salmon pink colour mixed from Napthol Red, Unbleached Titanium, a little Cadmium Yellow Medium and acrylic glazing medium. Mix a reddish brown from Napthol Red and a touch of Burnt Sienna. Using a #4 filbert brush, block in the shapes of the rocks.

![Images](images/f0128-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a soft violet blue from Ultramarine Blue, Unbleached Titanium and a tiny bit of Napthol Red. Using the #4 filbert brush, roughly block in the clouds and their reflections, leaving some of the underlying pink sunset colour showing through in parts. Use the same colour to block in the lighthouse and reflections.

![Images](images/f0129-01.jpg)

**3** Using a #1 round brush and a darker version of the violet blue from step 2, paint some detail on the lighthouse. From now on, you’re going to be applying thin glazes of colour, so add glazing medium to your mixes. Add more Unbleached Titanium to the salmon pink from step 1 to give a pinky orange. With the #4 filbert, glaze this over the pink part of the sky and its reflection.

![Images](images/f0129-02.jpg)

**4** Add more Ultramarine Blue to the violet blue mix from step 2 and apply to the darker part of the sky and reflection. Glaze some of the light pinky orange colour from step 3 over the lighthouse: the subject is bathed in the light of the setting sun and the glaze captures this warm, hazy effect beautifully.

![Images](images/f0129-03.jpg)

**5** The transition from ultramarine to violet to orange is quite stark here, so soften it by glazing over the painting with the light pinky orange mix from step 3; this also helps to unify the painting colourwise. For the light itself, use pure Titanium White.

![Images](images/f0129-04.jpg)

Glazing – building up the paint in very thin layers – gives a more convincing sense of the transient effects of light than painting directly, colour by colour.

### 46 Complementary colours:  
Lemon branch

Complementary colours are colours that are opposite each other on the colour wheel (see page 26). These colour pairs seem more intense than other colour combinations and can make for a very dynamic painting. One thing to be aware of is that if the complementaries are the same saturation and are placed right next to each other, they seem to vibrate: the adjoining edges appear to blur together and almost seem to be oscillating. Overly saturated colours can also look rather artificial. To get around this, a useful tip is to desaturate, or tone down, one or both of the colours by adding a grey or Unbleached Titanium. The cast shadows are also an important part of this composition. Note that the edge of the shadow is sharper the closer it is to the object.

Materials

![Images](images/f0130-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #1 round brush and Yellow Ochre thinned with glazing medium, draw the outline of the lemon and the leaves.

![Images](images/f0130-03.jpg)

**2** Block in the colour of the lemon, using a #4 filbert brush and Cadmium Yellow Medium. Add Mars Black to the yellow to create a dark olive green, and fill in the leaves.

![Images](images/f0131-01.jpg)

**3** On the left side of the lemon, apply Yellow Ochre with the #1 round brush, gradually adding Cadmium Yellow Medium as you move towards the more brightly lit part. Add more Cadmium Yellow Medium and a tiny bit of Unbleached Titanium to the green mix from step 2 and paint the parts of the leaves that twist up towards the light.

![Images](images/f0131-02.jpg)

**4** Yellow and violet are complementary colours, so here we’re using violet for the background. Mix Unbleached Titanium, Napthol Red, Ultramarine Blue and a tiny bit of Titanium White; the Unbleached Titanium desaturates the violet colour, while the Titanium White lightens it. Using a #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the lemon and leaves as you do so. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0131-03.jpg)

**5** Switch to the #4 filbert brush. Add more Mars Black to the green mix from step 2 and use this to darken parts of the leaves and paint the stem. Paint the cast shadows in a mix of Napthol Red, Ultramarine Blue and a little Unbleached Titanium. Start each shadow close to the object; as you get farther away, wipe your brush, dip it in glazing medium and pull the colour outwards so that the edge of the shadow becomes softer. Add the highlights on the lemon in Titanium White mixed with a tiny amount of Cadmium Yellow Medium.

![Images](images/f0131-04.jpg)

The muted violet background enhances the bright yellow of the lemon without detracting from it. To practise desaturating colours, why not paint a series of fruit using complementary colours – a lime on a red background, an orange on blue, a plum on yellow?

### 47 Translucency and transparency:  
Butterscotch sweet

The secret to painting transparent objects or materials such as cellophane is to use very thin layers of paint so that the underlying colour shows through. Start by painting whatever’s behind the transparent material, then glaze a very, very thin layer of white or grey over it, allowing some of the background to remain visible. Finally, add the highlights on top. Translucent objects, like the sweet in this exercise, require a slightly different approach. The sweet is obviously more solid than the cellophane it’s wrapped in, so the paint is applied a little more thickly – but it still needs to look luminous, so you need to use a colour that is more transparent. Not all manufacturers provide this information on the paint tube label, so it’s something you have to experiment with and learn for yourself.

Materials

![Images](images/f0132-02.jpg)

**1** In the centre of the canvas, using a #6 flat brush, paint an oval, or ellipse, in Cadmium Yellow Medium. Mix a dull orange-red from Napthol Red and Cadmium Yellow Medium and paint the background, cutting out the shape of the sweet as you do so.

![Images](images/f0132-03.jpg)

**2** When the sweet is dry, brush a very thin, transparent glaze of the background colour over the top left of the sweet, using a #4 bright brush. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0133-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a thin glaze of very pale grey from Unbleached Titanium, a tiny bit of Mars Black and lots of glazing medium. Using the #6 flat brush, put in a couple of rough triangles to begin to indicate the shape of the plastic wrapper, keeping the coverage uneven so that some of the underlying colour shows through.

![Images](images/f0133-02.jpg)

**4** Mix a shadow colour from Napthol Red and Mars Black. Using a #4 filbert brush, paint the cast shadow under the sweet, adding more Napthol Red as you move away from the sweet. Apply a little of the same colour to the wrapper, so that it looks as if you can see some of the cast shadow through the transparent cellophane.

![Images](images/f0133-03.jpg)

**5** Mix a very thin glaze of Titanium White, the tiniest bit of Mars Black and lots of glazing medium. Using the #4 filbert brush on its side, make a series of long, thin, slightly curving strokes over the wrapper for the highlights, taking care not to cover the underlying layers completely. (The #1 round brush would not hold enough pigment to create this kind of long stroke.) Add a few brighter highlights in pure Titanium White.

![Images](images/f0133-04.jpg)

The bright highlights applied in the last step indicate the crinkles in the wrapper: the light hits the flat top plane of the crinkles and falls off on the sides. Try this with different-coloured backgrounds and sweets, and make a note of how transparent each colour is.

### 48 Painting a backlit subject:  
Thistle

What do you see when you look at a backlit subject? Detail in the subject itself is often subdued because the light behind it is so intense, forming a halo effect that dazzles our eyes – so start with that halo and everything else will fall into place much more easily. Exploit the colour of the canvas, too. Here, I toned the canvas in a pale, off-white coat of Unbleached Titanium, which immediately gives a feeling of luminosity and will form the halo of light around the thistle. I then applied a Yellow Ochre mix to cut out the thistle shape from the background; this creates a feeling of warmth, as the thistle spines are bathed in the golden glow of the sun. With this particular subject, it was also important to use short, directional brushstrokes to convey the texture of the thistle head and flowers, and to leave some of the halo showing through; this, too, increases the sense of luminosity.

Materials

![Images](images/f0134-02.jpg)

**1** Using a #6 flat brush, tone the canvas in Unbleached Titanium. Mix a warm yellow from Yellow Ochre and Unbleached Titanium. Using a #6 flat brush, paint the background, cutting out the shape of the thistle as you do so. Use the same colour to paint the thistle shape in the centre, leaving a gap between the two to create a halo where the toned canvas shows through. Allow to dry.

![Images](images/f0134-03.jpg)

**2** Mix a dull red from Napthol Red, Burnt Sienna and a little Yellow Ochre. Using a #4 bright brush, paint over the background, this time cutting out the shape of the thistle spikes just outside the original halo so that a little of the Yellow Ochre from step 1 shows through to create a secondary luminous halo.

![Images](images/f0135-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a deep, dark green from Mars Black and Cadmium Yellow Medium. Using a #1 round brush, paint the stem and the bulbous part of the flower head; for the latter, use short, directional strokes that radiate outwards, leaving some gaps so that the underlying Yellow Ochre shows through.

![Images](images/f0135-02.jpg)

**4** Mix violet from Napthol Red, Ultramarine Blue and Titanium White. Using a #1 round brush, apply short, directional brushstrokes for the fluffy-textured flower head, adding more Ultramarine to the mix for the cooler bottom half and more Napthol Red for the warmer top half. Again, leave some of the Yellow Ochre showing through, both to convey the individual petals and to increase the sense of luminosity.

![Images](images/f0135-03.jpg)

**5** Darken the base of the thistle flowers with a stronger purple mix. With a mix of Cadmium Yellow Medium and Unbleached Titanium, establish a few more thistle spikes pushing out into the background to add to the feeling of luminosity.

![Images](images/f0135-04.jpg)

Detail in the subject itself is subdued, but the halo effect of light shining around the edge of the subject creates a lovely atmospheric painting.

### 49 Creating a sense of scale:  
Canyon vista

We’re all familiar with the basic principle of linear perspective – the way that things appear smaller the farther away they are – but there are other ways of conveying a sense of scale and distance. The first is colour temperature: objects in the foreground appear warmer and more intense than those in the distance. In this scene, for example, the foreground oranges and blues are pumped up and highly saturated, while those in the background are cooler and paler. The second is that texture and detail become less pronounced with distance. This project is also about abstracting a complex scene into a much simpler pattern. Even though the subject is a vast panorama, you can reduce it to basic shapes and blocks of colour, even moving towards abstraction if you so choose.

Materials

![Images](images/f0136-02.jpg)

**1** Mix a warm orange from Napthol Red, a little Cadmium Yellow Medium and tiny bit of Yellow Ochre. Using a #4 filbert brush, apply it over the land area, leaving the sky area untouched. Work from top to bottom, adding more Napthol Red as you move towards the foreground. Let dry.

![Images](images/f0136-03.jpg)

**2** Repeat the process, using blue for the shadow areas of the canyon walls. For the foreground shadow, add Burnt Sienna to Ultramarine Blue to neutralize and darken it. The shaded canyon walls in the left foreground are pure Ultramarine. As you work back add Unbleached Titanium, progressively lightening and cooling the colour. This has the effect of pushing the cliffs back in the scene. Add Titanium White for the sky area.

![Images](images/f0137-01.jpg)

**3** Mix a purple-pink from Napthol Red, a little Cadmium Yellow Medium and some Unbleached Titanium. Still using the #4 filbert brush, scumble this over part of the middle distance, varying the proportions of the colours in the mix.

![Images](images/f0137-02.jpg)

**4** For the trees, mix a dark green from Mars Black and Cadmium Yellow Medium, adding more black for the shadow sides. Glaze thinned down Burnt Sienna over the foreground shadow to warm it up a bit. Build up form on the foreground canyon with the orange mix from step 1, adding Unbleached Titanium for the most brightly lit parts.

![Images](images/f0137-03.jpg)

**5** Using a #1 round brush, put in some small trees in the middle distance to help create a sense of scale. However, it’s important not to get caught up in too much detail, especially on such a small canvas. Refine the shadow areas, using the same blue mixes as in step 2 and making the foreground shadows warmer, darker and more saturated.

![Images](images/f0137-04.jpg)

Practise painting perspective with other panoramic vistas, such as an Alpine mountain range, where the colour contrasts are likely to be less extreme than in this canyon scene. Then try the same approach with a flatter, but equally expansive, landscape such as an African plain.

### 50 Tonalism:  
Landscape with setting sun

Tonalism was an art movement that emerged in the US in the 1870s, and was characterized by muted and limited colour palettes. The tonalists were interested in capturing the effects of light – particularly moonlight or the diffused light of dawn or dusk. Large areas that are very close in value are set against small areas of higher contrast. Hazy, atmospheric landscapes were the most common subject matter. In this scene, the land forms a stark silhouette – although if you look closely you will see that it is not a solid black: instead, warm neutrals (browns, greys, green-tinged darks) intermingle. The Yellow Ochre underpainting imparts an overall glow and the small area of the setting sun and its reflection in the river below cut through the surrounding dark tones like a knife.

Materials

![Images](images/f0138-02.jpg)

**1** Mix Yellow Ochre with a tiny bit of Burnt Sienna and glazing medium. Using a #6 flat brush, roughly brush this colour over the whole canvas and let dry. Now mix Burnt Sienna and a touch of Yellow Ochre with glazing medium. Using a #4 filbert brush, paint the silhouette of the horizon line and trees. Keep your brushstrokes very loose and textural.

![Images](images/f0138-03.jpg)

**2** Over the top two-thirds of the canvas, apply a mix of Yellow Ochre, Burnt Sienna and a little Mars Black with the #4 filbert, leaving space where the setting sun will be. Add the same colour to the reflection of the sky in the river, leaving space in the centre where the setting sun will be reflected. Also put a little bit of the colour into the trees.

![Images](images/f0139-01.jpg)

**3** Paint over the land in a mix of Mars Black with small amounts of Burnt Sienna and Cadmium Yellow Medium, to shift the colour towards green, using loose, vertical strokes to echo the direction in which the trees and grasses grow. Mix a bluish grey from Mars Black, Unbleached Titanium, Ultramarine Blue and Yellow Ochre and brush this over the top part of the sky and into the reflection. Note how this grey is very similar in value to the dark olive mix from step 2.

![Images](images/f0139-02.jpg)

**4** Continue to darken the land and refine the sky; note how the trees on the horizon line now appear backlit and contrast dramatically with the glow of the evening sky. In terms of value, the top two-thirds of the canvas form a unified, harmonious space that will contrast with the setting sun and its reflection.

![Images](images/f0139-03.jpg)

**5** Continue to develop the sky with the same colours as before, smoothing out the paint but still keeping some texture. For the sun and its reflection, mix Titanium White with tiny amounts of Cadmium Yellow Medium and Yellow Ochre, and apply with a #1 round brush.

![Images](images/f0139-04.jpg)

Experiment with the position of the horizon line, moving it up or down and seeing what effect that has on the mood. You could also try changing the colour temperature, perhaps by making the clouds warmer (by adding Napthol Red, for example), but avoid too much saturated colour.

## Glossary

**Acrylic** 1. A polymer resin (type of plastic) derived from petrochemicals that takes many forms, including Perspex sheets and ‘woolly’ sweaters. 2. A type of water-soluble paint where pigments are suspended in an acrylic polymer resin binder that gives the paint workability and adhesion. It dries to a hard, durable, waterproof plastic film.

**Alla prima** A painting made all in one go, without underpainting.

**Canvas** 1. A textile, which may be cotton, linen, hemp, sacking, calico or sailcloth, that is stretched over a frame to make a surface to paint on. 2. Any surface to which paint is applied: textile, wood, board. 3. The painting as a work in progress.

**Chroma** Chromatic colour, colourful, made of colour. Describes the intensity of a hue, and the saturation of pigment.

**Complementaries** Colours opposite each other on the colour wheel. Placed next to each other, each has maximum intensity; mixed together, they cancel each other out.

**Composition** The arrangement of all the structural elements within a painting, including positive and negative shapes, horizon lines and diagonals, highlights and shadows, and colour patterns.

**Cool colours** The blue/green/violet side of the colour wheel. They appear to recede towards the back of the picture plane.

**Cutting out** The process of painting the whole canvas in a colour that’s very close to that of the main subject and then defining the shape of the subject by painting the background around it, so that the subject is ‘cut out’ from the background. Also known as painting the ‘negative space’.

**Dyad, Triad, Tetrad** Colour schemes built from two, three or four colours equally spaced around the colour wheel.

**Glaze** 1. Transparent colour thinly overlaid on another colour, modifying its hue or giving it more depth. 2. An acrylic mixing medium that adds transparency and spreading ability to paints; can be matt or gloss.

**Granulation** Particles of pigment separating in over-diluted paint; a grainy effect.

**Grisaille** An underpainting in grey tones that establishes the structure and values of a painting.

**Hot/warm colours** The red/orange/yellow side of the colour wheel. They appear to advance towards the foreground.

**Hue** 1. A particular colour. 2. The name of a colour.

**Imprimatura** A first, dilute coat of paint over the primer to ‘break the ice’ of a too brilliant, unsympathetic white surface.

**Key** A high-key colour scheme is bright, hot, saturated; a low-key scheme is more neutral.

**Knock back** To mix tiny amounts of the complementary into a colour to quieten it.

**Local colour** The actual hues on the surface of an object, perhaps reflected off other objects nearby.

**Maquette** Developed sketch used as a ‘blueprint’ for an artwork.

**Medium** (plural: mediums) 1. A binder or vehicle for pigment; a substance that modifies the handling properties of paint. 2. (plural: media) The broad type of drawing or painting material, such as oils or acrylic.

**Negative space** The spaces in a painting between, around or beyond the objects. These areas are part of the picture plane and also have to be painted, so make them shapely.

**Opaque** The opposite of transparent; fully opaque paint will obliterate what is underneath it.

**Optical** Effects that happen within the viewer’s eye.

**Palette** 1. An artist’s selection of colours. 2. The object on which the paints are laid out ready for work. 3. A surface on which paints are mixed together with a palette knife.

**Pictorial colour** The artist’s choice of colours to interpret the actual colours of objects; not necessarily lifelike.

**Picture plane** The two-dimensional surface that is the painting itself, regardless of pictorial illusions.

**Pigment** Dry, ground and powdered colour that is mixed into a medium such as acrylic polymer resin to make a chromatic paint. There are thousands of natural and synthetic pigments available, all of which behave differently.

**Plein air** French term for painting outdoors; alfresco in Italian.

**Primary colours** Yellow, red and blue – the colours that can’t be made by mixing others.

**Retarder** An acrylic mixing medium formulated to slow down drying times.

**Saturation** The purity and strength of a colour. Paint as it comes from the tube is at maximum saturation; once other hues are added, saturation decreases.

**Secondary colours** Green, orange and violet – made by mixing two primaries.

**Shade** 1. Darker value of a hue to which some ‘black’ has been added. 2. Any variation on a basic hue.

**Staining power** A pigment’s ability to transfer colour.

**Tertiary colours** 1. The six colours between primaries and secondaries on the wheel, named thus: yellow-green, yellow-orange and so on. 2. ‘Browns’ (russet, citrine and olive) made by mixing all three primaries in different proportions.

**Tint** A light value of a colour, made by adding a little of the hue to white.

**Tone** See Value. May refer to the lighter half or the whole range of values.

**Value** How light or dark a colour is, relative to its surroundings.

**Viscosity** Relative fluidity or thickness of the paint. Low is runny, high is sticky.
