## 3. Clear signals through stories, specialized language, and symbols.

Strong culture organizations typically are very clear in communicating how they expect people to behave, sometimes even signaling that the organization is not for everyone, and perhaps only special people will succeed (e.g., “The few. The proud. The Marines.”). These messages are relentlessly communicated through stories that provide vivid illustrations of employees whose attitudes and behavior exemplify the culture. They celebrate these individuals and often make heroes of them. Sometimes stories are told by leaders themselves, although it is also possible that cultural messages are spread by employees, customers, and other surrogates of leaders.[26] In addition, leaders and employees may use certain phrases, slogans, or terms that are special to the organization and reinforce membership and inclusion (e.g., “Googlers,” “All for one and one for all”). Further, symbols or symbolic actions can signal aspects of a culture. For example, imagine a company headquarters building that includes top management team members, human resource (HR) specialists, and customer service employees. What signal would be sent about the culture if top management members were located on the top floor, while HR and customer service employees were on lower floors? Alternatively, what would be the signal if the top management members were on the first or a middle floor, while HR and customer service employees were on the top floor? In short, even the layout of employees and units within physical buildings can be symbolic of a culture.

- 4. Selection and socialization.

Strategic leaders in strong cultures like to use selection and socialization procedures to help ensure that the culture gets continually reinforced. After all, the old adage, “the people make the place,” is true.[27] And if the people do make the place, it is essential that they be properly selected with the culture in mind, and then mechanisms put into place to learn or get socialized to the firm’s specific norms and values. For example, strong culture companies (e.g., Google, Southwest Airlines, Zappos, Bridgewater, and Cypress) often have multiple steps in their recruitment processes, and two things are common to such processes. First, new employees are selected based as much (or more) on their fit to the firm’s cultural values, as compared to their technical skills or experience for the job. Second, rather than just HR specialists involved in selection, actual team members or peers will get involved (e.g., existing team members interview prospective candidates). Procedures of this nature are designed to both help screen people for cultural fit, but also to give the applicant a chance to voluntarily exit. For example, toward the end of the Zappos training program, new employees are offered $3,000 to quit, ensuring that those who stay are doing it for more than a paycheck. In 2015, Zappos had more than 250,000 applications for 250 openings.[28] We should note that selection and socialization may be especially important for the lower-level management ranks in a firm. If a strategic leader cannot count on lower-level leaders to help build and shape culture in a unified direction, the chances of forming a strong culture that matches the vision of the strategic leader will be minimal.

Training is a key mechanism by which both skill development and socialization are achieved. However, getting back to the elements of competitive advantage that are presented at the beginning of this chapter, training should not be viewed as a substitute for culture; rather it’s simply another mechanism to help engrain culture. Let’s take the example of customer service. Values and beliefs pertaining to customer service can, over time, become part of a culture based on other mechanisms for leading culture that we mention in this section, such as day-to-day leader actions and communication. If customer service is not continually engrained or reinforced in a culture through these other mechanisms, customer service training is not as likely to have a positive impact on employee behavior. In short, training per se should not be viewed as a substitute for culture.

- 5. A carefully aligned reward system.

Finally, leaders in strong culture organizations ensure that the reward system is precisely aligned with the expected behavior. Clearly, money is an important motivator, but it is not a very good way to shape culture. Oftentimes, monetary rewards are too infrequent to reinforce specific behaviors. Instead, leaders in strong culture firms are more likely to use recognition, approval, and status to signal compliance with the norms. Sometimes these symbolic rewards (e.g., employee of the month, certificates of appreciation) can seem trivial, but when they are awarded with sincerity, they can act as powerful reinforcement of the culture. The big danger in many organizations is that the formal reward system (e.g., money and promotion) may not be aligned with the desired behaviors. When leaders claim, for example, that teamwork or innovation is important, but employees see others who are not team players getting ahead, or if those who try to innovate and fail are punished, then they quickly form the belief that their leaders are either out of touch with reality or, worse, manipulative. In short, cultural expectations will not become clear, and employees will become demotivated, if leaders actually reward A, while professing to hope for B.[29]

In sum, these five mechanisms are based on the idea that to shape culture and make it strong, leaders need to send strong signals about what attitudes and behaviors are expected. By consistently sending the same messages about expected behaviors, getting people involved, providing vivid illustrations of the culture, and visibly selecting, socializing, and rewarding those who live the values, leaders help employees understand what it takes to succeed. Over time, these expectations can become embedded in the organization such that new employees quickly understand what is expected of them.

## A Case Example

The key from a strategic leader's perspective is to send clear signals to organizational members. Figure 12.3 provides a broad outline for how this is done. First, the beliefs and values of strategic leaders can act as a basis for their actions. At Bridgewater Associates, the largest hedge fund in the United States, CEO Ray Dalio believes in what he calls "radical transparency."[80] He wants everyone in the company to consistently be totally honest with each other, even if it is uncomfortable telling the truth. Therefore, all meetings are videotaped and made available to all employees. New employees are often taken aback at how direct the criticism is, but Dalio believes that being completely honest is the only way to have an ideal meritocracy. His values and beliefs have shaped his actions.

Second, Dalio and his team constantly communicate to others what is important and how they are expected to behave. We know from research that people are likely to believe a message when it is communicated again and again. When strategic leaders constantly reinforce the signal that certain values and actions are important, we begin to understand that they are serious. Jack Welch, a famous former CEO of General Electric used to say that if leaders were going to be effective at managing culture, they had to do two things: (1) be relentless in sending the same message, and (2) be repetitive—that is, repeat the same message again and again.

If a strategic leader is relentless in signaling what the culture is all about, and if employees can see others being rewarded (or punished) for behaving appropriately, then norms will emerge that tell newcomers that certain behaviors and attitudes are expected. Over time, these norms become embedded. For example, at Bridgewater, employees use iPads to constantly evaluate each other and their supervisors. These evaluations are fed into an employee's permanent record and made public and reviewed. Designated managers who are known as “captains” constantly monitor the culture to ensure compliance with the principles. Dalio acknowledges that his culture is not for everyone, but he believes that it is core to the success of the company.

What Bridgewater and other strong culture companies have in common is that their strategic leaders are very clear in communicating what attitudes and behavior are needed to fit in and be successful. What they have also done is to ensure that the HR systems in the organization are aligned, including the types of people who they seek to hire, how they recruit, how new employees are trained and developed, what behaviors are rewarded, and the types of people who are promoted. The combination of clear leader signaling and aligned HR systems results in clarity about the expected behaviors.
