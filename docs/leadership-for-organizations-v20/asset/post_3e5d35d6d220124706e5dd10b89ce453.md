## Conclusion

As we have seen in this chapter, organizational culture can be a source of competitive advantage (e.g., Zappos) or, when it is not aligned with strategy, a competitive disadvantage (e.g., IBM in the early 1990s). It can also be a source of pride and motivation among employees, or a source of cynicism and turnover. Getting the culture right (i.e., creating or shaping culture) is a fundamental task of a strategic leader. As we have shown, if leaders think about culture as shared norms and values, and a pattern of behavior that is reinforced by people and systems, it can be created, shaped, and even changed over time. In the next chapter, we turn our attention to organizational change, including the possibility of significantly reshaping culture.

### 13.1 Why the Leading of Change Has Become So Important

What do the following firms have in common: IBM (computers), Ingram (electronics distribution), Johnson & Johnson (pharmaceuticals); Nokia (mobile phones), Vivendi (media), and Toyota (automobiles)? The answer may not be so obvious because these firms come from different countries and compete in different industries. But there is something that ties them all together: these are all old companies (average age is 135 years old) that are alive today only because in the face of the need for dramatic change, they had leaders who helped them move into new businesses. Each of these firms began in a different business and now competes in a new one. Now think about the following companies: Polaroid, DEC, U.S. Steel, Smith Corona, Kodak, and GM.
