# leader-member exchange (LMX)

The manner in which relationships between leader and followers forms or evolves; how a follower assumes roles; how the leader acts or responds towards the follower, and the “chemistry” that occurs.

How exactly do these relationships form or evolve? Much of the answer to this question lies in how a follower assumes roles, how the leader acts or responds towards the follower, and in the ‘chemistry’ that occurs. In other words, when it comes to leader-member relationships or what is known as leader-member exchange (LMX), it definitely “takes two to tango.” to quote an old adage. To some people, this may seem counterintuitive, since they might feel that leadership is all about the leader, and what he or she does in relation to followers. However, our premise in this chapter, as well as the next, is that followers also play a key role in LMX.

The main idea of LMX is that leader-follower relationships are based on exchanges. But what exactly do leaders and followers exchange? In other words, what are the “currencies” of exchange?[1] Essentially, there are two types of exchanges: (1) tangible, and (2) intangible. Tangible exchanges are in line with what we traditionally consider “currencies” to be all about. They are economic or productivity-based. The most basic exchange is one’s salary for satisfactory performance. However, tangible exchanges can be a bit more passive in the form of simply being left alone if one is performing satisfactorily. For example, a follower may simply prefer to be left alone or avoided by the boss if that follower is performing adequately, rather than being continually questioned, being the subject of abuse or ridicule, and so forth. In other words, such avoidance could be seen by some followers as a positive exchange—or at least a lack of negative exchange.

Tangible exchanges form the foundation of a relationship between leader and follower, and if missing or perceived to be unfair, could certainly cause problems. However, exchanges of this nature do not account for the full scope of possibilities with regard to leader-follower relationships, and they do not fully explain the long-term nature of relationships. Indeed, not all exchanges involve monetary currencies, or things that are readily translatable into monetary value. Intangible exchanges are also important to consider. An intangible exchange pertains to things like friendship (or friendliness), acceptance of mutual responsibility, loyalty, and trust. For example, trust is a powerful factor in any relationship, including those between leaders and followers. Without trust, the leader will feel a need to closely monitor the actions and behaviors of the follower, which takes a lot of time and tends to generate anxiety on the part of both leader and follower. Likewise, the follower will only put forth enough effort to satisfy the “contractual” nature of their relationship.[2] Moreover, the follower will not be motivated to be creative or find solutions to vexing problems, for fear of failure or reprimand. However, if trust exists, the relationship is more relaxed, and followers are likely to be motivated to put forth extra effort and creativity, and accept greater responsibility.

In terms of the characterizations of LMX, one of two basic directions are likely to occur. These different directions are commonly referred to as in-groups vs. out-groups, and they are depicted in Figure 3.1.[3] First, a follower could become part of a leader’s in-group, which is based largely on the type of intangible exchanges described above. Leaders embrace followers into their in-groups for different reasons, but one key reason is that a follower will demonstrate early on that he or she can perform well and take on increasing responsibility. This would obviously take pressure off of a leader to have to closely monitor the follower, which given the time demands of leaders, is always a welcomed relief. Alternatively, the leader might embrace a follower because he or she has similar values, interests, or background. For example, a leader who enjoys playing golf as a pastime will naturally take favorably to a follower who also plays golf. The two individuals will likely spend more time together, perhaps as compared to time spent by the leader with other followers, which then reinforces in-group membership. Keep in mind that such “membership” is informal, and not discussed or perhaps not even recognized by either party. The upshot of being in the leader’s in-group is that the follower will enjoy better work assignments or conditions (e.g., hours worked), receive positive coaching and feedback, and be able to provide a lot of input into decision-making and problem-solving.
