### Learning Objectives

- 1. Describe how leaders can have different bases of power, and how their power affects the manner in which leaders influence others.
- 2. Summarize what charismatic and transformational leadership are all about, and how they pertain to inspiration.
- 3. Explain how organizational missions can vary in ways that are more or less inspiring and sustainable.
- 4. Identify the elements of effective leader vision.
- 5. Diagram how leader values, inspiring/visionary leadership, and likely firm outcomes go together.

In many ways, the essence of leadership is to be able to inspire. When one inspires another individual, that person influences in a special way. The influence is not born out of fear, coercion, one's position, or monetary exchange. Instead, it occurs for positive and oftentimes very intangible reasons. Have you ever really felt inspired by another individual? If so, what was it about that person that seemed to inspire you, and what actions or behaviors on your part resulted from the inspiration?

In this chapter, we will unlock some of the mystique surrounding inspirational and visionary leadership. In so doing, we will also consider a key way that leaders inspire, specifically through vision. We will further consider some popular concepts associated with inspiration, including transformational leadership. And we will address a more controversial way of understanding leader inspiration, namely charisma. In addition, we will explain how mission or sense of purpose forms the basis of a new vision, and how a very narrow, product or economic-based mission can limit future vision. Finally, we will consider exactly what constitutes effective leader vision.

#### Hard Versus Soft Power

> **hard power**
> Coercive, formal/legitimate, and reward/resources sources of power that tend to be derived on the basis of one's position in an organizational hierarchy.

On the left side of the figure, we see the bases of power.[1] On top of the dashed line are three bases of what can be termed **hard power**: (1) coercive, (2) formal/legitimate, and (3) reward/resources. In organizations, these hard sources of power tend to be derived on the basis of one's position in the hierarchy. Based on a manager's position, that person could coerce people to perform better, or else those individuals would face punishment or reprimand (e.g., reduced salary, worse job assignments, lowered job security). On a more positive basis, hard power can be used to provide rewards and resources (e.g., pay increases, better training or equipment) for desired behavior. Coercion is featured in red, since in many ways it is the antithesis of what leadership is all about. This is not to say that coercion should never be considered by a manager. However, its overuse will only result in either resistance or a feeling of helplessness on the part of followers, neither of which is a good thing.
