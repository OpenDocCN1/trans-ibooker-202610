The companies that were able to adapt and change had leaders who were able to find new business opportunities and ways of making necessary change happen, while the companies that failed had leaders who were unable to adapt in the face of the need for change.

We all know that we live in an era of unprecedented change. But what is interesting and important is that recent research has documented two big insights that are critical for managers to understand. First, the evidence is that the pace of change is accelerating.[1] The penetration of new technologies like the internet, mobility, and artificial intelligence is happening faster than at any point in history. The average automobile today has more computing power than the Apollo mission that first landed Neil Armstrong on the moon in 1969. Netscape, the first widely used web browser, was introduced in 1993. Twenty-five years later, more than three billion people were using the internet. The Flip Video camera first appeared in 2006 and was the hottest selling Christmas gift in 2007. Four years later, the company was essentially obsolete, replaced by the cell phone. These technological shifts have doomed great companies like Kodak (digital imaging), Blockbuster (video streaming), Borders (ebooks), the Chicago Tribune (online news), and Circuit City (online shopping).

But it is not only technological change that is disrupting business today. We are also seeing significant shifts in consumer preferences (communication, transportation), government regulation (banking, airlines), competition (off-shoring, low cost producers), and new business models (hospitality, transportation). For example, the rise of ride-sharing (Uber, Lyft, ZipCar) and autonomous vehicles combined with millennials' lower needs for owning a car are threatening automakers, and especially, taxi companies. The world-wide deregulation of the airline industry that has given rise to widespread availability of low-cost airlines has driven many airlines into bankruptcy and threatened other full-service carriers. Online banking and electronic payments are threatening banks and financial services companies. In the face of these changes, the evidence is also that companies are failing at an increasing rate. One estimate is that the average lifespan of a company in the Standard and Poor's 500 (large U.S. firms) is roughly twelve years.[2] In the face of change, large firms are failing at an increasing rate.

More recently we all witnessed the COVID-19 pandemic. Many "non-essential" businesses were forced to shut down for extended periods of time. Others that managed to stay open were only allowed to do so with dramatic changes, such as social distancing rules for employees and customers and limited capacities. Still others have permanently closed.

It is certainly true that in many instances, reasons unrelated to leadership practices can be blamed for business failures or closures. However, in other instances, the reasons for business failures go beyond technology, diseases, and so forth. But instead, they include the inability of company leaders to lead change. An analysis of corporate failures has shown that oftentimes it is not so much the case that firms have missed the shifts in technologies or markets, but instead, they had leaders who failed to adapt to the shifts fast enough. For example, for almost fifty years Smith Corona was the world's largest typewriter company. They failed when manual typewriters were replaced by word processors and personal computers. Ironically, Smith Corona had one of the first word processors. However, their leaders ignored this and focused on making money from the typewriter business, rather than aggressively promoting word processing. The leaders at Kodak knew that digital imaging threatened film sales, and although the company invented the first digital camera, they focused on film sales until it was too late. The leaders at Blockbuster waited until it was too late to enter video rentals by mail. The evidence is that these failures stemmed not from a lack of understanding of the new technologies, but that the leaders of these companies were unable, unwilling, or lacked the courage or insight to help their companies adapt to these changes. The overall goal of this chapter is to help you understand why senior leaders seem to find it so difficult to manage change and to provide you with several frameworks and suggestions for how you as a leader, now or in the future, might think about leading organizational change.
