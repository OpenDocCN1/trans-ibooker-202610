
Also, and especially in his early years at ASU, Crow had a reputation for personally working tirelessly in the determined pursuit of goals and change efforts, even if such change was controversial in nature. Combined with his personal intelligence or brilliance, his efforts served as a personal role model of energetic, hard work. For example, stories abound of such things as the very few hours of sleep he obtained per night in lieu of hours devoted to his job. Given the high standards that he seemed to set for himself, it should not be surprising that he set high standards for ASU units and individuals. In terms of effort and devotion, it would seem clear that, at least at the beginning of his tenure, Crow led by example. In addition, in the early years of his tenure at ASU, Crow became known for attempting to spread his vision widely through off-campus speeches to the community, as well as on-campus town hall meetings.

Large public universities tend to be stodgy with long-term, ingrained problems. As such, they become like large ocean liners in terms of resisting change, not being entrepreneurial or innovative, and not being able to change course. However, Crow has shown little hesitancy to pursue quick changes or take advantage of potential opportunities. A term that he has coined in the pursuit of change is to take action “post-haste.” Especially in the early years of his administration, he used such language to emphasize the need for quick change, or else the university might suffer dire consequences. Examples of relatively quick, yet major changes or opportunities that he has personally spearheaded include:

- Tuition increases to make ASU more in line with that of comparable universities
- Support gained from the cities of Scottsdale and Phoenix to subsidize ASU growth
- Job cuts and furloughs in the 2009 recession
- Joint venture for online education with Starbucks
- Acquisition of the Thunderbird School of Global Management

Section 5 suggests that a favorable aspect of Crow’s leadership is his apparent not succumbing to internal politics in his decision-making. In general, major decisions or actions appear to be taken based on principle or vision, rather than in accordance with internal politics that are often in play at universities. On the other hand, he has demonstrated a good understanding of the external political environment surrounding the university, and in the process, has garnered a high degree of support from state and local governments, as well as large donors and other benefactors to the university—even the President of the United States.

Finally, Crow himself has received honors and accolades. For example, he was one of four recipients of the 2013 Academic Leadership award bestowed by the Carnegie Corporation of New York. The award is designed to honor university presidents who show such things as commitment to access on the part of potential students, international engagement, and the formation of partnerships with local communities. One feature of the award is that it is accompanied by a $500K grant to the recipient’s institution (i.e., ASU) to be allocated at the discretion of the recipient to further his/her academic priorities.

In sum, there are clearly favorable aspects regarding Crow’s approach to his leadership role in fostering change at ASU. Aside from stylistic issues, it is difficult to challenge the notion that the tangible accomplishments or outcomes mentioned above are largely due to his own ideas, efforts, and force of will. Nevertheless, problematic aspects of his leadership have also been identified by people within the university, as well as those who are external.

## Controversial Aspects of Crow’s Leadership

The controversial nature of Crow’s approach to leading change at ASU is captured by the mixed review that he has received in the media. On the one hand, articles describe him as charismatic, visionary, courageous, and bold. On the other hand, a more negative or dark side has also been portrayed in the media. To be fair, many of the more negative articles have come from a single source—a Phoenix-based tabloid-style newspaper, the New Times, and they were mostly earlier in his administration. For example, in a series of three articles in 2004 and 2007,[27] this newspaper used flamboyantly-titled articles to portray what it believes to be Crow's dark side as a leader in terms of instilling fear such that employees felt intimidated to not speak up or challenge a various changes. The media has also criticized Crow for attempting to make ASU operate like a business corporation, although Crow might argue that this was necessary because of budgetary demands. Some additional controversial aspects are portrayed in Section 5.

- *First*, generally when there is talk or writing about the new vision at ASU, it is seen as Crow's vision, rather than a common, shared vision that he has tried to form with existing ASU faculty and administrators. To be fair, many people have bought into his vision. But for the most part, Crow's strategy for vision formation and implementation has not been to try to co-create or build shared vision, or a guiding coalition of support, among existing personnel. Rather, he has brought in personnel (e.g., mid to high-level administrators) who largely “manage” their respective units in a top-down manner, rather than “lead” in a more collaborative manner (i.e., both within units and across units). For example, although Crow has met with existing faculty members, lower-level administrators, and students to share his vision, it is not as clear that their own aspirations for the future of ASU, or doubts regarding Crow's vision and its implementation, have truly been taken into account.

- *Second,* Crow's relationship with students has been at best, shaky over the years. His early push for steadily increasing tuition may have had merit, but it has understandably caused some friction between himself and students. As another example, it seems pretty apparent that Crow has been determined to get rid of ASU's image as a party school. One action taken along those lines is to reduce the fraternity/sorority (i.e. “Greek”) presence on campus. Some students have argued that Crow's statements and actions toward Greek life have fostered a somewhat hostile climate toward students who maintain a Greek affiliation.[28] On the other hand, it could be argued that some actions on the part of Greek-affiliated students have warranted criticism, such as racially-distasteful behaviors at a Martin Luther King, Jr. theme party that was held by the Tau Kappa Epsilon fraternity in January of 2014. As a result of those behaviors, Crow directed the university to permanently sever ties with that fraternity.[29]

- *Third,* Crow has shown a tendency to not share information about major, forthcoming changes until they actually have been planned out and formally announced to the media. Examples include the potential spinning off of the West campus to form a separate university in 2004, the Starbucks initiative, and the more recent acquisition of the Thunderbird School of Global Management. In each of these instances, press releases of the pending changes came before actual discussions with, and involvement of, faculty and students.

Crow might argue that information was only provided to individuals on a “need to know” basis, although in line with principles from Chapter 6, some people might question such thinking. To be fair, a counter-argument to early disclosure of these initiatives could be made. Specifically, such disclosure might have only led to extensive rumors, inaccurate media coverage, and political maneuvering. But at the same time, the lack of early communication to, and involvement of, people who must ultimately implement change (e.g., faculty and lower-level administrators) can leave them with feelings of distrust or a lack of empowerment to act. In such a case, changes might not be implemented as efficiently or timely as might otherwise occur.

- *Fourth,* once a significant organizational or structural change is announced, Crow has shown a tendency to simply delegate the implementation to lower level administrators. This style has contributed to Crow being perceived as a leader who operates only at “30,000 feet.” While such delegation can make sense given the time demands of his job, it may also make sense to become personally involved at least to some degree in implementation efforts. This type of involvement would help to ensure that the implementation coincides with his vision of the university.
