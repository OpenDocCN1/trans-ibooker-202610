![](img/5a5fcd49e67d713bc6c99c1cc791987a_122_0.png)

A typical 360-degree feedback system will involve a survey administered to at least one source of individuals (e.g., followers) in addition to one's boss. It also commonly includes self-perceptions. The survey will oftentimes be focused on the types of leadership qualities and behaviors that are considered in this book. It can include rated items and/or space for write-in comments. The identity of the individuals, other than one's boss, who complete the survey remains anonymous in an attempt to solicit honest feedback. That feedback is cumulated and put into a feedback report, which is then delivered to the feedback recipient, typically by one's boss or an external coach/consultant through a coaching process. Thus, although 360-degree feedback is primarily related to feedback receiving on the part of leaders, it can also be pertinent to feedback giving if facilitated by a leader's boss. The feedback report can include numeric scores on rated items, as well as transcribed write-in comments. Regarding numeric scores, averages for a given survey item could be fed back, as well as frequencies (e.g., how many people rated the individual as 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 on a 5-point scale).

In theory, the basic idea of 360-degree feedback is that recipients will receive the feedback, analyze and carefully consider it, and then make attempts to improve their leader's behavior based on the feedback. For some recipients, this is indeed the case as shown in some research studies.[17] However, in many instances, the effects of 360-degree feedback are minimal at best. Two key reasons may account for potential lack of success with a 360-degree feedback process. First, recipients may have a bad attitude toward the feedback or have the type of performance prove goal orientation that was considered earlier in this chapter. For example, if some of the feedback comes from followers who are viewed simply as “subordinates,” people receiving the feedback who are in formal leadership positions may not think that it is important. And they may even feel that subordinates do not have the “right” to supply such feedback. As another example, the feedback recipient may have vastly overrated him or herself in relation to the ratings of others on various dimensions. Accentuating these problems is the fact that 360-degree feedback systems are often administered simply as a developmental add-on to the more formal, performance appraisal process. In other words, it is used just for leader development purposes alone. However, it may have no “teeth” in a particular organizational setting in terms of directly determining raises, promotions, and the ability to stay in (or be promoted to) a leadership role.

Second, feedback reports might be delivered to recipients without appropriate follow-up coaching, which can potentially be delivered by one's supervisor, or outsiders (e.g., HR specialists, consultants, and executive coaches). A key advantage of 360-degree feedback is that it provides data that is systematically collected, rather than feedback that is based on hearsay. Accordingly, these types of processes can provide very tangible data upon which coaching can proceed. Yet, there is no guarantee that coaching will indeed accompany a 360-degree feedback process. For example, one's supervisor might deliver the feedback report without actually helping the recipient to work through the feedback to determine what might be learned and changed, as well as why such change is important (e.g., to learn from the feedback in order to obtain higher level leadership positions). Since leadership and coaching represent a two-way street, if the feedback recipient has a negative attitude toward the process, it will be that much harder for the supervisor/coach to help guide the recipient. So for the process to be successful, a coach needs to help the recipient to learn from the feedback, and the recipient needs to be open to change based on the feedback.

Finally, even if coaching accompanies the feedback, if the feedback recipient does not take follow-up steps to improve, then the 360-degree feedback process is liable to be all for naught. One possible approach is for the feedback recipient to take just three or four items or issues from the data, and have a meeting with those who provided the feedback. The goal is to learn more about why those items/issues were rated relatively low, or how the recipient can improve. Obviously, the goal should not be to identify who said what in the survey, or to debate the validity of the survey process. Instead, the feedback recipient should simply listen, take notes, and ask constructive questions regarding how improvements might be made. In our own experience with 360-degree feedback, this follow-up step is crucial if feedback recipients are to learn and change their behavior.

In sum, while 360-degree feedback can help develop leaders in terms of the receiving aspect of leader communication, its implementation has challenges. The feedback can reveal blind spots that the leader did not realize, and if improved, could greatly enhance the leader's development. On the other hand, aspects of the leader, as well as the context within which the feedback is administered, all work to determine whether or not 360-degree feedback will be successful.

## Conclusion

The goal of this chapter has been to highlight the importance of feedback for leaders. Although we typically think of leaders as feedback givers, it is equally important to consider how effective leaders seek and receive feedback themselves. They do so in order to continuously learn and develop. We have emphasized how formal mechanisms or programs, such as coaching and 360-degree feedback, can be used to help leaders both receive and give feedback.
