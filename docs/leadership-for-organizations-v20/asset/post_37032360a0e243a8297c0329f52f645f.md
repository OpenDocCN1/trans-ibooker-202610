
Zappos emphasizes hiring only people who fit their culture, train all newcomers in the Zappos way, and they encourage all employees to always help their customers (internal or external). This means there are no limits to how long a customer service representative may stay on the phone with a customer (the longest customer call was almost six hours). By 2008, the company had more than $1 billion in sales. In more recent times, more than 75 percent of Zappos customers are repeat buyers, and the company has moved from selling just shoes into housewares and other clothing items. This customer loyalty stems directly from their culture.

Cypress Semiconductor. Beyond helping to execute vision, mission, and strategies, organizational culture can also be valuable in attracting, motivating and retaining employees. If a company has very strong norms and values, these can act as a signal to potential employees and help them decide on whether the firm is a good fit for them. Cypress Semiconductor is a firm that is sometimes described as “the Marines of Silicon Valley.”(21) It largely has an achievement culture that emphasizes very high levels of individual performance, great attention to detail, and complete transparency and openness. It does not emphasize collaboration (i.e., not a clan culture) or customers. Nevertheless, because the culture is so clear, Cypress can attract people who like this type of work environment, and they are able to screen out those who will not fit. Some people might find the Cypress culture too aggressive and restrictive, but for those who like the culture, Cypress is a great place to work.

## “Strong” Culture and Competitive Advantage

By being clear about norms and values, a strong culture can also be a great source of motivation for employees. If employees like the culture and what the organization stands for, they may identify with the firm; that is, they admire what the firm stands for and are proud to tell others that they work there. Further, they may internalize the norms and values of the company as their own; that is, their own norms and values of behavior are the same as those of the organization. If employees identify or internalize the norms and values of the organization, research would suggest that they are more likely to be highly motivated and willing to go the extra mile for the company.(22)

In sum, culture can be important for leaders and their organizations for multiple reasons. First, acting as a social control system, it can help (or hinder) the execution of strategy. Second, when an organization has a strong culture, it is better able to attract, motivate and retain the right types of employees and more likely to screen out those who will not provide a good fit. But overall, when culture is strong (i.e., tightly shared norms, beliefs, and values among organizational members), it can help create competitive advantage. Specifically, a strong culture can meet the requirements of competitive advantage in that it provides value, is not easily imitated by other firms, and is difficult to substitute with some other, non-rare resource.

With that said, we should also acknowledge that as suggested earlier, a strong or tight culture is not always a good thing. It is possible for a strong culture to encourage norms and values that are destructive for both the firm and its employees, as was the case with Enron a number of years ago.(23) Enron had a culture that was strong, but characterized in terms of unethical norms and values. Although these norms and values were widely shared by employees, the resulting actions and behaviors on their part did not result in competitive advantage in the long run. As another example, a firm may develop a strong culture over time that not only fails to include adaptability as a dimension, but actually includes norms and values that discourage flexibility and change. In a world that increasingly demands ongoing change on the part of firms, such strong cultures can be counter-productive. The following section will describe how leaders can use these insights to create and shape culture for competitive advantage.

If, as we have described, culture acts through normative and informational influence, thus guiding employees in terms of what to focus upon and how to act, then the strategic leader's job is to make sure that people get very clear signals telling them what is important and how to act. In this sense, it is all about what is known as a social construction; that is, the fundamental task of a strategic leader is to help employees interpret and make sense out of what they are doing and why they are doing it. This may sound a bit incomprehensible, but as we will see, it is actually very straightforward.

Think about this challenge from a new employee's perspective. Imagine that you have just joined a new company. What is likely to be on your mind? One of your first concerns is to figure out how you need to behave in order to fit in and be successful. How will you decide how to behave in order to be accepted by the group and be successful at your job? One thing for sure is that you are likely to watch other people, listen carefully to what senior leaders say and do, see who gets rewarded, and ask others for advice about what to do or what to avoid. It probably will not take long before you will start to get a feel for what the culture is all about. Should I show up early and work late? How should I dress? How should I treat other employees or customers/clients? Is it OK to cut corners? Can I disagree with my boss? Is it OK to make mistakes? Over time, the answers to these questions will become clear. As we have indicated, in stronger cultures, there will be greater clarity about how you will need to behave to fit in and be successful. In weaker cultures, the appropriate ways of acting will not be as clear.

It is also interesting to note that few people among the older guard of an organization (i.e., those with longer tenure) will be able to openly articulate the shared norms and values of the firm, if asked directly. Since much of culture, such as norms and values, is metaphorically under the surface, organizational veterans may not be able to articulate aspects of their organization's culture. They have lived that culture on a day-to-day basis. But just like fish in water, people learn to take their culture for granted.[24] The same way can be said about the types of societal-level cultures considered in Chapter 10 of this book.

### Mechanisms for Leading Cultures

If culture operates through norms and values, what can leaders do to shape and change them? Understanding that culture operates through norms and values, it turns out that there are five common mechanisms or levers that leaders in these organizations use. These levers are all based on the underlying psychology that when people are in settings where there are very clear expectations for the "right" way to behave, it is very difficult to not comply. In these circumstances, we typically have only two choices: (1) comply and behave as expected, and consequently, fit in and are accepted; or alternatively, we (2) do not behave as expected and, in most cases, will be excluded either psychologically or physically from the organization. These are the five key levers:

- 1. Day-to-day leader actions.

Most people want to fit in and be accepted by their group or organization. To do this, we pay attention to our leaders. We listen to what they say and watch what they do. In other words, we "boss watch." We're trying to figure out what they think is important so that we can be successful. We note where they spend their time, what questions they ask, what they like and dislike, and what they see as high priorities. We also note whether they are leading-by-example (versus being hypocritical or inconsistent) and leading-by-walking around. We use this information to decide how to act ourselves, what to pay attention to and what to ignore, and how to be seen as a good employee. Think of leaders as signal generators communicating to the organization, which in turn, helps employees interpret how they should act. When their signals are clear and consistent, employees quickly learn how to act. At Zappos, for example, the late CEO, Tony Hsieh, had no private office—just a regular cubicle in the midst of others. Leader communication and behavior represent the first, and perhaps most important, lever for leading culture.

- 2. Social activities and ceremonies.

Strong culture leaders typically encourage social activities and ceremonies to build ties among organizational members. They emphasize intrinsic as well as extrinsic rewards (being a part of the group is a reward itself), and use group approval to signal group membership.
