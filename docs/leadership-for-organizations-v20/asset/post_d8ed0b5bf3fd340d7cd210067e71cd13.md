
## 3.2 Political Savvy

Leaders influence followers through building and maintaining good relationships. But oftentimes the target of influence is not formal subordinates or direct reports. Instead, leaders may influence peers, customers/clients, suppliers, and even their own superiors through the formation and maintenance of good relationships.[6] Here are examples of why they need to influence these types of individuals:

- **Peers**—to gain information or cooperation that may be relevant to the team's and the leader's effectiveness.
- **Customers/Clients**—to ensure that customers or clients will be satisfied, thereby continuing their business with leader and his/her organization.
- **Suppliers**—to ensure that appropriate supplies or input into a production or service process for which the leader is responsible will be of high quality and delivered in a timely manner.
- **Superiors**—to ensure that the leader has necessary information and resources, and be part of the boss's in-group.

**political savvy**

The ability to effectively understand others at work, and to use such knowledge to influence others to act in ways that enhance one’s personal and/or organizational objectives.

The key to achieving these influence outcomes is political savvy, which involves building and maintaining relationships with each of the types of individuals mentioned above. Leaders do so by using their political savvy.[7] Gerald Ferris and colleagues defined **political savvy** in terms of "the ability to effectively understand others at work, and to use such knowledge to influence others to act in ways that enhance one's personal and/or organizational objectives."[8] Accordingly, influence is attained by building and maintaining relationships. Ferris and colleagues identified four components that go into political savvy: (1) social astuteness, (2) interpersonal influence, (3) networking ability, and (4) apparent sincerity.

First, social astuteness involves the ability to carefully observe others, especially in social situations. Individuals with such astuteness can accurately understand and interpret social interactions involving themselves and others. In addition, they are highly self-aware of themselves and how they come across to others. As an example, a socially astute leader would be very observant at social gatherings in terms of who is talking to whom (e.g., to whom people with the most formal power are speaking), who appears to be enjoying themselves vs. those who are bored, and so forth. This leader would also have a keen sense of how others at the social gathering are reacting to him/her.

Second, politically skilled individuals have a persuasive way of achieving interpersonal influence. Some of this influence is relatively clear and not-so-subtle, such as attempts to clarify *quid pro quo* arrangements. But at other times, the persuasion is subtler, such as coy attempts at ingratiation. Further, politically savvy leaders are adaptable or flexible to different targets of influence or contextual conditions. For example, such leaders may realize that some targets of influence need very clear and overt *quid pro quo* understanding, while others would find such attempts at influencing to be manipulative and repugnant.

Third, networking and political savvy go hand-in-hand. Leaders with political savvy understand the nature of networks, and how through networking, they can better position both themselves and their organizations for success. In other words, they recognize how people in networks hold valuable assets and connections for getting things done. In particular, leaders may serve as, or recognize others who serve as, “brokers” in a network.[9] Brokers are individuals who connect other individuals in a network. For instance, a leader may recognize how a particular customer could connect the leader and his/her team to another potential customer.

Further, politically savvy leaders recognize the difference between *strong* and *weak* ties.[10] Strong ties imply a deeper relationship or friendship, while weak ties are more of an acquaintance nature. Politically savvy leaders recognize that not all of their linkages in a network can involve strong ties, since deep relationships are simply not feasible or even desirable with all individuals. However, a recognition and cultivation of numerous weak ties, especially with influential people, can be useful to the leader because they help provide important connections or information. In other words, both strong and weak ties are important for the politically savvy leader.

Fourth, politically savvy leaders are adept at showing apparent sincerity. The term 'apparent' is significant here. What is really important is that the leader comes off as seeming to be sincere in his or her relationships and transactions. But over time, if the leader is not genuinely honest and forthright, others will see through him or her, and the leader will damage relationships and the ability to influence. That is, the leader will be potentially seen as manipulative. For example, a global bank was struggling and appointed a new leader. He was initially seen by others as charismatic. He was a great orator and talked with passion about the importance of people, both employees and customers. Initially, people were excited and energized by his bold vision for the institution. He seemed sincere in his claims. But over time, it became apparent that he was sincere only about advancing his own career. Within a year, the employees turned against him, feeling deceived. In the end he failed completely to turn the bank around and was replaced.

There are connections between a number of traits or characteristics that were considered in Chapter 2 and political savvy. For example, honesty and integrity (or at least their appearance) are important characteristics if a leader is to be both politically savvy and effective. But over time, if the leader's "apparent" sincerity seems fake or inconsistent, the leader will come off as disingenuous, and thus, lose influence. In a similar manner, the leader might appear to be seeking personalized, rather than socialized, power. Inevitably, people who the leader is seeking to influence will not react favorably to his or her attempts at political savvy. As another example, emotional intelligence and political savvy appear to go hand-in-hand. It is hard to imagine that a leader who lacks emotional intelligence would somehow be able to show political savvy. In sum, the traits that we considered in Chapter 2 go hand-in-hand with the effective, or ineffective, use of political savvy. ## Conclusion

In this chapter, we have considered the importance of relationship building and maintenance for leaders. Effective leaders need to demonstrate consistently strong relationships with followers through leader-member exchange processes. But moreover, they also need to demonstrate favorable relationships with people who are not direct reports. They do so through their political savvy.

One thing that should be clear in this chapter is that as mentioned above, “it takes two to tango.” The role of followers in leadership processes is often forgotten or underestimated. However, in Chapter 4, the role of followers will come to the forefront.
