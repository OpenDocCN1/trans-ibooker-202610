
## 13.5 Appendix: Twenty Years of Leadership and Organizational Change—The Case of Michael Crow and Arizona State University

### Introduction

On July 1, 2002, Michael Crow succeeded Lattie Coor as President of Arizona State University (ASU). He arrived from Columbia University where he had served as Executive Vice-Provost from 1998–2002. Upon his arrival, President Crow reported in speeches to internal and external audiences that ASU was a good "regional" university. However, it had not received a high level of national or international prominence in terms of being considered a "world-class" university. Various metrics were cited in Crow's speeches including university rankings, level of endowment, and level of sponsored research funding.

At the same time, Crow's early vision pointed toward the necessity of having a world-class university in Phoenix if the city was to be considered world-class in its own right as it develops in the twenty-first century. From economic, sociologic, and political viewpoints, he made a strong case that it is not really feasible for a city to achieve world-class status without the development of a world-class university within its geographical proximity. To a large extent, this basic principle provided the foundation for his broader vision of ASU.[13]

### The Institutional Context of Universities

The change effort in which Crow and ASU have engaged over the past 20 years is taking place in a larger society, and more specifically, an institutional context involving universities. That context has existed largely unchanged for over 200 years. Traditional elements of this context that includes ASU and other universities are as follows:

- Students in traditional classroom environment at a designated location; in other words, not distributed either geographically or virtually (e.g., through online education)
- Basic (rather than applied or community-oriented) research emphasis
- Support for research provided solely by national agencies and foundations (e.g., National Institute of Health, National Science Foundation, and so forth)
- Specialized research efforts in disciplinary areas (e.g., chemistry, physics, engineering), as opposed to interdisciplinary efforts (e.g., engineering combined with management; sustainability, and so forth)
- Large percentage of public university budgets based on state legislature support

In many ways, the above elements form the genetic code for the traditional university. However, there are new trends and forces for change in the environments of universities that threaten to render aspects of this code as obsolete. Examples include:

- Demand on the part of students for the university to, in essence, go where they are (e.g., online education and locating the university at multiple sites, including international locations), rather than simply existing in the confines of a particular geographic location or physical classroom.
- More applied and interdisciplinary teaching and research efforts
- Support for research provided by sources (e.g., business corporations; municipalities) in addition to national agencies and foundations
- Dramatically less state funding for public universities as a percentage of their overall budgets (e.g., ASU has gone from around 40 percent at the beginning of Crow's tenure to around 10 percent in 2017)[14]

In short, in recent times, the institutional context of universities has been ripe for change—or at least the perceived need for change. At the same time, the actual institutions and many of their constituents may show resistance. It is into this environment that Michael Crow ventured.

### The New Vision

A few years into Crow's tenure as ASU president, there was a groundbreaking for a new building to house the Cronkite School of Journalism at the growing downtown Phoenix campus of ASU. At that event, the legendary news commentator, Walter Cronkite, described Crow as "a true visionary of our time. He entered the Phoenix metropolitan area, took the reins of the university, and gave it direction and energy beyond what anyone could have imagined."[15] His vision has been dubbed "the new American university for the 21st century." So what is it about this vision that was new?

The summarized vision and goals that Crow established for ASU over his first ten years can be found in Exhibit 1 at the end of this case.[16] A number of key elements, ideas, and principles are at the core of the vision. These elements and examples of how they have been implemented thus far are shown in Exhibit 2 at the end of this case. Exhibit 2 also shows challenges and even failures to achieve the vision. First, Crow has taken an interdisciplinary approach to research efforts. A key example is the $150 million Biodesign Institute that integrates researchers and projects from a number of functional areas such as physics, engineering, and microbiology. Other examples include the Global Institute of Sustainability and the School of Earth and Space Exploration. Second, Crow envisioned, and has sought to instill, an institutional culture of entrepreneurial enterprise. Specifically, he has attempted to speed up decision-making processes oriented toward change, as well as the actual implementation of those decisions. Third, Crow has emphasized a vision of "one university in many places." Some examples of the unification include:

- One accreditation for all campuses, as opposed to separate accreditations for respective campuses prior to his arrival
- Integrated evaluation and promotion processes for faculty members across campuses
- The evolution of unified faculty and student governing entities (e.g., one faculty senate) across campuses
- Elimination of campus Provost positions at the separate ASU campuses
- Combining of previously independent, but overlapping colleges (e.g., separate business schools on West and Tempe campuses)

Furthermore, Crow has emphasized a non-tiering (i.e., no status differences) of colleges/schools and campuses. Prior to his arrival, the norms, culture, and accreditation standards across campuses suggested a form of tiering, whereby the Tempe campus was at the top of the pyramid, while other campuses were somewhat lower in the hierarchy—in other words, what might be termed "satellite" campuses. Indeed, a longstanding tradition of the university was to refer to Tempe as the "Main" campus. At least on paper, that designation and terminology has disappeared. In short, the current approach at integration would, at least on the surface, attempt to minimize such tiering or status differences.

Fourth, Crow's speeches and written messages to the university and community have emphasized inclusiveness, rather than exclusiveness. He cites examples of universities that gauge their effectiveness in terms of the numbers and types of students that they are able to exclude (i.e., not admit). Instead, Crow stresses the importance of including and retaining a wide and diverse range of students, including students from lower economic backgrounds (see http://vimeo.com/110929715) and different country/cultural backgrounds. To this effort, he has attempted to get more student aid money (partially through increased tuition) to qualified, lower-income students and families.

### Outcomes to Date

In addition to the accomplishments and changes suggested above, to date, there have been a number of more financially or numerically-based outcomes that can be associated with Crow's presidency. For example, in 2006, ASU passed the $200 million in sponsored research funding and by 2020, it had reached about $673 million, making it one of the fastest-growing research enterprises in the country. ASU's current research funding puts it at number 6 in the country with regard to research-oriented universities that lack a medical school.[77] Along with sponsored funding, the university's endowment level has grown at rates equaling or surpassing almost any university in the country. In essence, this means that the university has been able to secure large amounts of funds from rich donors. Furthermore, as compared to when Crow took office, university scientists have engaged in increased technology transfer efforts. For example, in 2020, ASU ranked #10 worldwide in terms of patent awards at universities.[18] Moreover, both faculty and the university have gained from revenue streams that are the result of licensing/commercializing the intellectual property associated with faculty research.

The stature of ASU among universities has also gone up. For example, since 2016, ASU has been ranked #1 every year by the U.S. News & World Report in terms of "most innovative schools", ahead of famous institutions like Stanford and MIT.[79] As a specific example of innovativeness, by the spring of 2020, the Biodesign Institute at ASU had built and deployed a high-speed robotic system capable of processing 2,000 COVID-19 test kits a day. Indeed, during Crow's tenure, ASU has begun to shed its top party school image for one of academic excellence and research. Renowned researchers are prevalent in ASU's many nationally recognized departments and programs, including a Nobel Prize winner in economics. Under Crow, ASU has also been able to increase its ability to attract top students, including National Merit Scholars. Further, four-year graduation rates have greatly increased under Crow's tenure.

Furthermore, ASU has become a more inclusive university. Specifically, while growing ASU to be by far the largest university in North America, it has simultaneously increased percentages of Hispanics and other minorities. Here are some examples:

- ASU's colleges and programs have frequently been ranked in the top 25 for Hispanic students in the United States by Hispanic magazines.
- In the spring of 2014, ASU received an award from the Greater Phoenix Urban League for engendering a more ethnically and racially diverse university.[20]
- More than half of ASU freshmen students in the fall of 2017 who were from the state of Arizona were minority students.
- By 2017, ASU was graduating more Native Americans than all of the University of California system campuses combined (even though California has more Native American residents than any other state).
- Fifty percent of students in 2017 were from families at or below the median income level. In 1990, this number was 3 percent. In both 2016 and 2017, ASU also ranked #1 public research university chosen by international students (and number 4 overall among U.S. universities).

As an additional example of inclusiveness and innovativeness, Crow and ASU struck a deal with Starbucks in 2014 to provide online undergraduate degrees to its benefits-eligible employees (i.e., "partners"). As stated by Howard Schultz, founder of Starbucks, the deal was struck largely because of the online capabilities that ASU had developed over the years. The idea was to include Starbucks employees who have started college, but were unable to complete their degrees.
