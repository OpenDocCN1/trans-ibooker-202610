## conformist

A follower who is highly agreeable and ingratiating toward his or her leader, and one who does not “rock the boat.”

Given these three dimensions of follower effectiveness, we now overview three common types of followers: (1) conformist, (2) alienated, and (3) effective. The conformist is in line with the stereotypical view of a follower as being highly agreeable and ingratiating toward his or her leader, and one who does not “rock the boat.” The individual’s level of engagement in the job or organization may vary. Some conformists will be highly engaged, while others will be more withdrawn. Regardless, the conformist follower is likely to be very loyal to the leader. If this is the case, then the leader may be lulled into thinking of the follower as highly effective. After all, the conformist does his or her job in an unquestioning or reliable manner and can be counted on by the leader. In other words, the conformist does not rock the boat in any manner. However, in the long-run, if the leader is surrounded by conformists, nagging problems might not be solved, innovative ideas may not be pursued, and necessary change in the work setting may not occur. Indeed, the problem of groupthink, which we will consider in more detail in Chapter 8, becomes highly likely when a group of followers is composed largely of conformists.

## alienated

A follower who is a critical and independent thinker, but not highly engaged.

In a different way, the alienated follower is much more of a critical and independent thinker, which means that s/he at least has the potential to be a problem-solver and innovator. The problem is that the individual is not engaged, and instead may shy away from increased responsibilities, his or her leader, or even coworkers. A number of reasons could account for the individual's passiveness. For example, the person's ideas may have been routinely “shot down” in the past by the leader or conformist coworkers. Then over time, the alienated follower will become conditioned to not being fully engaged. This can obviously be a problem, since this sort of individual could have much to offer to the leader, the team, and the organization.

On the other hand, the alienated follower might have “made his/her own bed” by previously providing ideas in a very pushy or even disloyal manner. This can happen by, for example, the follower questioning the leader's knowledge or competence in an open forum (i.e., in a public sort of venue). It can also happen when the follower goes around the leader to that person's boss (i.e., the 2nd level manager). Either sort of action can help cause the follower to ultimately be in the leader's out-group, which only serves to alienate the follower. We should further note that it is always possible for a follower to be loyal to the leader, but nevertheless disengaged. It could be that reasons aside from the leader might be causing the follower to disengage, such as personal problems or organizational policies with which the follower disagrees.

## effective

A follower who is a critical and independent thinker, highly engaged, and loyal to the leader.

Obviously, both conformist and alienated followers are problematic for leaders, the organization, and even the followers themselves. So what exactly is effective followership all about? As shown in Table 4.1, effective followers are critical and independent thinkers, highly engaged, and loyal to the leaders. Based on these characteristics and actions, there are largely three types of actions that make them effective as followers: (1) assuming responsibility and accountability, (2) balancing service with the courage to challenge, and (3) supporting organizational goals and directions, or having the courage to leave. Each of these factors involve a combination of the qualities that are shown in Table 4.1. ### Assuming responsibility and accountability

Responsibility and accountability go hand in hand. An effective follower feels a broad sense of responsibility to meet the demands of his or her job (e.g., following instructions or established procedures), and serve relevant constituent groups (e.g., customers and clients). A highly responsible follower is also more likely to be very engaged in his or her work, and to think critically about ways to solve problems that might prevent responsibilities from being met.

Organizations have mechanisms to hold people accountable for their actions and performance. These mechanisms include written codes of conduct, performance appraisal systems, and so forth. For some followers, especially those who are less effective, their only accountability comes from these *external* mechanisms that might hold one to account, so to speak. In other words, there needs to be external demands or pressures (e.g., from one’s boss) for those followers to be accountable for their actions. In contrast, more effective followers go beyond being held to account by others in that they have their own *internal* sense of accountability. Thus, if responsibilities do not get met, the accountable/effective follower will not try to shuffle blame to others, but instead will live up to the adage, “the buck stops here.”[4] In contrast, less effective followers try to minimize their responsibilities and largely do not have an internal sense of accountability.[5]

It should be obvious that leaders admire and desire followers who are both responsible and have an internal sense of accountability. Such followers are likely to be viewed as both engaged and loyal by the leader, since they do not upwardly delegate. That is, they do not attempt to push responsibilities (e.g., responsibilities for solving problems that they have been given) back to the leader; nor do they blame the leader when problems or poor performance occur. Upward delegation can be very annoying for leaders, and they do not appreciate followers who consistently engage in such practices.

### Balancing service with the courage to challenge

Effective followers walk a tightrope between serving and challenging. By serving, we refer to being highly engaged, showing responsibility, and being loyal to the leader. On the other hand, there are times when effective followers demonstrate their critical, independent thinking by showing courage, challenging the status quo (e.g., established procedures), and even challenging their leaders. The question becomes precisely how, and under what circumstances, challenges are shown.

Let’s take a hypothetical example. If a follower challenges her leader to change an important work method or procedure, perhaps by using new technology, then such a challenge is appropriate and will likely be viewed as a demonstration of good followership. However, if the follower persists in pushing her point of view, perhaps to the point of engaging in acts that are seen as disloyal by the leader (e.g., the follower taking her ideas to the leader’s boss), then the follower is not balancing service with the courage to challenge. Instead, the balance has shifted too far away from service. Overall, this balance can at times not be an easy one to navigate. Nevertheless, an effective follower has both the emotional intelligence and political savvy (i.e., social astuteness and interpersonal skills) that we discussed in Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 that will allow him or her to deal with this tightrope. One practical way to find this balance comes from the military context where subordinates are often encouraged to challenge decisions so long as the discussion is still open. Once the boss indicates that a decision has been made, the subordinate is expected to “disagree but commit” to executing the decision.

### Supporting organizational goals and directions, or having the courage to leave

To be an effective follower means that one’s loyalty extends beyond one’s immediate formal leader, or supervisor. It also means being loyal to the organization. There are times when organizational members may question the direction or changes occurring in the organization. The changes may pertain to new technologies, organizational restructuring, new work methods, and so forth. As stated above, it is good for followers to be critical, independent thinkers, which could mean questioning the reasonableness or viability of such changes.

When that occurs, his critical, independent thinking is no longer productive, at least in the current organizational setting. When that occurs, there are only two courses of action.
