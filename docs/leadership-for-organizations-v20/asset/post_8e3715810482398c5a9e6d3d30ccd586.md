## Work With the Leader's Style, Goals, and Perspective

In some ways, leaders are like anyone else—they have their own style, goals, and perspectives on various issues. Some leaders play things “by the book,” so to speak; while others are more free-wheeling and loose regarding rules. Some leaders are very much quantity-oriented, while others stress quality or service. Of course, it is always possible that there might be stylistic or goal-based differences between a leader and a follower, making it difficult to manage up. But there can be a “meeting of the minds,” so to speak, as long as the follower recognizes the nature of the leader's style and goals, and attempts to make compromises.

Again, let’s take a hypothetical example. Suppose that a follower does not like to closely adhere to a lot of bureaucratic rules and procedures, but her boss would prefer that followers more closely adhere to their job descriptions, formal handbooks, and so forth. It could be that certain rules or procedures are most important to the leader, and if adhered to, the leader will be happy. To properly manage up, the onus is on the follower to determine the particular “trigger buttons” of the leader and thus avoid deviations that would upset the leader.

Let’s also take the example of students’ perspective on what constitutes fairness (e.g., in relation to grades or grading processes). In general, students think of fairness from their own personal perspective. Thus, they ask themselves the question, “was I treated fairly by this instructor?” However, instructors have to balance what is fair to an individual student, versus what is fair to the overall class of students. By at least attempting to understand the instructor’s perspective on fairness in grading, a student is demonstrating how to better manage up.

## Role Model the Behavior That You Seek

One of the authors once heard an individual complain about her boss, “I just wish that he would give me more recognition and praise...” In reply, the author asked, “How often do you give your boss recognition and praise?” With a bewildered look, she replied, “Well that’s his job and his responsibility. He’s the one who should be recognizing me for all of the things that I do right.” To be sure, we do not totally disagree with her; it is a leader’s responsibility to find and recognize followers for their actions and performance. But not to beleaguer a point that we have made throughout this chapter and the prior one, “it takes two to tango.” So if a follower is looking for her leader to praise her more, she should find ways to praise her leader. As another example, if a follower wants his leader to be more cheerful, he should smile and say hello when he greets the leader in the morning.

Obviously, there is no guarantee that behavior such as that suggested above will be reciprocated. After all, some individuals are just depressed or angry people, making them “tough nuts to crack” in terms of things like cheerfulness or pleasantness, despite one’s best overtures. However, call it karma, call it “one good turn begets another,” or whatever. If a follower would rather see a certain type or style of behavior from the leader, then the follower needs to manage up by role modeling such behavior.

## 4.3 Managing Sideways

managing sideways: When peers or coworkers are able to lead and influence each other.

Sometimes it is important for peers or coworkers to be able to lead and influence each other—or **managing sideways**. Students who work in teams know this to be all so true, and they also know that it can be frustrating to try to lead those over whom they have no direct authority. While there are no easy answers, it is important to remember that you and your peers generally have common superordinate goals, such as satisfying customers, clients, or instructors. Further, some of the principles of managing up apply to managing sideways. For example, it is important to assume good motives on the part of the peer, even if it might seem that the person is irresponsible or detached.

It is also essential to try to understand peers' individual needs, styles of working, and problems. Sometimes tasks among peers can be divided to better match each other's needs and desires. A peer who is attempting to effectively manage sideways will try to better understand or empathize with his or her peers.

Of course, when managing sideways, it is always possible to get one's formal leader involved. But such a course of action should be viewed as a last resort. In all likelihood, the boss would prefer that peers lead and influence each other in a cooperative manner, rather than always running to him or her when problems develop.
