Researchers, therapists, and physicians commonly measure physiological phenomena in the brain in terms of changes in blood flow and electrical activity. This activity occurs in one of two ways. First, the brain reacts physiologically to stimuli in our environments. For example, if a tiger were to walk into your immediate proximity, your brain would immediately engage in changes in blood flow and electrical activity that are related to fear and flight. Second, when your brain is in an alert but resting state (i.e., not being subjected to stimuli), it has what is known as a distinct, intrinsic electrical structure or pattern. Like height, weight, personality, and so forth, each of us have unique, intrinsic structures that can be assessed through what is known as quantitative electroencephalogram (qEEG).29 Such neural structures differ between individuals, and they are relatively stable over time. However, in line with the concept of brain plasticity, these structures can potentially change over time due to life experiences, traumatic injury, and even systematic training.

# Inside the Brain of a Leader

Can neurological rewiring boost leadership skills? https://www.inc.com/magazine/201306/eric-markowitz/brain-leadership-inside.html

In recent years, research has shown that intrinsic structures in the brain are associated with various aspects of effective leadership, such as visionary, ethical/moral, non-abusive, and complex reasoning forms of leadership.30 In other words, we differ on those intrinsic structures, and these differences are associated with whether or not we will tend to be effective as leaders. What is not as yet clear are the practical implications of such research. One possibility is that a technique like neurofeedback could be used to help develop the potential for better leadership behavior. Through neurofeedback, the brain might be trained to be more conducive to effective leader cognition, emotions, and behavior.31 While not a “magic bullet” for prospective leaders, perhaps neurofeedback might be paired with other leader development approaches, such as the 360-degree feedback processes considered in Chapter 7 of this book. Another possibility might be to actually use neurologically-based data to help form the basis for the selection or promotion of individuals into leadership positions. Indeed, the business world and media are starting to take note of these potential implications of neuroscience.32

Yet both of these potential implications raise ethical issues (e.g., privacy) that will most likely come into play in future years as this research moves forward. For example, is it ethical to change the electrical brain structures of individuals in an attempt to make them better leaders? Who says when and how brain-based data might be used to help select and promote leaders? Despite such difficult questions, one thing is for sure: the brain represents a new frontier for both understanding and developing leadership potential.
