During the Japanese side-conference, the Americans also huddle and talk among themselves. One of the Americans speaks up and says, "You know these Japanese guys are really rude. For the most part, when I'm speaking, they won't even make eye contact with me. To add insult to injury, they can't even be honest with me. I'd bet that they're plotting some slick maneuver in these negotiations."

After about 5–10 minutes, the Japanese negotiators return to the table, and announce, "It is getting close to dinner time. We think that this might be a good time to take a break, and for all of us to go to dinner together. Perhaps we can find a restaurant and continue getting to know one another over food and drinks." One of the Americans then whispers in the ear of one of his colleagues, "These guys are just trying to stall. We had planned on wrapping this negotiation up today and being on a plane back to the U.S. tomorrow. And now these guys are just talking about eating, drinking, and schmoozing. Jeeezzz ..."

As mentioned in Chapter 7, people have a tendency to form quick judgments or attributions, oftentimes negative in nature, about the motives, behaviors, and personalities of others. This tendency can be even stronger in cross-cultural situations that provide a lot of uncertain or confusing information, such as the ones mentioned above. Indeed, we can clearly see these negative attributions or judgments in the words of the American negotiators who are attempting to make sense of the behavior of their Japanese counterparts.

In many instances, such attributions are wrong, and can lead to counterproductive behavior (e.g., negative accusations). For example, more often than not, when negotiators whose first language is not English (e.g., the Japanese individuals in our example) want to take a side-bar huddle, it is not because they are plotting some sneaky maneuver. Rather, it is simply because they do not understand some of the English that is being spoken, and are attempting to interpret it among themselves. One might wonder why they simply do not ask for clarification directly to the Americans. But in doing so, they might feel embarrassed or lose face. [28] In the Saudi example, it may simply be customary in his culture to hold hands with a male colleague, and it has no sexual connotations. It follows that any attribution of motives to the contrary on the part of the American would be a misinterpretation.

> > **cultural blinders**
> > Making the mistake of assuming too many similarities between ourselves and those who come from a different culture.

The lesson to be learned from all of these examples is that a lack of understanding of one’s own and others' cultures can result in having what can be metaphorically termed "cultural blinders". When we wear such blinders, we are liable to make the mistake of assuming too many similarities between ourselves and those who come from a different culture. For example, one may think to herself, "I make eye contact when speaking to another person. So if this Japanese guy is not making eye contact with me, there must be something wrong here." The end result of such thinking is a tendency toward negative attributions or judgments of others' motives, and thus, misinterpretations. If the goal is to influence, which is obviously the case for leaders, that goal will not be furthered by negative attributions and the type of thinking or actions that are likely to result.

By having a global orientation, leaders will be slow to make negative attributions. Their cross-cultural know-how and tendency to engage enthusiastically with members of other cultures will prevent negative attributions and misinterpretations. Moreover, if understanding about a particular behavior is lacking (e.g., the Saudi hand holding), they might take the time to ask questions and seek advice from others who have more experience with a particular culture.

## Conclusion

Leading in a global context presents special challenges for leader effectiveness. In this chapter, we first considered what global leadership is all about. Then, using the example of American culture, we identified the importance of first understanding one’s own cultural background before we can accurately understand other cultures. We proceeded to describe how aspects of having a global leader orientation can help provide the basis of leading and influencing people from different cultures. We further cautioned against cultural misinterpretations and the accompanying negative attributions that can form toward people from other cultures.
