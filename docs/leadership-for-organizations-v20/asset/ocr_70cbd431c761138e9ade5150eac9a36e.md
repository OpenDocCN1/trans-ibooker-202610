At the same time, the inclusive leader will be sensitive toward other group members (e.g., white men) who may believe that reverse discrimination could be at play. It is the job of the leader to assure these majority group members that favorable or supportive actions toward those in the minority are not due to reverse discrimination, but rather due to the merit of these minority group members.

On the other hand, leaders in organizations have to understand that in most situations, they are leading a range of individuals, not just women or minoritized group members. Accordingly, leaders must be sensitive to issues such as reverse discrimination and how fairness can be interpreted in different ways among diverse individuals who are being led. Societal events in the United States in the summer of 2020 brought to light ongoing issues pertaining to the treatment of minoritized people, especially those who are Black, in society. Resulting efforts to bring about positive change in both society and organizations are laudable. However, those efforts could backfire in organizations if used as an excuse to “shame” or in some way discriminate in a reverse manner toward majority group members. Policies and leader efforts along such lines would only serve to damage the development of a team orientation and building of the type of cultural homogeneity or unity discussed next.

# Paradox: Building Unity, While Allowing for Diversity

As emphasized repeatedly in this book, effective leadership is largely about dealing with paradoxes. When it comes to leading diversity, a key paradox is balancing the leader’s desire to create a unified team culture and sense of purpose with the desire to embrace diversity. Leaders strive to get their followers on the same page (i.e., unity) when it comes to things like values and norms. For example, customer support and service may be a key value for a lot of leaders, and they attempt to make it an integral part of the culture for all team members. In other words, they try to build a strong, unified culture in the teams or organizations that they lead because of the cohesiveness and sense of purpose that follows.[44] On the other hand, in the spirit of embracing diversity, leaders also want to allow for individual differences between employees based on their backgrounds, personalities, styles, and sex.

Thus, the way to reconcile this paradox is to recognize potential problems with inclusiveness (e.g., both overt or unintentional discriminatory practices), while simultaneously treating everyone fairly (minoritized and non-minoritized members alike). Furthermore, leaders should set the goal or overarching values of the team, while simultaneously allowing employees some leeway to uniquely pursue those goals or values. For example, again consider the value/goal of customer service and support, which has been a theme of Southwest Airlines since its inception. Southwest allows employees to pursue customer service and support in their own unique ways. Some employees may use humor in an attempt to brighten the flying experience of customers, while others are more reserved, but nevertheless, helpful to customers in solving problems or making for a satisfying flying experience.[55] Moreover, given the increasingly diverse customer base of many organizations, having diverse employees can help a firm to better understand and adjust its practices to satisfy customer demands.
