# Case Examples

**Southwest Airlines.** Since organizational culture can be thought of as a social control system, the same logic of aligning the culture with vision, mission, and strategy applies. If a leader’s strategy emphasizes low costs, does the organizational culture support this? To illustrate this point, think about Southwest Airlines, the biggest and most successful low-cost airline in the U.S. What culture has Southwest developed in order to ensure low costs? Airlines are a capital-intensive business, and one of the keys to success in any capital-intensive business (e.g., airlines, hotels, oil refineries, semiconductor manufacturing) is high asset utilization. In Southwest’s case, this means keeping the airplanes in the air. Part of this issue is handled through the formal control system and aspects of an *order and consistency culture* that efficiently schedules airplanes. But an equally, if not more important part is by turning around the airplanes quickly and being responsive to customers. Here, the key is a culture that promotes both flexibility and creativity (i.e., *adaptability culture*), as well as teamwork (i.e., *clan culture*), on the part of all employees.

An example of teamwork is how employees at Southwest do not strictly delineate their roles. For the purpose of turning around the airplanes quickly, pilots, ramp agents, and flight attendants typically work together to get a parked airplane ready for its next flight. In short, because employees care about each other and the company, they are more willing to pitch in, help each other out, and use their initiative to solve problems when they arise.[17]

Figure 12.2 depicts a "triangle of success" at Southwest with organizational culture as one point in the triangle. However, as also depicted in Figure 12.2, some key decisions or strategies have been executed throughout the firm’s history to implement its emphasis on combining customer satisfaction with the pursuit of low costs. *First*, customer satisfaction is not just a slogan, but has been implemented using a number of policies and strategies. For example, Southwest attempts to have point-to-point (or nonstop) flights between cities. Although not totally unique in the industry, this scheduling strategy stands in contrast to the hub-and-spoke model of large airlines like Delta and United, which rely on funneling passengers through one or more hub airports with connecting flights to destination cities. While somewhat efficient as a means of scheduling, the downside of a hub-and-spoke model is that it forces extra, connecting flights that are less than desirable for passengers. In addition, Southwest stresses on-time performance and encourages friendly service on the part of employees.
