# # Incremental Change

Most organizational change is **incremental**, involving small improvements to existing products and processes in order to improve efficiency or make products and services more attractive to customers. For example, by refining existing processes, a manufacturer may figure out ways to improve production processes or reduce cost. An internet service provider may learn how to increase the speed of delivery by refining code. When Apple markets a new iPhone, although advertising may attempt to indicate otherwise, new versions are better or more attractive because of relatively small refinements in how they work and look. These improvements are based on existing capabilities and technologies that get incrementally better over time. Often these advances are based on a systematic continuous improvement process like six-sigma or kaizen that rely on a disciplined methodology of plan-do-check-act.[4]

From a leadership and change perspective, two things are important to note here. *First*, incremental changes are an expected part of day-to-day activities and do not necessarily require extraordinary efforts on the part of leaders. *Second*, because incremental changes are usually based on existing knowledge and capabilities, they are most often accomplished within existing organization structures.

# # Discontinuous Change

While incremental changes build on known processes, **discontinuous change** typically requires new skills, capabilities, and perspectives, and may also need significant new resources to implement. To succeed, these changes often require major shifts in structures and processes that can challenge the existing way of operating. For example, when Netflix began video streaming, it required a completely new set of technologies and challenged their conventional way of distributing DVDs by mail. When genetically modified seeds to protect crops from disease became available, it required agricultural chemical companies to learn new skills (biology rather than chemistry) and undermined their existing business of selling fungicides and herbicides. As another example, the COVID-19 pandemic has, to some degree, changed the perspectives of many individuals (and leaders) regarding the need for traditional work facilities and hours. That is, some people are questioning the need for devoted office space in central locations, as opposed to people working largely from home or other locations with more flexible work hours. The availability of advanced video-conferencing capabilities has contributed to this thinking. The upshot is that, as we move into the future, even without a pandemic, we may start to see some fundamental changes in how many organizations structure and schedule work.

Because discontinuous change can be so disruptive or create uncertainty, it requires leaders to think and act very differently in order to succeed in their change efforts. It can also lead to the type of widespread resistance to change that we will consider later in this chapter.
