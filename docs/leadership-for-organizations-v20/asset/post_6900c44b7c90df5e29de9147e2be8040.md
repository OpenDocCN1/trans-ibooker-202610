
## Incremental Change

Most organizational change is **incremental**, involving small improvements to existing products and processes in order to improve efficiency or make products and services more attractive to customers. For example, by refining existing processes, a manufacturer may figure out ways to improve production processes or reduce cost. An internet service provider may learn how to increase the speed of delivery by refining code. When Apple markets a new iPhone, although advertising may attempt to indicate otherwise, new versions are better or more attractive because of relatively small refinements in how they work and look. These improvements are based on existing capabilities and technologies that get incrementally better over time. Often these advances are based on a systematic continuous improvement process like six-sigma or kaizen that rely on a disciplined methodology of plan-do-check-act.[4]

From a leadership and change perspective, two things are important to note here. *First*, incremental changes are an expected part of day-to-day activities and do not necessarily require extraordinary efforts on the part of leaders. *Second*, because incremental changes are usually based on existing knowledge and capabilities, they are most often accomplished within existing organization structures.

## Discontinuous Change

While incremental changes build on known processes, **discontinuous change** typically requires new skills, capabilities, and perspectives, and may also need significant new resources to implement. To succeed, these changes often require major shifts in structures and processes that can challenge the existing way of operating. For example, when Netflix began video streaming, it required a completely new set of technologies and challenged their conventional way of distributing DVDs by mail. When genetically modified seeds to protect crops from disease became available, it required agricultural chemical companies to learn new skills (biology rather than chemistry) and undermined their existing business of selling fungicides and herbicides. As another example, the COVID-19 pandemic has, to some degree, changed the perspectives of many individuals (and leaders) regarding the need for traditional work facilities and hours. That is, some people are questioning the need for devoted office space in central locations, as opposed to people working largely from home or other locations with more flexible work hours. The availability of advanced video-conferencing capabilities has contributed to this thinking. The upshot is that, as we move into the future, even without a pandemic, we may start to see some fundamental changes in how many organizations structure and schedule work.

Because discontinuous change can be so disruptive or create uncertainty, it requires leaders to think and act very differently in order to succeed in their change efforts. It can also lead to the type of widespread resistance to change that we will consider later in this chapter.

## 13.3 The Role of the Leader in Change

Figure 13.1 provides a simple framework illustrating how these two types of change can interact. Typically, the most radical change involves proactive, discontinuous actions on the part of leaders and their firms. In other words, the change is fundamentally transformative. Given that people are largely leery of change, transformative change can be especially challenging for leaders. In Chapter 1, we made a fundamental distinction between _management_ and _leadership_, noting that managers work within existing structures to make systems operate effectively and deliver results, while leaders are responsible for setting a vision and helping the organization adapt to change. Based on Figure 13.1, a different way of saying this is that managers are most often responsible for incremental or reactive change, while true leaders are more often concerned with transformation and discontinuous change. While incremental change is important, it typically can be achieved through careful planning, measurement and feedback. On the other hand, discontinuous (both transformation and re-orientation) change is riskier, more important, and requires more involvement on the part of leaders.

For example, think about the leadership challenges associated with transforming a large organization like IBM. That firm began as a manufacturer of mechanical tabulating equipment, moved into computers and software (same customers but a completely different technology), leveraged these assets and capabilities to become a company that also provides business solutions and consulting, and is now trying to become a provider of artificial intelligence solutions. Each of these shifts required leaders to dramatically change how they did business and where they allocated their resources; in other words, major transformative change. In contrast, think about how the leaders of companies like Kodak or Polaroid who, when confronted with disruptive changes in their core business of film and instant photography, failed to help their organizations make the changes needed. What is it that differentiates the leaders of successful versus unsuccessful organizational change efforts?

To a large extent, we will attempt to answer that question in the remainder of this chapter. Undoubtedly, in a company as large as IBM or the former Kodak, one person alone cannot be responsible for the success or failure of the entire change process. However, people in leadership positions can play an especially large role. Let us take one of the larger-than-life icons of recent history in the business world, Steve Jobs, one of the founders of Apple. Jobs is known for many acts of leading and effecting change throughout a career that spanned approximately three decades. But one not-so-well known act was the use of a mouse on personal computers. Jobs came across the possibility of how a mouse could revolutionize personal computing around 1980 in the early stages of Macintosh development.[5] He and some colleagues at Apple witnessed a demonstration at a Xerox research and development laboratory (PARC). It featured an early prototype of a mouse on a computer that was originally meant to be for graphic design engineers.

In the middle of the demonstration, Jobs got up and started dancing around the room. Obviously, some people who were present thought that he was crazy, or at a minimum, a very strange individual. As his career went on, perhaps both opinions bore out some truth. Indeed, as noted earlier in this book, true leaders are oftentimes viewed as strange, different, or just “out there.” But what had happened in that particular moment is that unlike others who were present, Jobs was suddenly able to envision how this very early prototype could eventually become part of literally billions of personal computers (not just those associated with Apple) that would change billions of lives. Of course, it was not that simple, and it took much time, efforts at motivating others (e.g., Apple engineers), and dealing with setbacks to realize Jobs' original vision of the mouse. Nevertheless, the story began with the vision of just one individual. The rest is history.

## A Three-Stage Approach for Generating Organizational Change

Leaders realize that change and its effective implementation do not happen overnight. Instead, change represents a process that unfolds over time. Below, we will consider two ways of thinking about a change process. The first one has been around a while, but is conceptually simple and still represents a useful way for leaders to consider how to drive change in organizations. It essentially involves three steps: (1) unfreeze, (2) change, and (3) refreeze.[6]

**Unfreeze.** The first step is for leaders to provide a compelling reason for why change is necessary and important. For reactive change, this is typically less difficult. Employees may already be aware of declining profits, increased pressure on reducing costs, and the success of competitors. For proactive change, the challenge is more difficult. Can the leader create the perception of a looming crisis, or demonstrate a huge opportunity? This might involve providing evidence that demonstrates that the firm is under-performing using customer feedback or benchmarking data to convince people that storm clouds are on the horizon, or to paint a picture of a huge future opportunity. In short, the leadership challenge is to provide a credible reason, either a threat or opportunity, as to why employee’s need to embrace change. As the saying goes, “people change because they see the light or they feel the fire.”[7]

**Change.** Once there is a credible reason for change, then the leader needs to provide a reasonable plan for dealing with the change. This should include a transition manager (the person responsible for implementing the change), the transition team (who is on the team), the resources necessary (money, people), feedback metrics (how people will know when they are succeeding), and a clear time frame for implementation. In developing this plan, it is important for the leader to build support with key power groups within the organization and to anticipate and, if possible, to preempt resistance. This might include an analysis of who is likely to be affected positively or negatively by the change effort, and whose opposition might undermine or derail the change. It should be noted that change efforts should not be all top-down. These efforts could also provide for the involvement of employees to build commitment (e.g., task forces, town hall meetings, surveys).

**Refreeze.** Once the change effort has been accomplished, it is important to ensure that there is no backsliding. This means making sure that the new behaviors and processes are firmly embedded into the organization. This effort could include the development and monitoring of new milestones and metrics, the realignment of incentives to reward the new behaviors and to sanction the old ones, and the continued communication of the new vision.
