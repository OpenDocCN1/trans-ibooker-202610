# Some Hard Bumps Along the Path of Change

Despite the accomplishments and outcomes summarized thus far, there have been some definite bumps along the way for ASU and Michael Crow over the past 20 years. That is, the path toward change has not always been smooth, and some might argue that the bumpiness could have been predicted and even prevented. Some of the challenges have been due to economic problems in AZ and the country as a whole. But others are more personal to Michael Crow, his style of leadership, and approach toward change.

# Institutional and Economic Challenges to Change

Making broad-sweeping change in an academic institution is not an easy thing to do. There are a number of institutional challenges. First, bureaucracy is one such challenge because of how it represents an obstacle to change, de-empowering people to act in accordance with the goals of change. Crow himself has referred to it as "the virus of the agency".[22] Bureaucracy involves rigid adherence to rules and procedures, despite conditions that might dictate that such rules or procedures be softened or even altogether ignored. In a speech as early as the fall of 2002, Crow told ASU faculty and administrators that even after just three months as president, he had noticed what seemed to be an incredible amount of bureaucracy at ASU. Nevertheless, 20 years later, the bureaucracy at ASU seems to be going strong in terms of paperwork, rules, and rigid procedures for students, faculty, and staff alike. Indeed, in some ways, the COVID-19 pandemic only served to increase bureaucracy at ASU.

Second, there is the issue of faculty governance, which is a hallmark principle in academic institutions across the United States—as well as in many other countries. The basic idea is that that much of the strategic governance of the university should emanate from faculty members. Relevant aspects of governance include policies, curricula and programs, and evaluation procedures (e.g., evaluation of the faculty). Crow's top-down approach to change is not always congruent with faculty governance traditions and expectations. We know from research that a lack of voice on the part of employees tends to reduce organizational commitment.[23]

Third, additional long-standing norms or beliefs present challenges to the types of changes that Crow seeks. Despite recent claims to the contrary (see http://vimeo.com/110929715), other than some new colleges (e.g., Sustainability), there is little evidence of interdisciplinary teaching and research within the ASU system, and little evidence of increased entrepreneurship. As is the case at most traditional universities, ASU's organizational structure and systems (e.g., promotion and tenure systems for faculty members) largely continue to support a functional, "silo" approach that stresses disciplinary specialties—not interdisciplinary thinking and action, or entrepreneurship (e.g., on the part of faculty members).
