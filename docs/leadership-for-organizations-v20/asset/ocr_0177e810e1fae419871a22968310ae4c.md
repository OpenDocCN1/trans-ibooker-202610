For example, employees are likely to expect to hear about potential restructuring in their work environment (*e.g.*, reassignments or job cuts) early on. However, if they only get information from the leader when the restructuring is set into motion, they may perceive that the leader lacks integrity. In this case, what is not said is what becomes important to followers. Bill George, the former CEO of Medtronic and author of the popular book, *Authentic Leadership*, says “Integrity is not ‘not lying’—it is telling the whole truth.”[16]

Second, people who are considered to be genuine or authentic. In short, authentic leaders do not put on appearances or try to act in a manner that does not reflect their true dispositions. In a word, they are not fake; instead, they are themselves. This may seem obvious, but it is surprising how, especially when people assume leadership roles, they often attempt to change who they are. Examples include one’s accent, mannerisms, and so forth. Followers tend to be more confident in, or inspired by, leaders who maintain their authenticity.

Third, leaders have a strong sense of internal accountability. The Samurais in historic Japan would literally fall on their swords if they disgraced themselves and their emperors by losing in battle. This obviously constitutes an extreme act of accountability. But in general, to accept accountability means that one feels a strong sense of responsibility toward others or toward a cause, and if performance standards are not met, the individual accepts negative personal repercussions, and will ultimately attempt to rectify the substandard performance. Unfortunately, personal accountability is largely not the norm in modern organizations, and instead, people find ways of blaming others for performance failures. For example, during the 2008 financial crisis, people in positions of leadership at financial firms such as Lehman Brothers typically did not assume accountability for the misery that they caused, at least in part, toward customers. Instead, those individuals blamed the greater economy, government policies, and even the customers themselves (*e.g.*, customers who supposedly frivolously took on too much debt). In short, leaders do not blame others for failures; instead, they take personal accountability.

# Complexity and Paradoxical Nature

Really good leaders are complex, and sometimes even appear to be contradictory individuals. Indeed, they may be paradoxical in their qualities and in their approach to leading. As mentioned in Chapter 1, a **paradox** involves seemingly contradictory elements that appear to be logical when considered in isolation. But those elements will at first glance also seem irrational, inconsistent, and even absurd when juxtaposed. In other words, at least on the surface, they seem irreconcilable. That said, the idea of paradoxical leadership is to show how seemingly contradictory elements can exist both simultaneously and harmoniously in order to contribute to leader effectiveness.[17] Below are some examples.

> **paradox**
> 
> Seemingly contradictory elements that exist simultaneously and appear to be logical when considered in isolation. But those elements will at first glance also seem irrational, inconsistent, and even absurd when juxtaposed.

Narcissistic, but yet humble. Narcissistic individuals are very self-absorbed, and they demand attention and adulation from other people. At first glance, it might seem like narcissism is always a bad leadership quality. Indeed, narcissism can have bad effects in that narcissists can be abusive to others, including their potential followers.[18] In other words, it might seem clear that narcissism is indicative of what can be termed the “dark side” of leadership. But at the same time, we know that narcissists are motivated to make decisions and lead others (*e.g.*, provide vision), and they see themselves as natural leaders. This fact in and of itself is important, since some people simply do not want to make decisions or lead, and yet decision-makers and people who take the lead are necessary in organizations. In addition, as compared to non-narcissists, narcissistic leaders tend to be rather bold in their visions, and bold or new vision is something that is often necessary for organizations.[19]
