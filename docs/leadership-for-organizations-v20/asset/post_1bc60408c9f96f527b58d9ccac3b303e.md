Faced with the challenge of disrupting what has been successful in the past, the natural inclination of organizations and people is to question the need for change. This inertia protects us from bad ideas and allows for the stability required to manage large, complex organizations.

But sometimes, in the face of changing technologies and markets, real change is required and the natural resistance to change can be dangerous. Under these circumstances, it is the leader's responsibility to manage this resistance. A failure on the part of the leader to do this can put the organization at risk. This means that an inevitable part of leading change means overcoming the resistance to it.

Several years ago, a large multinational bank faced an existential threat. Competitors were stealing market share, and the bank's original competitive advantage was no longer protecting them. In the face of this challenge, the CEO crafted a new vision and strategy, gave stirring speeches about the need to change, restructured the organization, changed the reward system, and instituted a large training program to provide his managers with new skills. He used many of the steps or mechanisms for change that we outlined above. In short, he did everything but deal with the resistance to change, and in the end, he failed. He failed because he allowed several of his most important senior managers to passively resist his change efforts. They continually undercut his efforts and slowed the process down. In the face of this resistance, people throughout the organization questioned whether the change was really necessary, or if they could achieve the new goals. Political factions developed that slowed the process even further. When the CEO was informed of this resistance, he acknowledged it, but indicated that the resistors were powerful figures within the bank, and he did not think he could replace them. In spite of doing all the "right" things to drive the change, they were able to "passive-aggressively" kill the change program.

The lesson? A key success factor for change leaders is to understand whose cooperation is critical and who the key players are who can derail the effort. Once identified, the challenge for the change leader is to identify the key actors who can be enlisted to support the change, but to also identify those who might slow down or block the change effort. Leaders should realize that resistance is normal and can even be healthy, especially if some important information that is relevant to a change is not being taken into account, as is the case with groupthink (see Chapter 8 in this book). Accordingly, the ideas and fears of resistors should be taken into account, especially for those who have the best interests of the organization in mind. But if decisions for change have been made, and if the resistors cannot be converted, leaders must consider ways to minimize their impact by neutralizing or eliminating the threat before it damages the change effort irreparably.

> **fence-sitters**
> 
> Employees in a change process who are skeptical and wait for evidence that the change effort is serious and can yield good results.

All of that is easier said than done, and there are some specific things that leaders can do to prevent or deal with resistors. Before describing those things, it is first important to understand a rule of thumb when it comes to how organizational members are likely to fall out in terms of their support for change. It is known as the "one-third rule." Specifically, initially one-third of the people will buy in simply because they like change or trust the leader. Another one-third will be skeptical and wait for evidence that the change effort is serious and can yield good results. A final one-third will be resistant, regardless of the seriousness of the change effort and its potential to be beneficial. With that said, we acknowledge that these numbers are rough, and, of course, they can vary depending on the context. But overall, this means that the leader's job is largely to continually signal to the middle third (i.e., the “fence-sitters”) that the change is happening, and progress is being made. When two-thirds of a group adopt the change, the remaining third have little choice but to go along or be left behind. However, the leader cannot forget about the one-third that will tend to be ardently resistant.

With all of that in mind, what can leaders do to prevent or deal with “fence-sitters” or resistors? Regarding the middle one-third, we see several things that can help bring them around. First, by doing some of the things that are stressed by the eight-stage model, “fence-sitters” will tend to be pushed toward the supportive category. These include communicating a vision widely, making sure that reward systems are properly aligned with change efforts, and providing training to help people adapt to new changes. In addition, it does not hurt to allow “fence-sitters” to actually have a say (i.e., voice) in how change efforts will proceed.

### true resistors

Employees in a change process who actively or passive-aggressively attempt to undermine change efforts.

The resistors, or bottom one-third, pose a more serious challenge. As noted in our above example of the multinational bank, if left unchecked, these resistors can totally derail a change effort. It may be best to think of this bottom one-third in terms of bad versus worse. In other words, the “bad” are likely to stay relatively quiet. While they do not support change efforts, they also do not undermine them. Eventually, these individuals make a choice to either live with the change, or voluntarily depart the organization. On the other hand, a fraction of this one-third are “worse” in their behavior. They will actively or passive-aggressively attempt to undermine change efforts. Indeed, these “true resistors” are not just the problem of a higher-level change leader; they are also the nemesis of lower-level leaders who are responsible for implementing change. Fred Hasan, the former Chairman of Bausch & Lomb, is explicit about this, “The passive-aggressives are ones who on the surface appear to be nodding their heads, but they are actually not with you and they undermine culture change in a big way. You’ve got to get these types out of the system as soon as possible.”[12] Bottom line: “true resistors” must be dealt with directly, and if need be, in a harsh manner (i.e., firing).

## Conclusion

Some people might argue that at its most basic element, leadership is really about change. The role of a senior leader is to anticipate threats and opportunities and to help the organization address these issues. This chapter has described why this process is becoming more important as the pace of change accelerates in the business world, and how leaders might think about implementing change. We have provided both a conceptual framework to think about different types of change that might confront leaders and offered a set of practical steps that a leader might take to increase the probability of successful change. In Section 5, we have included a detailed case on the leading of a major change effort at Arizona State University over the past twenty years. The case represents an institutional setting (i.e., universities) in which major change is especially challenging.
