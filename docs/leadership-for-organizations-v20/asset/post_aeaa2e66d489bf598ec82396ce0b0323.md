## Two Caveats for Effective Followership

Sometimes it is not easy or straightforward to be an effective follower. There are a few caveats for effective followership that need to be mentioned. First, as stressed throughout Chapter 3, “it takes two to tango.” So just as effective followership is important to realize effective leadership, the reverse is also true—effective leadership is important for effective followership. If leaders do not encourage and reinforce independent, critical thinking and high levels of engagement on the part of followers, it is not likely to materialize. Instead, we are more likely to see either conformist or alienated followers.

We should further acknowledge that, in addition to the leader, organizational environments may or may not encourage effective followership. That is, aside from the leaders themselves, some contexts encourage independent thinking and engagement. In these environments, followers are likely to see other role models of effective followership, and they are likely to draw courage from others to challenge the status quo by being critical, independent thinkers. In short, it is easier to be an effective follower in some organizational contexts, as compared to others. Again, we return to a principle that was laid out in Chapter 1; specifically, that effective leadership processes involve three elements working in concert: the leader, the follower, and the context.

Second, loyalty can sometimes be taken too far—in terms of loyalty to either the leader, or the organization. Regarding both leaders and the greater organization, there may be times when excessive loyalty can be harmful, especially when moral values or even laws have been compromised. When this happens, the effective follower should no longer be blindly loyal, and instead, should speak up. A first step is to follow the maxim of “speaking truth to power.” The hope is that proper steps will be taken to change behavior or policies. As an example, Michael Woodford had been hired to be CEO by Olympus, the camera producer, several years ago. He discovered fraudulent behavior within the firm in order to cover up financial losses. He went to the Board of Directors, who essentially oversaw all actions and policies of the firm, including the CEO. However, the Board did nothing, except fire Woodford when he went public with the story. In the end, the Board ended up resigning, and several senior executives pleaded guilty to fraud.[6] In this particular instance, Woodford did not stay loyal to his superiors, nor to the organization that they were attempting to uphold. However, it could be argued that by “speaking truth to power,” he was defending the organization as it should be, rather than as it was.

In essence, the above example represents whistleblowing, and the risks for the individual who engages in such behavior. Whistleblowing is the disclosure by a person, usually an employee in a government agency or private enterprise, to the public or to those in authority, of mismanagement, corruption, illegality, or some other wrongdoing.[7] Whistleblowing is an important, albeit risky (i.e., risky to the whistleblower), mechanism in organizations and societies to reduce the possibility of the over-exertion of power. As such, it represents the ultimate form of “speaking truth to power.” On the other hand, it can also be controversial, especially if carried out in ways that have detrimental side effects. As an example, Edward Snowden leaked classified material publicly of the National Security Association in an effort to expose what he considered to be the over-surveillance of the American people. It could be argued that a better form of whistleblowing in his instance might have been to take his case to others in power, such as U.S. representatives in Congress or the Senate.[8]

## 4.2 Managing Up

> managing up
> Closely aligned with being an effective follower;
> involves building a good work relationship with the leader, while simultaneously getting one's needs met.

The idea of managing up is closely aligned with being an effective follower. It essentially involves building a good work relationship with the leader, while simultaneously getting one’s needs met. In many ways, it mirrors what we considered in Chapter 3 in terms of how leaders build relationships with followers. But in this instance, the table is turned, and the onus is on the follower to attempt to develop the relationship. To some, this may seem counterintuitive. After all, isn’t the burden of the leader to worry about relationship building with followers? The obvious answer is of course; however, it does not negate the necessity of followers doing their part by properly managing up. Here we consider several key aspects of managing up:

- accept feedback and direction;
- work with, rather than against, the leader’s style and goals; and
- role model the behavior that you would like to see from the leader.

### Accept Feedback and Direction

> feedback
> Information that is provided to an individual pertaining to that person’s behavior and performance.

Feedback involves information that is provided to an individual pertaining to that person’s behavior and performance. In Chapter 7, we will deal with the importance of accepting feedback from a range of sources. For now, suffice it to say that to manage up, one must first listen to, and accept, the feedback that is coming down from the leader. A strong relationship cannot be built by a follower with his or her leader if the follower is consistently not listening to, or devaluing, the advice or ideas that are provided by the leader. In some ways, it is again a balancing act. As mentioned earlier, effective followership is largely about independent, critical thinking. Good leaders will expect and accept such an attribute on the part of followers. But on the other hand, it can be taken too far. In other words, sometimes independent, critical thinkers shut out the ideas and advice of others. When this happens on the part of a follower, that individual will have a difficult time forming a good relationship with the leader, and the follower is not likely to get his or her needs met.

Let’s take a hypothetical example. Suppose that a follower has an idea on a project that could have much relevance to the effectiveness of that person’s boss and even the greater organization. He is proud of his idea since it represents an innovative approach that nobody else on that person’s team, including his boss, has considered. In short, the follower has shown independent, critical thinking by deriving the idea. When presented to his boss, she acknowledges the innovativeness of the idea, and thanks him for it. But at the same time, she provides reasons for why the idea should not be pursued, at least at this point in time. What should the follower do in order to properly manage up? Should he continue to pursue the idea, perhaps even going around his boss to other people (e.g., coworkers)? Should he attempt to find a compromise, or simply come back to the idea at a later point in time? There is no easy answer to these questions. However, for the purpose of managing up, it might behoove the follower to at least temporarily back off, and maybe reintroduce the idea later.
