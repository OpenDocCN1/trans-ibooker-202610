On the other hand, cross-cultural engagement does not imply that the global leader will set aside all of his or her own cultural values. For example, American culture emphasizes equality in terms of race, ethnicity, and gender. However, such norms and values are often lacking in other cultures. When engaging cross-culturally, the idea is to understand and avoid negative judgments about the norms, beliefs, and values that are prevalent in another culture. But at the same time, it does not imply simply accepting those aspects of the other culture and behaving accordingly.

Along similar lines, it is quite possible that a leader could feel tension in having a sense of patriotism or identity toward his or her own home country, while simultaneously having a global orientation. This sense of patriotism and identity is analogous to the concept of nationalism. That concept has been much maligned because of how some people would associate nationalism with negative phenomena, such as a feeling of superiority, disdain for or prejudice toward other groups, or isolationism. However, at its core, nationalism is simply about a strong sense of pride in one's own cultural background. That said, we recognize that the tension between nationalism (or country-specific patriotism) versus globalism represents yet another paradox that leaders will need to increasingly navigate as we look toward the future.

This tension can be seen in recent developments at Google. Despite some push-back from employees and other constituencies, leaders at Google moved ahead on plans to build a search engine in China that would allow for government-based censorship.[20] At the same time, the firm appears to be increasingly hesitant to work cooperatively with the U.S. Department of Defense.[21] Actions of this nature could be viewed as taking an imbalanced global stance, while not reflecting a sense of country-specific patriotism that many employees or customers might desire. In short, leaders at firms like Google are likely to increasingly face the paradox of dealing with nationalistic demands (or serving the interests of one's home country) on the part of various constituencies, while simultaneously pursuing global initiatives. We maintain that it is possible, and even largely desirable, for leaders to be both nationalists and globalists simultaneously. In other words, as noted in a recent article in the Harvard Business Review, nationalism versus globalism represents a false dichotomy.[22] It is possible, and even desirable, to pursue both simultaneously.

# 10.4 Avoiding Misinterpretations

The possibility of misinterpretations represents a key pitfall in cross-cultural interactions. A lack of cultural self-awareness, combined with a lack of awareness of other cultures with which one is dealing, can easily lead to misinterpretations and poor communication. These problems can occur in the course of business or social interactions. Consider the Saudi businessman who attempts to hold the hand of his male American counterpart as they walk together down a hall. Such behavior might be considered as “normal” by the Saudi, but could be misinterpreted as a sexual gesture by the American.

As another example, buyer/seller negotiations represent a common situation in which business people and leaders from different cultures might find themselves. Consider the case of a small team of Japanese negotiators interacting with a small team of American counterparts. Although the negotiations take place in Japan, they are conducted in English, which would not be uncommon.
