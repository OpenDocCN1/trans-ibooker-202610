A great deal of research has addressed cognitive and skill-based attributes of effective global leaders, although much less attention has been devoted to more emotional or affective qualities.[15] Here, we suggest that both know-how and emotional factors are required for global leaders. But what are such qualities all about?

## Global Orientation Factors

Based on emerging research, we identify three key factors that are associated with a global orientation on the part of leaders: (1) global business know-how, (2) cross-cultural know-how, and (3) cross-cultural engagement. Together, these factors represent both the cognitive and emotional make-up that is essential for a leader attempting to influence in a global context. We address each of these factors separately.

### Global business know-how

Business tends to be conducted differently in different countries around the world. Accordingly, the global leader needs to possess knowledge in such industry-level areas as customers, competitors, supply chains, and risks of doing business. A leader will also need know-how regarding how government and government regulations affect doing business in different countries, as well as the extent to which doing business coincides with non-business, cultural norms.

A classic example is the extent to which business and socializing, especially through the intake of alcohol, might mix. In cultures such as that of the U.S., there is the common cultural norm of “not mixing business and pleasure.” In other words, business pursuits are largely kept separate from personal or social activities. However, in many other cultures (e.g., Asian cultures) business and socializing are heavily intertwined. That is, the norm is that business largely gets accomplished through social activities that might involve the consuming of alcohol.

### Cross-cultural know-how

As mentioned earlier, it is important for a leader to first have knowledge of his or her own culture. Based on that knowledge, the leader can form a better understanding of other cultures, which is essential for effectiveness in a global leader role. Cross-cultural know-how pertains to a variety of issues including politics, religion, art, and history of cultures. Oftentimes in other cultures, these issues end up getting intertwined with how business is done.[16] For example, given that business and social pursuits can blend together, discussions of things like local politics and history can naturally arise in the course of discussing business matters with customers, suppliers, government officials, and even local employees. While no global leader is expected to be an expert on the cultures in which he or she is doing business, a rudimentary knowledge of basic issues will give the leader more credence. And perhaps more importantly, asking questions and showing curiosity may be even more important than a high level of expertise.

### Cross-cultural engagement

Global leaders are likely to face challenges that might be emotionally taxing. They need to not only have knowledge of other cultures. In addition, they must continually engage people and groups from other cultures, and then adapt to the multicultural environments in which they are immersed.[17] To engage means to show emotional enthusiasm, excitement, curiosity, and passion about people and ways of doing things in different parts of the world.[18] Note that it also means that these positive emotions will outweigh more negative ones, such as fear and anxiety. Engagement further implies having a high degree of openness towards diversity and life as it is experienced in different countries. Leaders with a strong desire to engage cross-culturally anticipate and experience a feeling of joy and excitement in unfamiliar cultural environments. For example, there is evidence that expatriates who show a lack of passion for diversity and engaging in cross-cultural contexts tend to experience failure.[19]

On the other hand, cross-cultural engagement does not imply that the global leader will set aside all of his or her own cultural values. For example, American culture emphasizes equality in terms of race, ethnicity, and gender. However, such norms and values are often lacking in other cultures. When engaging cross-culturally, the idea is to understand and avoid negative judgments about the norms, beliefs, and values that are prevalent in another culture. But at the same time, it does not imply simply accepting those aspects of the other culture and behaving accordingly.

Along similar lines, it is quite possible that a leader could feel tension in having a sense of patriotism or identity toward his or her own home country, while simultaneously having a global orientation. This sense of patriotism and identity is analogous to the concept of nationalism. That concept has been much maligned because of how some people would associate nationalism with negative phenomena, such as a feeling of superiority, disdain for or prejudice toward other groups, or isolationism. However, at its core, nationalism is simply about a strong sense of pride in one's own cultural background. That said, we recognize that the tension between nationalism (or country-specific patriotism) versus globalism represents yet another paradox that leaders will need to increasingly navigate as we look toward the future.

This tension can be seen in recent developments at Google. Despite some push-back from employees and other constituencies, leaders at Google moved ahead on plans to build a search engine in China that would allow for government-based censorship.[20] At the same time, the firm appears to be increasingly hesitant to work cooperatively with the U.S. Department of Defense.[21] Actions of this nature could be viewed as taking an imbalanced global stance, while not reflecting a sense of country-specific patriotism that many employees or customers might desire. In short, leaders at firms like Google are likely to increasingly face the paradox of dealing with nationalistic demands (or serving the interests of one's home country) on the part of various constituencies, while simultaneously pursuing global initiatives. We maintain that it is possible, and even largely desirable, for leaders to be both nationalists and globalists simultaneously. In other words, as noted in a recent article in the Harvard Business Review, nationalism versus globalism represents a false dichotomy.[22] It is possible, and even desirable, to pursue both simultaneously.

## 10.4 Avoiding Misinterpretations

The possibility of misinterpretations represents a key pitfall in cross-cultural interactions. A lack of cultural self-awareness, combined with a lack of awareness of other cultures with which one is dealing, can easily lead to misinterpretations and poor communication. These problems can occur in the course of business or social interactions. Consider the Saudi businessman who attempts to hold the hand of his male American counterpart as they walk together down a hall. Such behavior might be considered as “normal” by the Saudi, but could be misinterpreted as a sexual gesture by the American.

As another example, buyer/seller negotiations represent a common situation in which business people and leaders from different cultures might find themselves. Consider the case of a small team of Japanese negotiators interacting with a small team of American counterparts.
