# TABLE 10.1 American Versus Other Cultures: Leader Problem-Solving and Decision-Making

| American | Non-Western |
| :--- | :--- |
| 1. Difficult situations should be viewed as problems to be solved | 1. Acceptance of, and dealing with problematic situations the way that they actually are |
| 2. "Get it in writing" | 2. Relationship and trust building |
| 3. Linear or sequential thinking when working through the issues of a deal | 3. Integrative or holistic thinking when working through the issues of a deal |

Second, America tends to be a litigious society, which goes by the saying, “get it in writing.” However, many other cultures are not so litigious, and deals are made more through time taken to build relationships and trust. This type of thinking can confuse an American leader who might want to consummate a deal quickly by getting corporate lawyers to simply put details in a contract, and having that contract signed by all parties concerned. In contrast, instead of relying on lengthy contracts, leaders from other cultural contexts may want to move slowly and build a relationship, perhaps through social activities outside of the work setting.

Third, American leaders are largely linear or sequential thinkers when it comes to deal-making, while other cultures (e.g., especially Asian cultures) may be more integrative or holistic in their approach to a deal. Take the case of a buyer-seller relationship, which typically can involve various aspects of the deal—quantity of product or services that are sold, delivery date, service agreements, and so forth. The typical American thinking would be to settle each aspect of the deal sequentially before moving on to the next aspect. However, leaders from other cultures might want to take a more holistic approach where all aspects of the deal are continually negotiated until everything is settled simultaneously at the end of a bargaining process.

Fourth, there is more of a tendency to delegate decision-making, or at least attempt to delegate, in American culture. Some American students may be thinking that you knew a leader who was not so adept at delegating, and there are many cases of such leadership. But overall, there is at least more of a tendency to delegate decision-making and responsibility in American or Western cultures, as compared to other cultures in which there is an expectation that leaders do not delegate. Instead, it is their role to make decisions, while subordinates implement those decisions.

Fifth, American leaders are expected to be quick in their decision-making, as well as sometimes bold with the decisions that are made. Perhaps you have heard the expression, “paralysis by analysis,” which implies that leaders should not over-analyze a problem and instead, should take quick action. In contrast, in other cultures, the norm is to move more slowly and more cautiously when it comes to decision-making.

Finally, in American culture, it is the job of the leader to help followers solve problems for themselves. To do so, American leaders might refer followers to various information sources or provide them with training. But in many other cultures, the job of the leader is to be the expert who can provide answers for an array of issues that their followers might bring to them. In other words, leaders are expected to have all of the answers. After all, isn’t this why they were chosen to be leaders in the first place? Such thinking may seem foreign to the typical American mind because American culture stresses that nobody is perfect or has all of the answers, including people in leadership roles.
