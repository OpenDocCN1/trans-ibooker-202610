### 2.1 Leader Traits

trait
A distinguishing quality or characteristic (i.e., physical, cognitive, emotional).

A trait is a distinguishing quality or characteristic (i.e., physical, cognitive, emotional). For many years, researchers and practitioners (e.g., leadership coaches and trainers) have been attempting to identify individual traits, or sets of traits, that can distinguish leaders from non-leaders. Formally, this has become known as the “Great Man approach” to understanding effective leadership.[1] Over time, a number of traits have been potentially identified including both physical and psychological traits. As an example of the former, researchers have looked at such physical qualities as gender, height, and physical appearance. As can be seen in the term “Great Man,” the initial thinking (or bias) in this approach was that only men could truly be leaders. As we will see throughout this book, nothing could be further from the truth, and indeed, women have as much or more potential for effective leadership. Overall, the search for physical qualities that would separate out people who might be effective leaders has not been very successful or consistent. But with that said, some research would point toward the relevance of certain psychological or emotional traits, or combinations of such qualities. We separate these traits into the following categories.

- Psychological capital
- Self and other awareness [including emotional intelligence]
- Motive profile
- Character
- Complexity and paradoxical nature

### Psychological Capital

People who are seen as leaders tend to be positive. In other words, they are anything but “Debbie Downers.” Psychological capital is a term that pertains to positive characteristics of leaders. Let’s take a look at the four factors that make up a leaders’ psychological capital.[2] First, leaders tend to have and retain hope. There are many factors that can drive down hope, such as economic uncertainty, chaos or division in society, and so forth. But through it all, leaders with strong psychological capital retain their hope.

Second, leaders maintain their strong self-efficacy. That is, they are confident in what they can do and what can be accomplished. Without such confidence, they may not attempt "stretch" goals that extend themselves and their followers beyond what they might have originally conceived as possible. In the movie, Invictus, Nelson Mandela (played by Morgan Freeman) asked a captain of the national rugby team, Francois Pienaar (played by Matt Damon), how a leader can inspire people to achieve more than they expect they can achieve. It first takes self-efficacy on the part of a leader to raise up the self-efficacies of their followers. We will consider inspirational leadership further in Chapter 11.

> **grit**
> Courage and resolve.

Third, leaders maintain resilience, and they persist, even in the face of setbacks. Disappointments and lack of success are bound to happen as people pursue goals. However, effective leaders understand that failures are inevitable, and they are part of learning. They possess resilience, which enables them to persist in the face of setbacks, rather than quickly giving up. There is a saying that "winners never quit, and quitters never win." Leaders take this saying to heart. In short, the common denominator is that leaders have grit—in other words, courage and resolve. On the other hand, it is important for leaders to understand the fine line between resilience and persistence on the one hand, and "cutting bait" on the other. That said, leaders tend not to "cut bait" too easily.

Fourth, leaders portray optimism. Colin Powell, the former Army general and Secretary of State, claimed that "Perpetual optimism is a force multiplier."[45] Optimism is key to leadership because it gives people who might follow the leader a sense of hope, and it is very much related to the idea of transformational leadership, which we will consider in Chapter 11 of this book.

A theme that runs through all of the factors of psychological capital is that the leaders who possess them have a passion for the goals that they pursue. That passion is accompanied by a high level of energy. When combined, their passion and energy tend to be contagious, which is a key reason why others follow their lead. Steve Jobs, co-founder of Apple, was notorious for his deep passion and energy to produce high-tech products that could be made both useful and simple for consumers. His followers were strongly influenced by him, despite his tendencies to be critical and even abusive at times.[46] They were caught up in his ideas, passion, and drive to see those ideas come to fruition in terms of personal computers, cell phones, and other products.

It should be noted that all of these qualities are not only relevant to leaders, but entrepreneurs as well. Successful entrepreneurs are known for their passion, energy, optimism, resilience, and persistence. Without these qualities, they might succumb to naysayers or doubters who would question their ideas and not provide necessary support, such as initial financial support.

**internal locus of control**

One's belief that by and large, the things that happen to that individual in life are due to his or her own actions and efforts. On the other hand, a person with an external locus of control believes that luck, fate, or outside forces (e.g., other people or events) are the determinants of his or her outcomes in life.

**Internal locus of control** is related to the qualities described above. This characteristic pertains to one's beliefs about fate or luck, versus personal control. An individual with a strong locus of control believes that by and large, the things that happen to that individual in life are due to his or her own actions and efforts. On the other hand, a person with an external locus of control believes that luck, fate, or outside forces (e.g., other people or events) are the determinants of his or her options in life. Can you guess which type of individual would probably be seen as more of a leader? If you guess the internal locus of control, you are correct. People tend to follow those who believe that they are the masters of their own fate.

As an example, Nelson Mandela, while imprisoned for twenty-seven years, maintained a mindset that he was the master of his own fate, not his captors. This mindset inspired not only himself to endure the hardships of prison, but also others around him. In communication with fellow prisoners on Robben Island in South Africa, he would frequently quote the words of Winston Churchill, “We are still masters of our fate. We still are captains of our souls.”[5] David Neeleman was the founder and CEO of JetBlue Airways, but in 2007 he was fired. After getting over the shock, he said, “The worst thing in life is to be a victim.”[6] He moved to Brazil and founded a new airline, Azul, which is now very successful. His resilience stems from his internal locus of control.

### Self and Other Awareness

Leaders are people who are aware not only of themselves, but also other people to whom they relate in some manner. Self-awareness implies an understanding of one’s own beliefs, emotions, values, strengths, and weaknesses. Take the example of a person who strongly values community service and social responsibility. If that individual does not understand why he or she possesses such values, it would be hard to explain to or convince others (i.e., those who might follow this person’s lead) why certain actions are necessary. Further, by understanding how one’s social responsibility values can be blended with the fiduciary responsibilities of the firm, the leader will have a better chance of “selling” those values to employees, customers, shareholders, and so forth.

On the other hand, effective leaders are also aware of others’ strengths, motivators and desires, and fears and self-doubts. Most importantly, leaders can recognize how these things differ across individuals. Armed with such capabilities, leaders are able to better influence people toward goals. Liz Smith is the CEO of Bloomin Brands, a $4 billion restaurant chain with 1,500 locations and 100,000 employees. When asked what she thought was the most important trait for a leader, she replied, “The one thing you need more than anything else as a leader is self-awareness.”[7]

### Emotional Intelligence

In Chapter 1, we considered differences between “management” and “leadership”. One key difference pertains to affect or emotions. In managerial terms, emotions can represent a nuisance, and something to be either ignored or dismissed as irrelevant in work settings. In other words, managers tend to think in more task-oriented terms about getting work done in as efficient a manner as possible. But to leaders, emotions are to be embraced and harnessed, and they are key to understanding themselves, as well as how to influence others. Emotions can come in many forms—including both positive and negative emotions. Positive emotions pertain to things like joy, excitement, and enthusiasm, while negative emotions involve sadness, fear, anger, and anxiety. It should not be surprising that the ability to deal with one’s own as well as others’ emotions, or what has become known as emotional intelligence, is a key personal quality that is relevant to effective leadership.[8]
