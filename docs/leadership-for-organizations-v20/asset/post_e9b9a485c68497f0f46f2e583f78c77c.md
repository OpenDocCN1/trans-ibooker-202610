
Beyond change, transformational leadership is also about developing a bond between leaders and followers, largely through the building of trust. More transactional forms of leadership, such as the path-goal approach that we addressed in Chapter 2, are oriented toward getting goals accomplished in the here-and-now. While transactional leadership can be beneficial, it does not help to build the long-term bonds and trust that accompanies transformational leadership.

## 11.3 Mission as a Basis for New Visions

**mission** Represents a relatively enduring core purpose of an organization that is established by its founding leader(s), and is its reason for existing.

As previously mentioned, vision is a core element of what makes a leader inspirational. Before describing the nature of leader vision, it is important to consider the connection to an organization’s mission. Mission (also known as core purpose) represents a relatively enduring quality of an organization that is established by its founding leader(s). It endures despite changing technologies, economic circumstances, and other changes in an organization’s environment.[13] Mission is essentially the organization’s broad purpose or reason for existing. Although it is not a vision per se, mission provides a basis for creating new visions as the organization develops over time.

The nature of a firm’s mission can vary, which has implications for how it is managed, and whether or not key stakeholders (e.g., investors, potential employees, and customers) are drawn to the firm. Moreover, the nature of its mission can affect firm performance over time. So how exactly can a firm’s mission vary, and why is firm performance affected? Think of mission as a continuum that goes from narrow to broad. When narrow, mission is framed in terms of a specific product or monetary goal (e.g., profits). When broader, mission is framed in terms of: (1) innovation, (2) excellence, or (3) socially-based, core purpose. Some examples are shown in Table 11.1.[14]

### TABLE 11.1 Alternative Core Purpose (Mission) Statements

| Narrow, and not Actual (but could have been possible) | Broader, and Actual |
| :--- | :--- |
| Mary Kay: Sell cosmetics | Mary Kay: Enrich the lives of women |
| 3M: Make adhesive and abrasives | 3M: Have a perpetual quest to solve problems |
| Dollar General: Sell things for less than $1 and still make a profit | Dollar General: Give low income people a good deal |
| Liberty Mutual: Sell insurance | Liberty Mutual: Help people live safer, more secure lives |
| General Electric: Be competitive in each industry in which it does business | General Electric: Be either #1 or #2 in each industry in which it does business |

| Starbucks: Sell cups of coffee to go (or to drink in the store) | Starbucks: ??? |

The “Narrow, and not Actual” mission statements on the left side of Table 11.1 are all hypothetical. In other words, they could have been the actual mission statements of these organizations, but because of the original and subsequent leaders of these firms, those “Narrow, and not Actual” statements were not the actual basis of these firms’ missions over time. In contrast, the “Broader, and Actual” mission statements on the right side of Table 11.1 were in reality implemented over time. Our purpose in displaying the left side of the table is simply to form a hypothetical comparison with these firms' actual missions on the right side.

The common denominator of the hypothetical statements on the left side is that they focus on a particular product or profits. On the right side of Table 11.1, we see a different story or theme. All of these statements are broader in their own way. An example of an innovation theme can be seen with 3M. The GE mission was reshaped under the leadership of perhaps its most famous CEO, Jack Welch. It clearly represents an excellence theme. A number of these themes are clearly social purposes in nature. For example, Mary Kay Ash established her firm for the core purpose of enriching the lives of women. "Enriching" to some degree took on a literal meaning in that she stressed how her employees could make money through their efforts, and thus achieve security. When her firm was first developing in the 1970s and 1980s, this was very important, since women had few options in the workforce for the purpose of making substantial money and having a sense of security through their work. It should be noted that sometimes a firm may wish to combine these themes, as is the case with the Motorola mission, which combines innovation and social purpose.

Three things need to be emphasized when comparing the potential (but hypothetical) missions on the left side of Table 11.1 with those on the right side. First, those on the right side are more compelling and inspiring. For example, Joel Peterson is the former CEO of a large real estate company, the current Chairman of JetBlue Airways, and a professor at Stanford. He has said, "People have values, dreams, and goals. If people believe that you can help them live this and do something meaningful, they'll follow you."[15] Brad Smith, the CEO of Intuit, echoes this notion by stating that "People want to be part of a cause greater than themselves. They want to solve important problems that matter to their mother and father, their friends... so have a cause, don't just be a product."[16] Kevin Johnson, the NBA all-star and former mayor of Sacramento, California, has said "You need to make people feel like they are doing something bigger than their individual work."[17] Indeed, potential employees, customers, and even investors tend to get more excited by the "bigger" statements on the right side. In the case of Liberty Mutual, it is mundane to be guided by a simple mission of selling insurance. On the other hand, since the overarching mission is actually to help people live safer and more secure lives, employees and other stakeholders are more likely to be inspired. Of course, this assumes that organizational leaders and practices (e.g. human resources practices) reinforce the mission, rather than treating it as simply a slogan.

Second, missions on the right side of Table 11.1 tend to be more sustainable over time. Take the case of Motorola. In its early days, Motorola started off just focusing its product lines on televisions. A key competitor was Zenith. But unlike Motorola, the leaders of Zenith focused its mission on just making better televisions, while Motorola's mission was broader. When other firms (largely from Japan) became highly competitive in the television market, Zenith was not able to compete, and eventually was taken over by LG Electronics, based in South Korea. In modern times, for the most part, LG has dropped the Zenith brand from its portfolio of products. On the other hand, Motorola has used its mission to continually innovate and produce products other than televisions. It continues to thrive as an organization.

Third, the establishment of a narrow mission, such as those seen on the left side of Table 11.1, tend to restrict change and new visions. When a narrow mission or core purpose is ingrained into the culture of an organization, people do not think about or potentially accept radically new visions. Nevertheless, as we will see in Chapter 13, such new visions are important for the revitalization and even survival of firms.

How do your depictions of their missions differ from what you see on the left side of Table 11.1? Apple began in the late 1970s producing and selling personal computers. At the firm's inception, that was indeed the mission. However, in the case of Apple, the mission did change over time. What if it had not changed? Would Apple have become the giant firm that we see today?

## 11.4 Effective Leader Vision

Like goals, vision represents a future-oriented articulation on the part of leaders. However, goals tend to be very tangible and short-term (e.g., monetary goals like profits or market share), while vision is less tangible and oriented toward a long timeframe. Accordingly, a basic feature of visionary leadership is that it is oriented toward the future. How do you stack up in terms of having a future orientation? To help answer this question, try taking the survey that is in Section 6.

As a classic example of leader vision, in 1961, President John Kennedy went in front of Congress and then made speeches to the public about how he envisioned the United States putting a man on the moon, and returning him back to the earth safely, by the end of the decade. Below, we describe five key elements of effective leader vision. As we consider these elements, we will refer back to President Kennedy's vision.

- 1. Long-term and bold. In their book, Built To Last: Successful Habits of Visionary Companies, Collins and Porras coined the acronym **BHAG**, which stands for “big hairy audacious goal.”[18] A BHAG largely characterizes what vision is all about. It is both long-term and bold. If President Kennedy had proclaimed that the United States should put a man in space and return him to earth, it would have been neither long-term nor bold, since at the time, the country was on the cusp of already doing so. But the idea of putting a man on the moon was a bold and daring stretch.

- 2. Change-oriented. A vision does not involve relatively incremental tweaks to the status quo. Instead, it is highly change oriented, and can even be described as radical in nature. Moreover, in order to justify radical change, visionary leaders point toward important environmental changes and threats. For example, as a justification for his vision, President Kennedy pointed toward threats from the Soviet Union, and what was termed the “space race” at the time.

- 3. Reflects high ideals and values, provides meaning, and energizes. Similar to the core purpose or mission statements that we see on the right side of Table 11.1, vision is all about high ideals and values. The pursuit of these ideals and values, in turn, provides meaning to people, energizes them emotionally, and brings them together as a collective. In the case of President Kennedy's vision, values such as innovation and exploration, which were already deep within the American psyche, were brought to bear. As a result, people across the United States felt energized and excited to make the vision become a reality.

BHAG Big hairy audacious goal; oftentimes used to characterize what vision is all about because it is both long-term and bold.
