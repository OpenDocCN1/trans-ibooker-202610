# 4.2 Managing Up

> managing up
> Closely aligned with being an effective follower;
> involves building a good work relationship with the leader, while simultaneously getting one's needs met.

The idea of managing up is closely aligned with being an effective follower. It essentially involves building a good work relationship with the leader, while simultaneously getting one’s needs met. In many ways, it mirrors what we considered in Chapter 3 in terms of how leaders build relationships with followers. But in this instance, the table is turned, and the onus is on the follower to attempt to develop the relationship. To some, this may seem counterintuitive. After all, isn’t the burden of the leader to worry about relationship building with followers? The obvious answer is of course; however, it does not negate the necessity of followers doing their part by properly managing up. Here we consider several key aspects of managing up: (1) accept feedback and direction; (2) work with, rather than against, the leader’s style and goals; and (3) role model the behavior that you would like to see from the leader.

# Accept Feedback and Direction

> feedback
> Information that is provided to an individual pertaining to that person’s behavior and performance.

Feedback involves information that is provided to an individual pertaining to that person’s behavior and performance. In Chapter 7, we will deal with the importance of accepting feedback from a range of sources. For now, suffice it to say that to manage up, one must first listen to, and accept, the feedback that is coming down from the leader. A strong relationship cannot be built by a follower with his or her leader if the follower is consistently not listening to, or devaluing, the advice or ideas that are provided by the leader. In some ways, it is again a balancing act. As mentioned earlier, effective followership is largely about independent, critical thinking. Good leaders will expect and accept such an attribute on the part of followers. But on the other hand, it can be taken too far. In other words, sometimes independent, critical thinkers shut out the ideas and advice of others. When this happens on the part of a follower, that individual will have a difficult time forming a good relationship with the leader, and the follower is not likely to get his or her needs met.

Let’s take a hypothetical example. Suppose that a follower has an idea on a project that could have much relevance to the effectiveness of that person’s boss and even the greater organization. He is proud of his idea since it represents an innovative approach that nobody else on that person’s team, including his boss, has considered. In short, the follower has shown independent, critical thinking by deriving the idea. When presented to his boss, she acknowledges the innovativeness of the idea, and thanks him for it. But at the same time, she provides reasons for why the idea should not be pursued, at least at this point in time. What should the follower do in order to properly manage up? Should he continue to pursue the idea, perhaps even going around his boss to other people (e.g., coworkers)? Should he attempt to find a compromise, or simply come back to the idea at a later point in time? There is no easy answer to these questions. However, for the purpose of managing up, it might behoove the follower to at least temporarily back off, and maybe reintroduce the idea later.
