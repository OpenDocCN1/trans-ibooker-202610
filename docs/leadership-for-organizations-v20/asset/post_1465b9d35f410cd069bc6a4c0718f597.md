
## 2. People can change or better themselves under the right conditions.

For many years, the U.S. has been known as the country of opportunity, which is why so many people have wanted to immigrate to the U.S. That is, they come from countries where the socioeconomic class in which one is born becomes fixed for life at birth. In contrast, in the United States, cultural norms and values encourage people to better themselves; for example, through education. In recent times, some people may question the extent to which all groups in the U.S. have equal opportunity in terms of bettering oneself. But nevertheless, the overall principle remains characteristic of American culture.

## 3. Individualism.

The U.S. has historically been known for its “rugged individualism.” In other words, it has traditionally encouraged, celebrated, and rewarded individualism efforts. Other cultures tend to be more collective in nature, with individualism being discouraged. For example, an old saying in Japan is, “the nail that sticks out will get hammered in.” With all of that in mind, more collective sentiment and efforts nevertheless exist within American culture, especially when people feel threatened by outsiders, as is the case during war or major terrorist attacks. In recent times, the COVID-19 pandemic stimulated a more collective sentiment, as could be seen in such phrases as “we are all in this together.” Indeed, a form of dualism exists in the American culture that in some ways celebrates individualism, while also encouraging collectivism. For example, there is the saying that “There is no I in team” or a member should sometimes “Take one for the team.” Dualism is similar to the paradox theme that is stressed throughout this book. In other words, leaders need to embrace and try to harmonize seemingly disparate phenomena that they encounter.

## 4. Universalism.

Americans traditionally have tended to be universalists, rather than particularists or what is also termed relativists.[9] Universalists believe that there are certain principles or values that hold true across all contexts. For example, Americans tend to believe that telling lies or acting hypocritically is always a bad thing. In contrast, particularists will tend to believe that it all depends on the situation or context. For example, if one must tell a lie or simply omit part of the truth to serve the needs of a family member or friend, so be it in the mind of a particularist. With that said, once again, cultural dualism comes into play. For example, although universalism has been more a traditional element of American culture, particularism appears to be on the rise among many people.[10]

## 5. “Doing” is better than “being.”

Do you “work to live”, or do you “live to work”? While many Americans might want to think of themselves as working to live, in reality, for many Americans, work becomes a form of identity or even calling—that is they “live to work.” In other words, the American culture is very performance-oriented, and work life is central to who a person is.[11] As such, career goals will often become equal or greater in value than personal life goals (e.g., having and devoting time to family). In contrast, in a “being” culture, people tend to place more value on non-work activities, such as family and enjoying life outside of work. Given the looseness of American culture mentioned above, there is certainly variance among Americans in terms of this aspect of culture. However, “doing” and accomplishing things, especially in association with one’s work life, is predominant in American culture.

## 6. Time should be revered and carefully managed.

Have you ever heard the expression that “time is money”? Some cultures, especially “doing” cultures, tend to revere and value time. American culture is one example. Other cultures are more nonchalant or carefree with regard to time. Thus, in those cultures, if an individual arrives 15 minutes late for a meeting, the common sentiment might be: who should care?

## 7. The past is gone, and only the present and future are really important.

The band Fleetwood Mac is famous for the song “Don’t Stop.” In this song, they talk about focusing on the future, rather than the past. This song is emblematic of American culture.

In many ways, Americans do not dwell on the past or value history. Instead, the focus is on the present and future possibilities. While there is some value in stressing the present and future, there is also merit in understanding history so that there is less of a tendency to not repeat the mistakes of the past. However, some other cultures may value an understanding of history more than is evident in the American culture.

## 8. Openness and directness.

Americans tend to be open to other people, even strangers, rather than remaining private or aloof. As an example, Americans tend to smile at, and even say hello, to strangers on the street. In a similar vein, Americans tend to value being direct and to the point. As an example, when Americans say “yes,” they mean yes, and when Americans say “no,” they mean no. To some people reading this characterization, it may seem so obvious as to elicit the thought of how anyone person who does not practice such directness must be dishonest. But this is exactly the point when it comes to understanding our own culture, and how it might differ from the cultural norms and values elsewhere. That is, certain aspects of behaving may seem so obvious to us that we do not even realize that they are the result of cultural influence, and that norms and values may not be the same in different cultures. For example, in a culture like Japan, it is considered to be impolite and even provocative to say “no,” even if that is how one feels. In other words, it is not dishonest to avoid saying “no,” even though that might reflect one’s true feelings or intentions. So in Japan, instead of coming right out and saying “no,” the individual might instead say something like “that would be difficult” or use body language that would be suggestive of “no” in that culture.

As a note of caution, while making distinctions between cultures can be helpful, it can also be overdone, which can then lead to inappropriate stereotyping of cultures and their people. While there are noticeable differences between cultures, there are also noticeable differences in cultural values, beliefs, and norms within a given culture, such as the American culture. An understanding of one’s cultural background, as well as that of others, must take this fact into account.

Finally, we will add one more aspect to our characterization of American culture. And unfortunately, despite other more positive aspects of its culture (e.g., the norm that people can change or better themselves), this aspect is not so positive. Specifically, American culture tends to be dominated by parochialism, and even ethnocentrism and arrogance, regarding cultural norms and values. In other words, while the rest of the world tends to be quite aware of what American culture is all about, Americans typically have little knowledge of other cultures. Moreover, Americans oftentimes believe that their culture is superior to others. As an example, since they highly revere time, an American tourist in France is likely to expect fast service in a Parisian restaurant. However, in Paris, such service would seem highly disrespectful, since it would be perceived by the Parisian service provider as impolitely hurrying the dining experience.
