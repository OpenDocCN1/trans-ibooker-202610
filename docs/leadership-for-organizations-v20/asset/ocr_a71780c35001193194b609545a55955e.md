The answer is to be found in the power of norms, or social expectations, and how in many situations, we look to either the context or people in leadership positions for guidance in how to feel and act. In the prison experiment mentioned above, subjects who were put in the position of prison guards started to feel powerful and act in a coercive manner toward other subjects who were prisoners. In the Milgram experiments, subjects reacted in an obedient way to the experimenter, even when they themselves felt uncomfortable and questioned their own behavior. In social settings, including organizations in which we work, we often rely on information from the context and others (e.g., leaders) to guide our own behavior. This influence comes in two forms: (1) informational social influence or descriptive norms (e.g., no one wears a tie around here), or (2) normative social influence or injunctive norms where there is approval or disapproval for behaving or not behaving in a particular way (e.g., we make fun of people who wear a tie). Although this agreement may seem unimportant, the overwhelming evidence is that descriptive and injunctive norms are powerful determinants of how people in organizations behave. Agreement among an organization’s members about how to think and behave helps people fit in and coordinate actions. A failure to comply can result in an individual being isolated or excluded from the group.

> **strong culture**
> 
> Tightly shared norms, beliefs, and values among organizational members.

When members of an organization agree that certain behaviors are important (there is agreement about the content of the norm), and when people feel strongly and are willing to sanction others for noncompliance (there is intensity about the norm), then a strong culture exists. That is, a strong culture is one in which there are tightly shared norms, beliefs, and values regarding appropriate behavior among organizational members. Under these circumstances, new members have two choices: (1) they can either comply and fit in, or (2) not comply and are likely to be excluded socially and psychologically. What many people underestimate is how powerful the effects of culture are on behavior. Often, we tend to think that behavior is a function of the individual’s values, needs, and personality, rather than the expectation of others. While personality may be one important determinant of behavior in unconstrained situations, it is much less important in groups and organizations where there are very strong norms and values (e.g., religious organizations, the military). In these situations, behavior is largely driven by the expectations of others. Perhaps most importantly, leaders can have a large effect on norms and values—and their enforcement. Thus, it behooves leaders to understand the norms and values that develop in their organizations and can become ingrained in culture. A strong culture can be both positive and valuable. But as also shown in the experiments described above, a strong culture can potentially be negative and destructive.
