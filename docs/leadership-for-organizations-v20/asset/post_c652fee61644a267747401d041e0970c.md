## 12.2 What Is Organizational Culture All About?

organizational culture
The expectations that organizational members share about how to think and act.

Culture can be considered at different levels. For example, societies or countries have identifiable cultures.[8] But our focus here is on understanding and recognizing culture at an organizational level. Broadly speaking, **organizational culture** refers to the expectations that organizational members share about how to think and act. These expectations are determined based on shared assumptions, values, and beliefs that define what is important, as well as norms that define the appropriate attitudes and behaviors of organizational members. As such, these social expectations act as important guides for how members should behave when no one is watching or there is no one to ask for guidance. In sum, organizational culture represents shared values (that define what is important) and norms or shared social expectations that define appropriate attitudes and behaviors for organizational members.

One helpful way to think about organizational culture has been suggested by Ed Schein, perhaps the most important scholar of organizational culture.[9] In line with an iceberg metaphor (see Figure 12.1), he suggested that we think about culture as occurring at three different levels: (1) basic underlying assumptions and beliefs about the organization that may be unconscious or taken for granted (e.g., people are self-interested and need to be carefully watched, versus people are inherently good and can be trusted), (2) norms and values that can be espoused (e.g., employees are our most important asset and can be trusted, versus we really do not trust employees), and (3) cultural artifacts like company symbols, dress, and language that are visible but sometimes hard to decipher. In his seminal book, Schein began by claiming that “the only thing of real importance that leaders do is to create and manage culture (p. 2).” He finishes some 300 pages later by concluding that “The unique and essential feature of leadership is the manipulation of culture (p. 317).”

Norms are clearly a key aspect of organizational culture. Depending on the company, these norms may include important things like how we:

- deal with each other
- deal with customers
- handle disagreements
- show concern for getting things right (e.g., pay attention to detail)
- try to do new things or innovate
- treat other people

They may also tell us other, more tangible things such as:

- how to dress
- how formal or informal we are with each other (e.g., use of titles vs. first names)
- what the physical layout of the organization looks like (e.g., private offices vs. cubicles)
- what types of terminology we use (e.g., very formal vs. more colloquial or pop culture terms)

For instance, in some organizations, people are expected to be polite and never challenge the boss. If someone does so, we may observe others warning that person, “This isn’t how we do things around here.” If the individual continues to speak out and disagree, that person may even be sanctioned or asked to leave. In other organizations, like Intel (a large semiconductor firm) or McKinsey (a major consulting firm), exactly the opposite behavior is expected. Employees at Intel are taught “constructive confrontation,” whereby they are expected to challenge others whenever they disagree. At McKinsey, all employees are taught through cultural norms that they have “an obligation to dissent” if they disagree with others, even if the other person is a senior executive.

At Zappos and Nordstrom, high levels of customer service are expected, and employees are encouraged through norms and values to try to do whatever they can to help customers, even if it means referring them to a competitor. At Nordstrom, for example, the employee handbook (i.e., an artifact that can be observed above the surface of the metaphoric iceberg) consists of a single page with just one rule, “Use your good judgment in all situations. There are no other rules.” What this means is that employees are expected through norms and values to do whatever they can within reason to satisfy a customer, even if it means accepting returned merchandise with no questions. Other companies may have different norms and values, whereby the customer is less important. For example, think about the last time that you called a customer service hotline and waited for 20 minutes while a recorded voice reminded you that “your call is very important to us.”

But where should a leader interested in building and shaping culture begin? Although there is a great deal of interesting research on all three of these levels that are depicted in Figure 12.1, and each can be important, from a leadership perspective, it is most useful for us to focus on norms and values because these can be shaped and managed directly.[10] From this perspective, cultural norms and values can be reinforced by leaders and the systems that those leaders put into place (e.g., what is measured and rewarded in performance appraisal systems).

## How Norms Affect Behavior

In the 1970s, Phil Zimbardo, a Stanford Professor, conducted an experiment in which students acting as guards in a mock prison began to abuse the other students who were prisoners.[11]

![](img/5a5fcd49e67d713bc6c99c1cc791987a_191_0.png)

In the well-known Milgram experiments in the 1960s, subjects were asked to give an increasing level of electric shocks to another person when that person failed to answer correctly.[12] Results typically show that when pressured by an authority figure (i.e., experimenter), more than 60 percent of participants are prepared to deliver what they think could be a fatal electric shock. Why would people behave this way?

The answer is to be found in the power of norms, or social expectations, and how in many situations, we look to either the context or people in leadership positions for guidance in how to feel and act. In the prison experiment mentioned above, subjects who were put in the position of prison guards started to feel powerful and act in a coercive manner toward other subjects who were prisoners. In the Milgram experiments, subjects reacted in an obedient way to the experimenter, even when they themselves felt uncomfortable and questioned their own behavior. In social settings, including organizations in which we work, we often rely on information from the context and others (e.g., leaders) to guide our own behavior. This influence comes in two forms: (1) informational social influence or descriptive norms (e.g., no one wears a tie around here), or (2) normative social influence or injunctive norms where there is approval or disapproval for behaving or not behaving in a particular way (e.g., we make fun of people who wear a tie). Although this agreement may seem unimportant, the overwhelming evidence is that descriptive and injunctive norms are powerful determinants of how people in organizations behave. Agreement among an organization’s members about how to think and behave helps people fit in and coordinate actions. A failure to comply can result in an individual being isolated or excluded from the group.

> **strong culture**
>
> Tightly shared norms, beliefs, and values among organizational members.

When members of an organization agree that certain behaviors are important (there is agreement about the content of the norm), and when people feel strongly and are willing to sanction others for noncompliance (there is intensity about the norm), then a strong culture exists. That is, a strong culture is one in which there are tightly shared norms, beliefs, and values regarding appropriate behavior among organizational members. Under these circumstances, new members have two choices: (1) they can either comply and fit in, or (2) not comply and are likely to be excluded socially and psychologically. What many people underestimate is how powerful the effects of culture are on behavior. Often, we tend to think that behavior is a function of the individual’s values, needs, and personality, rather than the expectation of others. While personality may be one important determinant of behavior in unconstrained situations, it is much less important in groups and organizations where there are very strong norms and values (e.g., religious organizations, the military). In these situations, behavior is largely driven by the expectations of others. Perhaps most importantly, leaders can have a large effect on norms and values—and their enforcement. Thus, it behooves leaders to understand the norms and values that develop in their organizations and can become ingrained in culture. A strong culture can be both positive and valuable. But as also shown in the experiments described above, a strong culture can potentially be negative and destructive.
