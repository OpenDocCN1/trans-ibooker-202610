# # A Three-Stage Approach for Generating Organizational Change

Leaders realize that change and its effective implementation do not happen overnight. Instead, change represents a process that unfolds over time. Below, we will consider two ways of thinking about a change process. The first one has been around a while, but is conceptually simple and still represents a useful way for leaders to consider how to drive change in organizations. It essentially involves three steps: (1) unfreeze, (2) change, and (3) refreeze.[6]

**Unfreeze.** The first step is for leaders to provide a compelling reason for why change is necessary and important. For reactive change, this is typically less difficult. Employees may already be aware of declining profits, increased pressure on reducing costs, and the success of competitors. For proactive change, the challenge is more difficult. Can the leader create the perception of a looming crisis, or demonstrate a huge opportunity? This might involve providing evidence that demonstrates that the firm is under-performing using customer feedback or benchmarking data to convince people that storm clouds are on the horizon, or to paint a picture of a huge future opportunity. In short, the leadership challenge is to provide a credible reason, either a threat or opportunity, as to why employee’s need to embrace change. As the saying goes, “people change because they see the light or they feel the fire.”[7]

**Change.** Once there is a credible reason for change, then the leader needs to provide a reasonable plan for dealing with the change. This should include a transition manager (the person responsible for implementing the change), the transition team (who is on the team), the resources necessary (money, people), feedback metrics (how people will know when they are succeeding), and a clear time frame for implementation. In developing this plan, it is important for the leader to build support with key power groups within the organization and to anticipate and, if possible, to preempt resistance. This might include an analysis of who is likely to be affected positively or negatively by the change effort, and whose opposition might undermine or derail the change. It should be noted that change efforts should not be all top-down. These efforts could also provide for the involvement of employees to build commitment (e.g., task forces, town hall meetings, surveys).

**Refreeze.** Once the change effort has been accomplished, it is important to ensure that there is no backsliding. This means making sure that the new behaviors and processes are firmly embedded into the organization. This effort could include the development and monitoring of new milestones and metrics, the realignment of incentives to reward the new behaviors and to sanction the old ones, and the continued communication of the new vision.
