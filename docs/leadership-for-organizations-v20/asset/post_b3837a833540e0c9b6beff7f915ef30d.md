
## CHAPTER 12 Strategic Leadership and Shaping Organizational Culture

> "If you're really going to transform an enterprise, you've got to understand culture... It took me to age fifty-five to figure that out. Culture is everything."—Lou Gerstner, CEO of IBM 1993–2002

### Learning Objectives

- 1. Explain the key elements of competitive advantage and how the shaping of organizational culture is relevant to achieving such advantage.
- 2. Describe the issues involved in aligning culture with vision, mission, and strategy.
- 3. Determine how culture strength can be either a good or bad thing.
- 4. Explain and manage the steps that a leader takes to create and reinforce culture.

In prior chapters of this book, we have largely considered leadership qualities and behaviors that are relevant at various levels of management in organizations. In Chapter 10, we began to turn our attention to more strategic or upper levels of management in our consideration of core purpose and vision. In this chapter and the next, we continue our focus on strategic leadership. The overarching goal of strategic leadership is to create **competitive advantage** for a firm. What exactly is competitive advantage? One way of answering this question is to use a resource-based perspective in order to understand a firm's valuable resources and how those resources might be brought to bear in terms of creating a competitive advantage. Such advantage can be created if resources are:

- valuable by enabling a firm to improve its efficiency and effectiveness;
- rare and not easily imitated by other firms; and
- non-substitutable or not easily replaced by some other non-rare resource.[1]

**competitive advantage**

The advantage that a firm creates for itself based on valuable, rare and not easily imitated, and non-substitutable resources.

Organizational culture is one means through which competitive advantage of a firm can be achieved. Culture can be valuable; for example, in terms of potentially motivating employees, helping to serve customers, and stimulating innovation. However, being valuable alone does not ensure competitive advantage. If something that is valuable can be easily copied or imitated by a competitive firm, then it will not serve as a competitive advantage. That said, culture is not easily copied or imitated because it involves elements like values, beliefs, and norms that take a long time to develop and become engrained and shared among organizational members. Further, culture and the potential value that it creates is non-substitutable. In other words, it cannot be easily replaced by a non-rare resource (e.g., training programs). Stated another way, the valuable effects that culture can create are largely unique to culture itself. Later in this chapter when we consider mechanisms for leading culture, we will see how training programs can help solidify or engrain culture. But training per se, should not be viewed as a substitute for culture as a competitive advantage.

As portrayed in this chapter, it is the job of the strategic leader to identify, protect, and nurture resources that meet the above criteria of competitive advantage. The leader does so through three key steps:

- 1. establishment or redirection of a mission (or core purpose) and a shared vision
- 2. formulating strategy
- 3. implementing mission, vision, and strategy

Step 2, formulating strategy, is the primary issue addressed in books and courses on strategic management. Steps 1 and 3 pertain to strategic leadership, which is why we focus on these two steps in this book. To some degree, we addressed step 1 in Chapter 11, and we will continue to address “redirection” in Chapter 13 as we consider the role of strategic leadership in organizational change. In the current chapter, we consider how strategic leaders use step 3 to shape organizational culture for the purpose of establishing, implementing, and sometimes redirecting mission, vision, and strategy—ultimately to help achieve competitive advantage. Before attempting to describe further the connection between leadership, culture, and competitive advantage, we first focus on how an organization’s context or environment creates the ongoing need for active, strategic leadership, including the building and shaping of organizational culture.

### 12.1 Environmental Context, Competitive Advantage, and Organizational Culture

It is important to put strategic leadership in context; specifically, the environmental context of firms. In many ways, strategic leadership is about continuously monitoring or scanning an organization’s environmental context for the purpose of determining new trends, threats, and opportunities—all of which can potentially affect (positively or negatively) competitive advantage. That context can be largely characterized along three dimensions:

- 1. economic (i.e., globalization trends, increased competition);
- 2. regulatory (i.e., changing local, national, and global laws/regulations that can affect how a firm does business); and
- 3. technological (i.e., how changing technology can affect work processes, products, and services).

Aspects of the environment are more or less relevant to a particular firm depending on the nature of its business and customers. For example, regulatory issues are especially relevant to healthcare and energy sectors. Technological issues are especially relevant to firms in electronics industries. Regardless of the industry in which a firm competes, the job of the strategic leader is to continuously keep an eye on trends and changes that might challenge the status quo, or how the organization is currently maneuvering in its environment to maintain competitive advantage. In this chapter, our focus is on how a strategic leader builds or shapes organizational culture to help ensure that his or her firm remains competitive in its environment. If there is a culture gap, then an organization’s values, norms, and behavior will not be aligned with the needs or demands of its environment, which can engender competitive disadvantage. In other words, there will be a difference between desired culture and actual behavior. It is the job of strategic leaders to identify when gaps of this nature are occurring, and then take steps to reduce the gap.

Let’s take an example. In the early 1990s, the IBM Company was failing. Its stock price had plummeted, and many Wall Street analysts were calling for the firm to be broken up and sold off in pieces. By 1992, more than 60,000 jobs had been lost and, in spite of the previous CEO’s efforts at transformation, the company seemed doomed. In 1993, Lou Gerstner became the first outsider in its history to be appointed as CEO. Over the next decade, under his leadership, the company was transformed from a failing maker of computers to a successful software and services business. In describing this transformation, Gerstner claimed that the main reason the prior management could not transform IBM was not due to attempts to execute new strategy. Instead, it was a case that the values and culture had been created for an earlier point in time and other strategies. He argued that “Until we got to the issues of culture and values and behavior, we never would have changed IBM.”[4]

The IBM story is just one example of many whereby organizational culture and leadership are identified as either a source of competitive advantage (e.g., Southwest Airlines, Google, Nordstrom, Zappos), or alternatively, as a barrier to change and a source of competitive disadvantage (e.g., IBM, Wells Fargo, Kodak, Enron). A recent Deloitte study revealed that 86 percent of U.S. executives believe that culture is a potential competitive advantage and important to business success.[5] Another recent survey of executives found that 78 percent of Fortune 1000 CEOs and CFOs viewed culture as one of the top three things affecting a firm’s value.[6] A review of the subject by researchers revealed more than 4,600 studies on the topic.[7] Yet, in spite of all this attention, there is still a lack of clarity about what culture is and how it can be managed. For instance, in the Deloitte study, 68 percent of the respondents indicated that they did not understand culture, and only 19 percent believed that their organizations have the right culture.

Culture is clearly seen as important by company leaders, but what is it really? And why is it so important for creating competitive advantage? And, maybe most importantly, how do leaders create, manage, and change cultures? If we are not clear about what culture is, then it is difficult to see how it might matter for the purpose of competitive advantage and, more importantly, how leaders can create and change it. We address these issues in this chapter. In so doing, you will see how culture, when properly aligned with strategy, can be a clear source of competitive advantage. Alternatively, when the culture is misaligned, it can hinder the implementation of strategy and become a competitive disadvantage.
