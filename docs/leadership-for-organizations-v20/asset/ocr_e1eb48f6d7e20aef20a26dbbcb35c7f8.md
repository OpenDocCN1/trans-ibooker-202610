# 4.3 Managing Sideways

managing sideways
When peers or coworkers
are able to lead and
influence each other.

Sometimes it is important for peers or coworkers to be able to lead and influence each other—or **managing sideways**. Students who work in teams know this to be all so true, and they also know that it can be frustrating to try to lead those over whom they have no direct authority. While there are no easy answers, it is important to remember that you and your peers generally have common superordinate goals, such as satisfying customers, clients, or instructors. Further, some of the principles of managing up apply to managing sideways. For example, it is important to assume good motives on the part of the peer, even if it might seem that the person is irresponsible or detached.
It is also essential to try to understand peers' individual needs, styles of working, and problems. Sometimes tasks among peers can be divided to better match each other's needs and desires. A peer who is attempting to effectively manage sideways will try to better understand or empathize with
his or her peers.

Of course, when managing sideways, it is always possible to get one's formal leader involved.
But such a course of action should be viewed as a last resort. In all likelihood, the boss would prefer
that peers lead and influence each other in a cooperative manner, rather than always running to
him or her when problems develop.
