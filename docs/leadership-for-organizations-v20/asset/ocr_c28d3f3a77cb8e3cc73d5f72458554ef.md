# 8.3 Shared Leadership

shared leadership
Mutual influence and shared responsibility among team members, whereby they lead each other toward the team's goals.

Ultimately, the goal of a team leader should be to develop team members to the point of being able to share in the leadership role. Coach Phil Jackson captures this saying, “Leadership is not about forcing your will on others. It’s about mastering the art of letting go.”[15] Shared leadership occurs when there is mutual influence and shared responsibility among team members, whereby they lead each other toward the team’s goals.[16] But why is shared leadership so important in teams and organizations? The answer to this question is actually quite simple. The various functions of teams in terms of getting tasks done, dealing with the personal or emotional needs of team members, and effecting change when necessary oftentimes cannot be readily handled by a single leader, especially in larger teams. Instead, it helps to think of the overall leadership capacity of a team in terms of its multiple members.[17]

Let’s take an example. Imagine a situation in which you are involved in a project team, and the formal leader of the team has delegated control of some important decision-making and implementation of decisions to team members. You have a peer on the team who for whatever reason seems unmotivated. However, this individual, and her cooperation is imperative if the project is to be successful. In this instance, it is essential that you or other team members attempt to show leadership influence for the purpose of motivating the individual, rather than waiting for the formal leader to act.

This particular example is reflective of a situation that many team members find themselves in these days as organizations increasingly structure in terms of semi-autonomous teams. In such instances, teams are largely autonomous in terms of control from formal leaders. Instead, team members organize and lead themselves.[18] While semi-autonomous teams can increase member motivation and psychological well-being, they also necessitate that members share in the leadership function.

Throughout this book, we have stressed that an important part of the leadership function for formal leaders is to develop followers not only in terms of their technical abilities, but also with regard to their ability to make decisions, solve problems on their own, and assume increasing responsibility. For example, in Chapter 5, we stressed how the developmental model of decision-making put forth by Vroom and Jago can be used in an attempt to develop followers’ problem-solving and decision-making skills.[19] The overall goal for the formal leader should be to enable successful delegation of decision-making to followers. Reed Hastings, the founder and CEO of Netflix has said, “I take pride in making as few decisions as possible.”[20] In his view, great leaders get great performance by orchestrating the appropriate context (i.e., selecting the right people, leading-by-example, and reinforcing the right behavior), rather than trying to control all decision-making. But beyond decision-making, the goal should also be to delegate other aspects of the leadership role, including the motivation of fellow team members. We are not suggesting that formal leaders practice laissez-faire, which implies being absent when they are needed or not getting involved when important issues arise for which the team could use assistance.[21] But nevertheless, if followers can be developed to assume more of the leadership role, teams and organizations will be in a better position to achieve effectiveness.
