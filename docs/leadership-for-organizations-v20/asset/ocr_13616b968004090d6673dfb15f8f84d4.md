Yes, it is true that many leaders overtly or more subtly encourage such behavior on the part of followers. However, behavior of this sort is not good for leaders, followers, or their organizations in the long run.

Before we actually describe what effective followership is all about, we first provide a rationale for why it is so important for organizations. One way to understand the importance of effective followership is to consider what can happen in the leader-member exchange (LMX) processes that were considered in Chapter 3. When a follower is a “yes” man or woman, the proverbial “brown-noser,” or just simply withdrawn and passive (e.g., barely meeting minimal work expectations), the LMX relationships could get strained, eventually leading to the follower being relegated to the leader’s out-group. Perhaps in some instances, leaders will like a follower who always agrees with, or ingratiates, him or herself to the leader. However, especially for effective leaders, such follower behavior tends to run thin over time, and a leader will expect the type of effective follower behavior that we consider below.

At the organizational level, when followers are generally just agreeing with leaders and “sucking up,” we can expect to see a lack of initiative, creative problem-solving, and an upward flow of important information that leaders need to make better decisions. The matter gets even worse if some followers retreat into silence and lack of engagement, and only do their jobs according to established expectations (e.g., in line with their formal job descriptions).

# Effective Versus Less Effective Follower Types

As shown in Table 4.1, follower effectiveness can vary along three dimensions: (1) thinking style, (2) level of engagement, and (3) loyalty to the leader.<sup>[2]</sup> First, followers who are independent, critical thinkers are inquisitive, do not take things at face value (e.g., question supposed “truths” or established ways of doing things), and attempt to show creativity and innovative approaches to work and problems. On the other hand, followers who are dependent, uncritical thinkers accept the status quo and established ways of thinking and acting. They pursue performance expectations, but do not attempt to be very creative or innovative.

Second, followers can be either highly engaged, or disengaged. To be engaged, means to be fully committed to one’s job and to the responsibilities that it entails. Such individuals are self-starters, rather than waiting to be told what to do. Although they are adept at following instructions and established procedures, they are also likely to go beyond the basic requirements of their jobs and engage in what is known as organizational citizenship behavior.<sup>[3]</sup> This behavior includes doing extra or discretionary things to improve the organization and its image, and helping coworkers or new people who may be struggling. Conversely, a less engaged follower is not so committed, does demonstrate organizational citizenship behavior, and may even appear to be lazy or irresponsible. In addition, an engaged follower takes responsibility for their own development, rather than simply waiting for the leader or organization to suggest training and development opportunities.
