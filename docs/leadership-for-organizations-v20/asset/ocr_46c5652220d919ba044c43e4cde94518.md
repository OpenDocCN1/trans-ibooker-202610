3. Create a vision for change—Leader vision is an important aspect of effective leadership, and its role has been considered multiple times in this book. Perhaps nowhere is it as important as in the leading of change. Indeed, a key aspect of the leading of change is to provide an emotionally compelling reason for the change. Vision messages need not be complicated, and indeed, the purpose of the change effort should be expressed in a simple, emotionally compelling message. This vision and the associated strategy can help people understand what you are trying to achieve through the change, and how the effort can benefit them. It should help others “see” what the future organization will look like. The main goal is to provide a simple vision that inspires an emotional reaction. For example, when the Japanese construction equipment firm Komatsu was threatened by the American firm Caterpillar, their leaders adopted the simple vision of “Destroy Caterpillar” as a rallying cry.

4. Communicate and role-model the vision widely—Once formulated, the vision and reason for the change needs to be communicated frequently and powerfully at all levels of the organization. The communication should not simply be a slogan or message, but instead, illustrated with stories and examples. If employees are to be convinced that a change effort is important, they need to see and hear the message again and again, and through different means. Multiple forms of media should be used including video-casts, town hall meetings, and simple leading-by-walking around. Moreover, key leaders (e.g., CEOs) need to communicate directly to organizational members. But especially in larger organizations, they can also use surrogates (i.e., trusted followers or coalition members) to go out and spread the word regarding the need for change.[9] While a vision is important, if people do not see their leaders behaving consistently with the vision, it becomes little more than hot air. Worse, if leaders are not seen to be walking the talk, the vision will generate cynicism and resistance on the part of the employees. The implication is clear: senior leaders need to be continually modeling the changes that they want to see.

5. Remove obstacles—As the change unfolds, careful attention should be paid to identifying obstacles to the change so that people in the organization are empowered to act. These obstacles might include anything from the way the organization is structured, processes in place that currently reward or even train inappropriate behavior, and dealing with the people who oppose change. If a structure, HR process, or metric is lacking or seen to be hindering the change, leaders should remove the obstacle, and replace it with processes that are more favorable to the change. For example, training procedures may need to be changed. Further, a term that is anathema to change is bureaucracy. Bureaucratic procedures are designed to reinforce the status quo—not to help smooth the way for change. Many people in organizations have done quite nicely in the past in terms of job security and their careers by enforcing the current bureaucracy. Accordingly, there is a disincentive for such individuals to change. It follows that leaders of change need to be continually on the lookout for impeding bureaucracy and bureaucrats who might be resisting change. Indeed, direct action needs to be taken to deal with bureaucratic structures or rules that impede change, as well as the bureaucrats who might resist change. Similarly, large-scale organizational change may require new reporting relationships, which can also signal the importance of change.

6. Create short-term wins—In any change effort, many people will be initially skeptical and looking for indications that the effort is serious and can be successful, even if in little ways. A powerful way to signal this is to look for small initial successes, or quick wins, that can be held up as examples of the change effort. This might require finding easy wins that can be achieved without help from critics or that require little investment. For example, individual employees who are living or personifying the change should be acknowledged through stories, celebrations, or awards.
