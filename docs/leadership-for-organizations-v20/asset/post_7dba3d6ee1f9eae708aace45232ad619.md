## Some Hard Bumps Along the Path of Change

Despite the accomplishments and outcomes summarized thus far, there have been some definite bumps along the way for ASU and Michael Crow over the past 20 years. That is, the path toward change has not always been smooth, and some might argue that the bumpiness could have been predicted and even prevented. Some of the challenges have been due to economic problems in AZ and the country as a whole. But others are more personal to Michael Crow, his style of leadership, and approach toward change.

## Institutional and Economic Challenges to Change

Making broad-sweeping change in an academic institution is not an easy thing to do. There are a number of institutional challenges. First, bureaucracy is one such challenge because of how it represents an obstacle to change, de-empowering people to act in accordance with the goals of change. Crow himself has referred to it as "the virus of the agency".[22] Bureaucracy involves rigid adherence to rules and procedures, despite conditions that might dictate that such rules or procedures be softened or even altogether ignored. In a speech as early as the fall of 2002, Crow told ASU faculty and administrators that even after just three months as president, he had noticed what seemed to be an incredible amount of bureaucracy at ASU. Nevertheless, 20 years later, the bureaucracy at ASU seems to be going strong in terms of paperwork, rules, and rigid procedures for students, faculty, and staff alike. Indeed, in some ways, the COVID-19 pandemic only served to increase bureaucracy at ASU.

Second, there is the issue of faculty governance, which is a hallmark principle in academic institutions across the United States—as well as in many other countries. The basic idea is that that much of the strategic governance of the university should emanate from faculty members. Relevant aspects of governance include policies, curricula and programs, and evaluation procedures (e.g., evaluation of the faculty). Crow's top-down approach to change is not always congruent with faculty governance traditions and expectations. We know from research that a lack of voice on the part of employees tends to reduce organizational commitment.[23]

Third, additional long-standing norms or beliefs present challenges to the types of changes that Crow seeks. Despite recent claims to the contrary (see http://vimeo.com/110929715), other than some new colleges (e.g., Sustainability), there is little evidence of interdisciplinary teaching and research within the ASU system, and little evidence of increased entrepreneurship. As is the case at most traditional universities, ASU's organizational structure and systems (e.g., promotion and tenure systems for faculty members) largely continue to support a functional, "silo" approach that stresses disciplinary specialties—not interdisciplinary thinking and action, or entrepreneurship (e.g., on the part of faculty members).

As a further example of entrenched norms or beliefs, many faculty members and students have their doubts about the inclusiveness principle. Simply stated, they believe that it's a code word for lowering standards and allowing students into the university who are either unprepared or simply lack basic abilities. Many faculty members and students further believe, right or wrong, that the true motive is to receive more tuition money from students, as well as more allocation of state, federal, and private (i.e., donor) funds. The above-mentioned partnership with Starbucks could be viewed largely as an attempt to increase student tuition revenue through various sources, including both federal and private (e.g., Starbucks) funds. For Starbucks, support for a single, online program through ASU might lessen pressures on store managers to make scheduling accommodations for employees who attempt to attend traditional university classes while maintaining their jobs. As such, for both ASU and Starbucks, it is questionable as to whether the initiative truly represents an attempt to "do good," versus being more economic or strategic in terms of its motives.[24] Moreover, there is some evidence that the goal of inclusiveness, especially if it is based largely upon online education, makes it all the more difficult to achieve university retention goals, and thus, keep the university highly ranked.[25]

Finally, there have been some significant setbacks along the way. As shown in Figure 1, early on Crow noted the importance of having a medical school as part of ASU. Commonsense would suggest that a glaring deficiency in a metropolitan area the size of Phoenix is the absence of a major medical school that is associated with a university in the local area. From the beginning, Crow recognized this deficiency, as well as the potential of a medical school to add significant research funding possibilities for the university. However, he also noted the difficulties in starting such a school from scratch (e.g., the need for state and federal funding). Thus, he attempted a partnership with the University of Arizona, which saw benefits in expanding its own medical school efforts into the Phoenix area. But because of economic concerns in the state of Arizona and other reasons that have not been made public,[26] in the spring of 2010, the joint venture was dissolved. Subsequently, it appears that the University of Arizona will go it alone to expand its own medical school into the downtown Phoenix area.

More recently, and to his credit, Crow has developed a partnership with the Mayo Clinic, to pursue medical education in Scottsdale. Some ASU faculty are now associated with this effort, and research grant dollars (that are typically generated by medical schools) flow into ASU. Thus, ASU may reap some of the benefits of a medical school without having one formally under its control.

## Michael Crow as a Leader of Change

No one can question that Michael Crow has been a force for change, personally spearheading a number of changes at ASU during his tenure as President. But just as Crow has had his supporters, he has also had his critics and detractors—both internally and externally. Favorable versus problematic or challenging aspects of his leadership approach are summarized in Section 5 at the end of this case. These issues are considered below.

### Favorable Qualities

On the positive side, Michael Crow has clearly shown visionary qualities. Especially early on, he was able to light a fire for change by putting forth new, bold, value-laden ideas and goals that projected out in the future—and by framing such change as being absolutely necessary to meet budgetary challenges and increase the stature of the university. Indeed, especially in his early years at ASU, he had been known to talk about ASU thirty and forty years into the future, as well as how steps taken in the present pave the way for that future. He has also been a staunch defender of the university. In the midst of budget cuts in 2009 (i.e., the height of the recession), Crow went to the media, including television shows, to make a strong case against the state legislature and its proposed budget cuts. He went so far as to claim that such cuts to Arizona universities would condemn the state to "third-world status" in the future.
