# # 13.5 Appendix: Twenty Years of Leadership and Organizational Change—The Case of Michael Crow and Arizona State University

# ## Introduction

On July 1, 2002, Michael Crow succeeded Lattie Coor as President of Arizona State University (ASU). He arrived from Columbia University where he had served as Executive Vice-Provost from 1998–2002. Upon his arrival, President Crow reported in speeches to internal and external audiences that ASU was a good “regional” university. However, it had not received a high level of national or international prominence in terms of being considered a “world-class” university. Various metrics were cited in Crow’s speeches including university rankings, level of endowment, and level of sponsored research funding.

At the same time, Crow’s early vision pointed toward the necessity of having a world-class university in Phoenix if the city was to be considered world-class in its own right as it develops in the twenty-first century. From economic, sociologic, and political viewpoints, he made a strong case that it is not really feasible for a city to achieve world-class status without the development of a world-class university within its geographical proximity. To a large extent, this basic principle provided the foundation for his broader vision of ASU.[13]

# ## The Institutional Context of Universities

The change effort in which Crow and ASU have engaged over the past 20 years is taking place in a larger society, and more specifically, an institutional context involving universities. That context has existed largely unchanged for over 200 years. Traditional elements of this context that includes ASU and other universities are as follows:

+   - Students in traditional classroom environment at a designated location; in other words, not distributed either geographically or virtually (e.g., through online education)
+   - Basic (rather than applied or community-oriented) research emphasis
+   - Support for research provided solely by national agencies and foundations (e.g., National Institute of Health, National Science Foundation, and so forth)
+   - Specialized research efforts in disciplinary areas (e.g., chemistry, physics, engineering), as opposed to interdisciplinary efforts (e.g., engineering combined with management; sustainability, and so forth)
+   - Large percentage of public university budgets based on state legislature support

In many ways, the above elements form the genetic code for the traditional university. However, there are new trends and forces for change in the environments of universities that threaten to render aspects of this code as obsolete. Examples include:

+   - Demand on the part of students for the university to, in essence, go where they are (e.g., online education and locating the university at multiple sites, including international locations), rather than simply existing in the confines of a particular geographic location or physical classroom.
