The companies that were able to adapt and change had leaders who were able to find new business opportunities and ways of making necessary change happen, while the companies that failed had leaders who were unable to adapt in the face of the need for change.

We all know that we live in an era of unprecedented change. But what is interesting and important is that recent research has documented two big insights that are critical for managers to understand. First, the evidence is that the pace of change is accelerating.[1] The penetration of new technologies like the internet, mobility, and artificial intelligence is happening faster than at any point in history. The average automobile today has more computing power than the Apollo mission that first landed Neil Armstrong on the moon in 1969. Netscape, the first widely used web browser, was introduced in 1993. Twenty-five years later, more than three billion people were using the internet. The Flip Video camera first appeared in 2006 and was the hottest selling Christmas gift in 2007. Four years later, the company was essentially obsolete, replaced by the cell phone. These technological shifts have doomed great companies like Kodak (digital imaging), Blockbuster (video streaming), Borders (ebooks), the Chicago Tribune (online news), and Circuit City (online shopping).

But it is not only technological change that is disrupting business today. We are also seeing significant shifts in consumer preferences (communication, transportation), government regulation (banking, airlines), competition (off-shoring, low cost producers), and new business models (hospitality, transportation). For example, the rise of ride-sharing (Uber, Lyft, ZipCar) and autonomous vehicles combined with millennials' lower needs for owning a car are threatening automakers, and especially, taxi companies. The world-wide deregulation of the airline industry that has given rise to widespread availability of low-cost airlines has driven many airlines into bankruptcy and threatened other full-service carriers. Online banking and electronic payments are threatening banks and financial services companies. In the face of these changes, the evidence is also that companies are failing at an increasing rate. One estimate is that the average lifespan of a company in the Standard and Poor's 500 (large U.S. firms) is roughly twelve years.[2] In the face of change, large firms are failing at an increasing rate.

More recently we all witnessed the COVID-19 pandemic. Many "non-essential" businesses were forced to shut down for extended periods of time. Others that managed to stay open were only allowed to do so with dramatic changes, such as social distancing rules for employees and customers and limited capacities. Still others have permanently closed.

It is certainly true that in many instances, reasons unrelated to leadership practices can be blamed for business failures or closures. However, in other instances, the reasons for business failures go beyond technology, diseases, and so forth. But instead, they include the inability of company leaders to lead change. An analysis of corporate failures has shown that oftentimes it is not so much the case that firms have missed the shifts in technologies or markets, but instead, they had leaders who failed to adapt to the shifts fast enough. For example, for almost fifty years Smith Corona was the world's largest typewriter company. They failed when manual typewriters were replaced by word processors and personal computers. Ironically, Smith Corona had one of the first word processors. However, their leaders ignored this and focused on making money from the typewriter business, rather than aggressively promoting word processing. The leaders at Kodak knew that digital imaging threatened film sales, and although the company invented the first digital camera, they focused on film sales until it was too late. The leaders at Blockbuster waited until it was too late to enter video rentals by mail. The evidence is that these failures stemmed not from a lack of understanding of the new technologies, but that the leaders of these companies were unable, unwilling, or lacked the courage or insight to help their companies adapt to these changes. The overall goal of this chapter is to help you understand why senior leaders seem to find it so difficult to manage change and to provide you with several frameworks and suggestions for how you as a leader, now or in the future, might think about leading organizational change.

## 13.2 Four Basic Frames of Organizational Change

Before turning our attention to the leading of change, it is first useful to differentiate among several different types of change. A two-dimensional model of organizational change is shown in Figure 13.1. Whether it comes from shifts in technology, new business models, or changes in the market, organizational change can be categorized in one of two ways: reactive vs. proactive. In addition to this dimension, change can also be thought of as incremental vs. discontinuous.

### Reactive Change

A first type of change occurs when, in the face of some performance threat (e.g., declining revenues and profits, new competitors stealing customers, changing technologies that make existing products less attractive), firms change after the threat has affected the existing business. For example, as low-cost airlines like Southwest began to attract customers, the initial response by the major U.S. airlines (e.g., United, Delta, and American) was to do nothing. It was only after they saw significant financial losses and several bankruptcies that the major airlines began to respond aggressively by cutting costs, increasing efficiencies, and, in some cases, starting their own low-cost airlines. More recently we have seen brick and mortar stores like Walmart begin to react to the threat of online sales by aggressively trying to beef up their own online capabilities.

As an even more recent example, the COVID-19 pandemic led to wide scale operational changes with regard to how firms do business. Here are just some of the changes that we witnessed:

- 1. Mask-wearing on the part of employees and customers of retail outlets;
- 2. Widespread availability and use of disinfectants for both customers and employees;
- 3. Social distancing of employees and customers;
- 4. Increased use of employees working from home.

**reactive change**
Change that occurs after a threat has affected the existing business.

From a leader’s perspective, **reactive change** can often be difficult because it begins from a position of weakness. Firms may have fewer resources to apply, have less time to experiment, and if they are late to react, they may not have the depth of expertise that their competitors do. While being a first-mover does not always give a company a strategic advantage, being late to the game may make it more difficult for leaders to catch up. One small advantage that reactive change may offer, however, is the presence of a “burning platform.” That is, leaders can point to lower profits, loss of market share, loss of jobs, or the success of competitors as a way to motivate and focus employees on the need to change.

### Proactive Change

**proactive change**
Anticipating a coming change and actively embracing it before the competition does.

With **proactive change**, leaders anticipate a coming change and actively embrace it before the competition does. In other words, they see an opportunity gap that they wish to fill. Reed Hastings, the founder and CEO of Netflix, began his business by renting videos and DVDs by mail. But he anticipated that as broadband became faster and more widely available, customers would be able to stream video content, rather than rent videos, so he proactively invested in the technology to permit subscribers to stream content, even though he knew it would undermine his existing distribution business. Then, he anticipated that the generation of new content would become critical to future success, so he embarked on an aggressive strategy of developing his own content.

Amazon began as an online bookstore, but its founder, Jeff Bezos, has aggressively moved into other businesses, selling everything from diapers to industrial robots, as well as groceries (Amazon Fresh), electronic hardware (Kindle, Echo), computing services (Amazon Web Services, Twitch), producing movies and television content (Amazon Studios), and providing transportation services (Prime Air, Amazon Flex). Hearst, a $10 billion media company, began in 1880 as a newspaper company but has been transformed into a digital media company. In each of these transformations, the leaders of the firm saw a coming disruption and proactively invested in the new businesses, sometimes by leveraging existing assets and capabilities and sometimes by acquiring other companies.

Proactive change (i.e., making changes before a disruption occurs) is clearly a good thing to do. But ironically, proactive change is also harder than reactive change. This is because firms, when successful, develop inertia or resistance to change. O’Reilly and Tushman refer to this as the “success syndrome.” [3] They describe how successful firms develop skills, systems, processes, structures, metrics, and cultures that enable them to become more efficient over time. But, the same alignment of people, structure, and culture that produces success makes change more difficult. When successful, people both see less need for change and are more reluctant to change the formula that has made them successful, especially when that change might lead to negative personal outcomes (e.g., reductions to their pay and bonuses, at least in the short term). Complicating this is the fact that when successful, people in the company do not see any compelling reason to change. There is no “burning platform” to motivate the need to change. People often ask, “Why do we need to change? We’re already successful. Why should we devote resources to a new business that might be less profitable?” As a result, while successful firms may have the resources to invest in new initiatives, they are often resistant to making these changes. This places the burden on leaders to create a compelling vision for *why* it is important to change and to ensure that the forces for stability and inertia do not preclude proactive change efforts.
