# FIGURE 3.1 In-Groups Versus Out-Groups

| | In-Group Characteristics | | Out-Group Characteristics | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | Leader | Follower | Leader | Follower |
| | grants autonomy, responsibility, and interesting assignments | strong performance; assumes responsibility | assigns routine tasks and closely monitors | weak performance; does not assume responsibility |
| | provides support and encouragement | has values, background, etc. similar to the leader | is quick to point out mistakes made by the follower | has values or background that differs from the leader |
| | allows for mistakes | shows loyalty toward the leader | does not allow for follower input into decision-making | shows a lack of loyalty and may even undermine the leader |
| | allows for follower input into decision-making | volunteers input in a constructive manner, rather than undermining the leader | shows little trust toward the follower | demonstrates a lack of trustworthiness |
| | shows trust toward the follower | demonstrates trustworthiness | | |

The bottom line is that an in-group relationship between a leader and follower is based on trust. The leader trusts that the follower will assume increasing responsibility with little need for close supervision. The follower trusts that the leader will support him/her and look out for that person's best interests. While feelings of trust can potentially start to form early on in their relationship, in order to solidify, the leader and follower will continually seek cues from each other over time and observe each other's actions.

On the other hand, it is possible that an out-group relationship might form, and be based primarily on tangible exchanges. Out-groups can be characterized as the mirror opposite of in-groups. An out-group member is likely to be a poor performer or one that simply cannot or will not assume a higher level of responsibility. He or she often does just enough work to satisfactorily meet expectations. The out-group member may have a much different background (e.g., based on gender, age, or ethnicity) from the leader, and not share values or interests with the leader. One thing that can quickly and irrevocably put an individual into the out-group is if that individual publicly disrespects or undermines the leader. This can happen in many different ways. For example, a follower might show disrespect toward the leader in a team meeting or in their conversations with other leaders in the organization (e.g., the leader's boss). As another example, the follower may continually try to ignore or undermine attempts at organizational change or new directions that the leader is attempting to pursue. If these sorts of actions occur on the part of a follower, there will be a lack of trust on the part of the leader toward the follower.

One thing that should be made clear is that the in-group vs. out-group distinction regarding leader-member relationships is a pure, and perhaps sometimes artificial, distinction. As an alternative, three other possibilities come to mind. First, some followers may be in-between, i.e., neither in the in-group nor the out-group. These followers may be moderately valued and trusted by the leader, but not to the extent of in-group members. Second, it is possible that all followers are in the out-group. This obviously represents a toxic situation, and if it occurs, it is probably the result of poor leader behavior. Third, it is possible that all followers are in the in-group. As discussed below, this situation represents the ideal, and it is likely to require positive actions on the part of both the leader and all followers in order to be realized. Again, “it takes two to tango.”

# The LMX “Dilemma”

For example, since by definition, out-group members tend to be marginal performers, their presence is likely to negatively affect an organization's performance.
