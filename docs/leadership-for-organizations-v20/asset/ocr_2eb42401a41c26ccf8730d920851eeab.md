# The Bottom Line: Know Thy Own Culture

In sum, the overall purpose of this section on American culture is to stress that a necessary first step of being an effective global leader is to understand one’s own cultural influences. In so doing, one can get a better feel for some of the reasons as to why s/he thinks, feels, and behaves, and how individuals from other cultures may differ. Note that we have stressed both the broader cultural or societal context, as well as how culture applies to work setting issues. Thus, the global leader must inevitably deal with how culture affects life both personally, as well as in work settings. Indeed, as we will see, work and non-work lives often blend together when working in cultures outside of the United States. We now turn our attention to what a global leadership orientation is all about, and why it is important.

# 10.3 Global Leadership Orientation

In this book, we have considered, and in subsequent chapters will continue to talk about, different leadership styles. For example, we have considered participative and servant leaders. Accordingly, one possible approach to understanding effectiveness in global leader roles is to determine how these styles may, or may not, apply well across cultures. Indeed, there is much research along these lines. For example, participative leadership tends to fit better in cultures, such as the United States and Western European countries, with low power distance. However, participative leadership is not as accepted in Asian cultures. On the other hand, more paternal approaches to leadership (i.e., being a “fatherly” figure or taking care of followers and their families) is more applicable to Asian cultures. Furthermore, leadership qualities like visionary and inspirational tend to be equally important in most cultures around the world.[13]

Rather than considering how various styles of leadership may or may not apply to leaders in different cultural contexts, another way of considering effective global leaders is to focus on the type of orientation or mindset that such leadership requires. Is there such a thing as a global mindset, and if so, how exactly does it apply to global leaders? Some authors have indeed attempted to answer these questions.[14] One way to approach this issue is to look at what is required from leaders in terms of both know-how and emotional engagement.

Effectiveness in global leader roles requires the individual’s ability to make sense of unfamiliar, ill-defined, and changing situations. They then must take actions, sometimes quite novel or creative, to deal with interactions with a diverse set of stakeholders. These stakeholders may be diverse not only in terms of ethnicity or culture, but also in terms of their function or roles. They could include suppliers, customers, representatives and governmental and non-governmental organizations, and so forth. At times, the global leader is likely to encounter constraints that could thwart his or her goals. For example, Cuba represents a longstanding, Communist country that is beginning to open itself to capitalism and free markets. Organizations from Western countries are increasingly attempting to do business there. However, various rules are imposed on businesses, which represent a holdover from Communist thinking and culture. For example, we know of one instance of a business that was accustomed to motivating employees through incentives—a practice that is common to capitalistic thinking and Western ways of doing business. But in Cuba, in line with Communist/Socialist traditions, employees must be paid equally with no room for individual incentives, at least in terms of formal compensation practices.

If a leader has both the requisite know-how and emotional make-up, they stand a good chance of overcoming and dealing with such constraints.
