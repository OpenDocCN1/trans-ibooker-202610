# # CHAPTER 12 Strategic Leadership and Shaping Organizational Culture

# ## Learning Objectives

+   1. Explain the key elements of competitive advantage and how the shaping of organizational culture is relevant to achieving such advantage.
+   2. Describe the issues involved in aligning culture with vision, mission, and strategy.
+   3. Determine how culture strength can be either a good or bad thing.

In prior chapters of this book, we have largely considered leadership qualities and behaviors that are relevant at various levels of management in organizations. In Chapter 10, we began to turn our attention to more strategic or upper levels of management in our consideration of core purpose and vision. In this chapter and the next, we continue our focus on strategic leadership. The overarching goal of strategic leadership is to create **competitive advantage** for a firm. What exactly is competitive advantage? One way of answering this question is to use a resource-based perspective in order to understand a firm's valuable resources and how those resources might be brought to bear in terms of creating a competitive advantage. Such advantage can be created if resources are:

+   * valuable by enabling a firm to improve its efficiency and effectiveness;
+   * rare and not easily imitated by other firms; and
+   * non-substitutable or not easily replaced by some other non-rare resource.[1]

**competitive advantage**

The advantage that a firm creates for itself based on valuable, rare and not easily imitated, and non-substitutable resources.

Organizational culture is one means through which competitive advantage of a firm can be achieved. Culture can be valuable; for example, in terms of potentially motivating employees, helping to serve customers, and stimulating innovation. However, being valuable alone does not ensure competitive advantage. If something that is valuable can be easily copied or imitated by a competitive firm, then it will not serve as a competitive advantage. That said, culture is not easily copied or imitated because it involves elements like values, beliefs, and norms that take a long time to develop and become engrained and shared among organizational members. Further, culture and the potential value that it creates is non-substitutable. In other words, it cannot be easily replaced by a non-rare resource (e.g., training programs). Stated another way, the valuable effects that culture can create are largely unique to culture itself. Later in this chapter when we consider mechanisms for leading culture, we will see how training programs can help solidify or engrain culture. But training per se, should not be viewed as a substitute for culture as a competitive advantage.
