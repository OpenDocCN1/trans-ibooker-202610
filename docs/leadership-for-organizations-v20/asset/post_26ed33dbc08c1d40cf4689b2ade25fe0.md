
## Communication Media

Gradually over time, the options of communication media have grown in number and complexity. Over a hundred years ago, leaders had one simple option: face-to-face communication. Then with the invention of the telephone, leaders had an additional means of communicating with followers. In most recent times, the options have grown even further. Less than thirty years ago, the telephone was restricted to landline capabilities, but now cell phones are extremely common around the world, making phone communication much more available and convenient. Moreover, email, texting, video conferencing, and "chat" pose additional communication media possibilities. Accordingly, in any given instance in which a leader feels the need to communicate or give feedback to one or more followers, the logical question becomes: which medium should be chosen?

That is not an easy question to answer. The COVID-19 pandemic oftentimes restricted the face-to-face option and made more virtual modes of communication unavoidable in many instances. But even under more normal circumstances, a leader will mistakenly use an electronic, virtual medium, such as email or texting, simply because it is expedient. In other words, the leader may be experiencing a busy day, and feels the need to send a follower a quick message, so he or she simply sends an email or text message. However, the appropriate medium depends on the nature of the communication or the feedback. The key factor to consider is the "richness" of the communication medium, and whether that richness matches the nature of the feedback to be given. In essence, richness refers to the extent to which communication not only conveys information, but also properly takes into account emotions—on the part of both the sender and the receiver. As mentioned in Chapter 2, emotions are not ignored by effective leaders; instead, they are embraced.

Electronic or virtual media such as email, chat, or text messaging can effectively and efficiently send information, sometimes to thousands or even millions of people in a single message.[7] But those media may not adequately deal with emotions or potentially contentious matters. In other words, the emotions of the communication sender may not come through clearly, and perhaps more importantly, the emotions of the communication receiver may not be properly understood or handled by electronic media. In contrast, media such as phone or face-to-face contact are much better in terms of effectively dealing with emotions. The communicator can adequately get his or her emotions across, and the receiver can adequately show the emotions that result from the communication. Overall, if the leader is the sender of feedback, he or she is in a much better position to deal with emotions effectively through either phone or, even more so, face-to-face communication.

With all of that in mind, here are a number of recommendations to which leaders should adhere as givers of feedback when it comes to electronic and other communication media:

- Think carefully about the nature of the feedback that is given. If it is controversial, laden with potentially negative emotions (either in terms of the emotions being portrayed by the sender, or the emotions that are likely to be generated on the part of the receiver), or highly complex, then electronic forms of communication are not best. Instead, the leader should simply pick up the phone or communicate face-to-face.
- If using email: (a) use an appropriate subject line to grab the receiver's attention, and (b) keep the message relatively short and to the point.
- If either email or text messaging does not seem to be working well in terms of the results that are expected by the feedback sender, then just pick up the phone and call the feedback receiver. The call might serve to quickly clear up the confusion or quell negative emotions.
- Be careful not to send information in email messages that you might not want to be distributed widely. Even though you may have appropriately restricted an email message to just one or a few people, email has a way of finding itself in the hands of an unintended audience.
- Think very carefully before you hit the “send” button for either email or text messages. Sometimes email or texting is viewed as a quick and easy way to get something “off your chest.” That is not the proper use of such communications media.
- Consider the nature of feedback receiver. If the person is someone with whom you already have a somewhat long-term, amicable relationship, then there is less potential for misunderstandings or negative emotions when using electronic communication. However, if the communication receiver is not as familiar with the sender, or does not have a close working relationship, there is much more room for problems with electronic communication.[8]

An interesting aspect of these recommendations is that they can apply to more than feedback giving on the part of leaders to followers. They can also apply to co-workers, or even personal relationships. For example, one might consider these recommendations when communicating with a significant other, such as husband or wife.

## Judgments and Attributions

People have a natural tendency to judge. They judge or size up situations, and more importantly, they judge other people. Leaders are no exception. They evaluate the actions of followers and form judgments or attributions regarding the cause or motives behind those actions. An **attribution** is essentially a judgment regarding a person’s motives or personality as a perceived explanation for the cause of that person’s behavior or performance. On the one hand, it is possible for attributions to be external. In such instances, the cause (or blame) is not placed on the individual, but rather the individual’s circumstances (e.g., family issues, lack of cooperation from co-workers, unfair procedures to which the follower has been subjected, and so forth). Father Greg Boyle runs Homeboy Industries, an on-the-job-training program in Los Angeles to help former gang members. He constantly deals with people whose actions can easily invoke negative emotions. He describes how his approach is “to stand in awe—not judgment.”[9] Rather than judging them, he understands the difficult situations in which they live and appreciates how hard it has been for them.

On the other hand, we often make attributions that are quite internal (i.e., to the person’s motives, nature, or personality) and negative in nature. Indeed, the tendency that we have to overestimate the effect of internal factors, while underestimating the effect of external factors, has traditionally been termed the fundamental attribution error.[10] Some examples of making strong internal attributions, and actually communicating those attributions, include the following:

- “You show up late to class because you do not care about this class or your education.”
- “When you express your opinions so strongly it shows that you don’t really value the opinions of others and that you are arrogant.”
- “When you do not show concern for my frustrations, it just goes to show how you don’t care about the feelings of others.”
- “When they are in negotiations, people from Asian cultures (such as Japan) avoid eye contact because they are trying to be deceptive as a way of manipulating the situation.”

As a feedback giver, negative attributions can seem cathartic for leaders. That is, a communication of an attribution can help in terms of quickly “getting something off your chest.” In addition, a leader might feel that he or she is getting to the point and stating feedback in terms that can be quickly understood and acted upon by followers. However, attributional communication on the part of leaders can be very problematic. Specifically, people do not like to be judged, and the communication of attributions is likely to make the feedback receiver very defensive, rather than successfully leading to behavior change. If one becomes defensive, then the goal of the feedback (i.e., performance improvement) has little chance of success. Kevin Johnson, the NBA all-star and former mayor of Sacramento, California, offered a suggestion to avoid such negative attributions when he said, “Stick to your principles, but abandon your assumptions.”[11] Marine Corps General James Mattis said the same thing slightly differently, “Don’t apologize for your values, but don’t assume that they are right.”[12]
