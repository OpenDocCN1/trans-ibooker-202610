For example, employees are likely to expect to hear about potential restructuring in their work environment (*e.g.*, reassignments or job cuts) early on. Second, people who are considered to be genuine or authentic. In short, authentic leaders do not put on appearances or try to act in a manner that does not reflect their true dispositions. In a word, they are not fake; instead, they are themselves. This may seem obvious, but it is surprising how, especially when people assume leadership roles, they often attempt to change who they are. Examples include one’s accent, mannerisms, and so forth. Followers tend to be more confident in, or inspired by, leaders who maintain their authenticity.

Third, leaders have a strong sense of internal accountability. The Samurais in historic Japan would literally fall on their swords if they disgraced themselves and their emperors by losing in battle. This obviously constitutes an extreme act of accountability. But in general, to accept accountability means that one feels a strong sense of responsibility toward others or toward a cause, and if performance standards are not met, the individual accepts negative personal repercussions, and will ultimately attempt to rectify the substandard performance. Unfortunately, personal accountability is largely not the norm in modern organizations, and instead, people find ways of blaming others for performance failures. For example, during the 2008 financial crisis, people in positions of leadership at financial firms such as Lehman Brothers typically did not assume accountability for the misery that they caused, at least in part, toward customers. Instead, those individuals blamed the greater economy, government policies, and even the customers themselves (*e.g.*, customers who supposedly frivolously took on too much debt). In short, leaders do not blame others for failures; instead, they take personal accountability.

## Complexity and Paradoxical Nature

Really good leaders are complex, and sometimes even appear to be contradictory individuals. Indeed, they may be paradoxical in their qualities and in their approach to leading. As mentioned in Chapter 1, a **paradox** involves seemingly contradictory elements that appear to be logical when considered in isolation. But those elements will at first glance also seem irrational, inconsistent, and even absurd when juxtaposed. In other words, at least on the surface, they seem irreconcilable. That said, the idea of paradoxical leadership is to show how seemingly contradictory elements can exist both simultaneously and harmoniously in order to contribute to leader effectiveness.[17] Below are some examples.

> **paradox**
> 
> Seemingly contradictory elements that exist simultaneously and appear to be logical when considered in isolation. But those elements will at first glance also seem irrational, inconsistent, and even absurd when juxtaposed.

Narcissistic, but yet humble. Narcissistic individuals are very self-absorbed, and they demand attention and adulation from other people. At first glance, it might seem like narcissism is always a bad leadership quality. Indeed, narcissism can have bad effects in that narcissists can be abusive to others, including their potential followers.[18] In other words, it might seem clear that narcissism is indicative of what can be termed the “dark side” of leadership. But at the same time, we know that narcissists are motivated to make decisions and lead others (*e.g.*, provide vision), and they see themselves as natural leaders. This fact in and of itself is important, since some people simply do not want to make decisions or lead, and yet decision-makers and people who take the lead are necessary in organizations. In addition, as compared to non-narcissists, narcissistic leaders tend to be rather bold in their visions, and bold or new vision is something that is often necessary for organizations.[19]

Something that may seem surprising is that recent research has shown that it is quite possible for a narcissistic leader to also be humble, or at least be perceived by others as possessing humility.[20] In such an instance, the leader would be self-absorbed and believe in his/her greatness. But at the same time, this leader would acknowledge that other people are great as well, and he or she would listen to others and absorb their feedback. In other words, such a narcissist is able to see or at least communicate that he or she is not an expert or master of everything, but instead other people have their own special qualities that the narcissist does not possess. Michael Maccoby referred to such individuals as “productive narcissists”. [21] Research has shown that this seemingly paradoxical mix of narcissism and humility ends up resulting in favorable performance outcomes for leaders and followers alike. [22]

Gaining control by giving up control. Managing is all about control—how to gain it, use it, and maintain it. Leading is different. In a paradoxical sense, effective leaders realize that the only true way to gain control (or at least a sense of control) is to give it up. A good example of such a leader is Ricardo Semler, a successful businessman from Brazil. Early in his career, Semler led in an authoritarian manner. But as time went on, Semler found that leading was more satisfying, and his organization actually ran better, when he gave up control to employees on a wide range of matters (e.g., salaries, work hours, operational decision-making, and so forth). In other words, in a paradoxical sense, by giving up control, he actually gained true control. [23]

Analytic, but intuitive. Managers are very good at using analytical tools to make decisions. Leaders rely on analytics to some degree. But at the same time, they are not afraid to rely on their “gut”—or use intuition and instincts. The use of intuition and instincts is obviously fuzzier than utilizing tools that involve quantification and systematization. But nevertheless, effective leaders find ways of blending together all of these tools and processes.

Rebel, but with a cause. James Dean was an actor who became famous in the early 1950s. One of his primary movies was Rebel Without a Cause. Like in the movie, Dean in real life had a reputation as a rebel, and like most rebels, there was no particular goal or cause associated with his rebellious nature. He was just rebellious, plain and simple. Leaders tend to also be rebels. They sometimes dress differently, do things that are different, and say things that may at times appear to be “out there” or off-the-wall. But one key thing separates leaders from James Dean—they are rebels with a cause. In others, leaders are rebels who also have goals or visions. Moreover, they try to get others to buy into their goals, ideas, and sense of purpose.

### 2.2 Leader Behaviors

A consideration of effective leader traits is interesting, and it is frequently the topic of discussion in both academic and popular culture circles. However, traits are not easily developed or modified. This fact runs in contrast to how in Chapter 1, we stressed the notion that effective leaders are at least somewhat “made,” rather than born. So what exactly can get “made,” so to speak?

**leader behaviors**
Actions taken in the pursuit of influencing others and attaining goals.

To answer this question, leadership thinkers have for years turned to behaviors. [24] In other words, the goal is to identify specific behaviors, or categories of behaviors, that might be associated with effective leaders. **Leader behaviors** involve actions taken in the pursuit of influencing others and attaining goals. They can be thought of in two ways: (1) generalized, and (2) decision-making.
