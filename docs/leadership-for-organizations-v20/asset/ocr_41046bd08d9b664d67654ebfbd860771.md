Let’s consider an example. An out-group member may set a dangerous precedent that poor behavior (e.g., marginal performance) is acceptable. Accordingly, others may question the fairness of having to behave or perform at a higher level, or to “pick up the slack.” Moreover, although LMX is all about relationships between leader and follower, out-group members are likely to have poor relations with other followers, especially those in the in-group. Again, we get back to the adage, “one bad apple can spoil the whole bunch.” The more that a team contains individuals with out-group relationships with the leader, the higher the chance of such spoilage occurring.

Further, to this point, in using the terms “in-group” and “out-group”, we have specifically considered the nature of separate relationships between a leader and each follower. However, if a person has a poor relationship with his or her leader, that person is also likely to have poor relationships with fellow team members. An exception might be when all or most team members have poor relationships with the leader. Nevertheless, in general, when out-group relationships go beyond the leader to affect follower-follower relationships, there is bound to be a negative impact on team cohesion and morale. Subsequently, team performance will suffer.

So the bottom line is that although out-group formation tends to be a natural phenomenon when it comes to relationships between a leader and individual followers, it is not something that should be tolerated or ignored over time. But if that’s the case, then what is the solution? How can out-group formation be remedied, or just prevented in the first place?

Those are not easy questions to answer. And it might be very tempting to simply put the onus on the leader to take actions to minimize out-group membership. To be sure, it is indeed fitting and proper for the leader to take actions to either prevent or remedy out-group formation. However, recall in Chapter 1 that inevitably, leadership effectiveness involves a combination of three factors: the leader, followers, and their context.

With that said, obviously, the leader plays an important role. To prevent out-group formation in the first place, the leader needs to maintain the leading with love philosophy that was considered in Chapter 2. With that overall philosophy, the leader stands the best chance of developing in-group relationships with the greatest percentage of followers. In addition, the leader needs to be cognizant of how certain followers might slip into an out-group, and how the leader him/herself could instigate such processes. As an example, we are aware of a faculty member who seemed to be in a Department Chairperson’s (i.e., the faculty member’s first-level supervisor) out-group. One sign is that the faculty member was never asked to get involved in formal, important committee work, such as being responsible for search activities for new faculty members. Instead, only the same, relatively small group of faculty members or “favorites” (i.e., in-group members) were targeted for important committee work. In this instance, the targeting had little, if anything, to do with performance of the people who were placed on committees. In short, it is imperative that the leader not play favorites, monitor if out-group formation might be occurring, attempt to understand the individual needs or styles of each follower, and remedy the situation if it appears that out-group formation is taking place.

On the other hand, followers play an important role in the relationship-making process with leaders, and thus in understanding what it takes to achieve effective leadership. Stated another way, leaders can demonstrate in an equal or uniform manner the effective characteristics and behaviors that were discussed in Chapter 2. But if particular followers do not reciprocate in kind, problems will develop causing those followers to sink into the leader’s out-groups.
