+   - Being punctual and conscientious, and
+   - Showing loyalty to the leader and certainly not doing anything that would undermine what the leader is attempting to accomplish.

One obvious exception to the last bullet above is if the leader was engaging in some sort of unethical, unsafe, or clearly unproductive act, in which case the issue of whistleblowing would arise. We will consider such issues in more depth in later chapters in this book.

Finally, there is the greater organizational context that comes into play that can affect the nature of leader-follower relationships or LMX. As mentioned in Chapter 1, leadership is not just about leaders, or even just about leaders and followers. The context in which they exist must always be considered if we are to truly understand effective leadership processes. A context can either help to facilitate leader-follower relationships, or it could even serve to damage them.

Let's consider a few key examples. First, many people in leadership positions are simply not aware of the potential occurrence of the LMX dilemma that is described above. Nor are they aware of what they themselves could do to potentially prevent such a situation from occurring, or at least remedying it after the fact. Accordingly, leaders should either be coached by their own supervisors, or trained to help deal with such problems. For example, leaders could be trained to recognize how their own biases and stylistic preferences could cause them to gravitate toward some followers (i.e., in-group members), while essentially shunning or not fully accepting others (i.e., out-group members). Such training could also include ideas for how to understand different individuals and their styles, and how to be more inclusive of all followers.

Second, organizational staffing processes can help prevent the LMX dilemma. Oftentimes, staffing is focused on technical skills and experience, rather than taking into account the extent to which an individual might fit within the greater organization, and more specifically, a team. But when person-organization fit is properly taken into account, we would expect to see fewer instances of out-group membership.[4] What exactly does that mean? For one thing, it would mean that any individual who might be inserted into a team would be thoroughly vetted by the formal leader through interviewing and having access to selection information (e.g., reference checking, resumes, and so forth). But in addition, other members of the team should be involved in the staffing process to ensure that the new member would get along with the team as a whole. While LMX is specifically about the relationship between a leader and a follower, if there are difficulties in relations among team members, it could end up affecting relationships between the leader and individual followers. So the bottom line is that while it may be important to properly screen individual followers in terms of technical skills or experience, they should also be selected to match (or “fit”) the leader and the team.

Southwest and WestJet Airlines represent organizations that have always stressed person-organization/team fit and the importance of relationships in the work setting.[5] For example, at time of selection into the organization, WestJet looks at personal qualities (e.g., sense of humor, penchant for wanting to have fun, desire to help others, and so forth) at least as much as they consider technical qualifications of candidates. In doing so, they help to ensure that everyone in the organization, and within teams, are on common ground in terms of key personal characteristics or values. As such, the chances of eventual out-group formation are minimized.

With all of that said, we should note that there is a potential danger in too much stress on person-organization, or person-team, fit. With too much emphasis on fit, the leader and team might be prone to select new members who only look like them, think like them, and act like them. When this happens, we might end up seeing a lack of diversity, which could result in complacency, lack of change, and what is often referred to as groupthink. Groupthink occurs when members of teams become so cohesive that they refuse to even listen to divergent points of view, or to filter in outside information that might run counter to decision-making processes. It tends to happen more in homogeneous or non-diverse teams. We will address the topics of team and diversity leadership in more detail in Chapter 8 and Chapter 9. For now, we simply caution that while person-organization or person-team fit is important in order to minimize the LMX dilemma, it can also be taken too far.
