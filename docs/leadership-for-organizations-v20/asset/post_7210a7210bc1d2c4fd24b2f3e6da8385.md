
## Kotter's Eight-Step Model of Planned Change

The general principles of unfreeze, change, and refreeze have been elaborated by John Kotter into a widely-used eight-step change model.[8] This model is portrayed in Figure 13.2:

- 1. Create a sense of urgency—Confronted with the need to change well-established routines, many employees ask themselves three important questions about the proposed change: Why? When? What's in it for me? Thus, there is the need to develop among employees a sense that there is an important need to change, as well as a commitment to make something happen. If the change is reactive, this is most often seen in the form of a potential threat. If the change is proactive, it often takes the form of a great opportunity that can be captured. Although in general, the message (or vision) to followers should be positive when promoting change (e.g., by being excited and enthusiastic about change), at least at the beginning, leaders may have to provide a dose of negative messaging. That is, there needs to be a message (or vision) that if change is not pursued, negative things are likely to happen for the firm, its employees, and its customers.

- 2. Form a powerful coalition—Since organizations are often inertial and resist change, the beginnings of a change effort require the identification of those key people who will support the change. This group can provide the initial energy for the change effort. As a part of forming this coalition, it is also useful to think carefully about how these change warriors can be used to identify and overcome those who might resist the change effort. On the opposite side of the coin, it is also important to identify powerful individuals who might thwart change. Such individuals need to be either persuaded, or potentially fired. The relationship building skills described in Chapter 3 can be quite handy in helping to form a powerful coalition for change.

- 3. Create a vision for change—Leader vision is an important aspect of effective leadership, and its role has been considered multiple times in this book. Perhaps nowhere is it as important as in the leading of change. Indeed, a key aspect of the leading of change is to provide an emotionally compelling reason for the change. Vision messages need not be complicated, and indeed, the purpose of the change effort should be expressed in a simple, emotionally compelling message. This vision and the associated strategy can help people understand what you are trying to achieve through the change, and how the effort can benefit them. It should help others “see” what the future organization will look like. The main goal is to provide a simple vision that inspires an emotional reaction. For example, when the Japanese construction equipment firm Komatsu was threatened by the American firm Caterpillar, their leaders adopted the simple vision of “Destroy Caterpillar” as a rallying cry.

- 4. Communicate and role-model the vision widely—Once formulated, the vision and reason for the change needs to be communicated frequently and powerfully at all levels of the organization. The communication should not simply be a slogan or message, but instead, illustrated with stories and examples. If employees are to be convinced that a change effort is important, they need to see and hear the message again and again, and through different means. Multiple forms of media should be used including video-casts, town hall meetings, and simple leading-by-walking around. Moreover, key leaders (e.g., CEOs) need to communicate directly to organizational members. But especially in larger organizations, they can also use surrogates (i.e., trusted followers or coalition members) to go out and spread the word regarding the need for change.[9] While a vision is important, if people do not see their leaders behaving consistently with the vision, it becomes little more than hot air. Worse, if leaders are not seen to be walking the talk, the vision will generate cynicism and resistance on the part of the employees. The implication is clear: senior leaders need to be continually modeling the changes that they want to see.

- 5. Remove obstacles—As the change unfolds, careful attention should be paid to identifying obstacles to the change so that people in the organization are empowered to act. These obstacles might include anything from the way the organization is structured, processes in place that currently reward or even train inappropriate behavior, and dealing with the people who oppose change. If a structure, HR process, or metric is lacking or seen to be hindering the change, leaders should remove the obstacle, and replace it with processes that are more favorable to the change. For example, training procedures may need to be changed. Further, a term that is anathema to change is bureaucracy. Bureaucratic procedures are designed to reinforce the status quo—not to help smooth the way for change. Many people in organizations have done quite nicely in the past in terms of job security and their careers by enforcing the current bureaucracy. Accordingly, there is a disincentive for such individuals to change. It follows that leaders of change need to be continually on the lookout for impeding bureaucracy and bureaucrats who might be resisting change. Indeed, direct action needs to be taken to deal with bureaucratic structures or rules that impede change, as well as the bureaucrats who might resist change. Similarly, large-scale organizational change may require new reporting relationships, which can also signal the importance of change.

- 6. Create short-term wins—In any change effort, many people will be initially skeptical and looking for indications that the effort is serious and can be successful, even if in little ways. A powerful way to signal this is to look for small initial successes, or quick wins, that can be held up as examples of the change effort. This might require finding easy wins that can be achieved without help from critics or that require little investment. For example, individual employees who are living or personifying the change should be acknowledged through stories, celebrations, or awards.

- 7. Build on the change—Major change efforts are more like marathon races, rather than sprints. A few short-term wins do not guarantee long-term success. For this reason, leaders need to be careful not to declare victory too soon. This might include setting a series of goals to be accomplished and emphasizing the need for constant feedback and improvement.

- 8. Anchor the changes in the organizational culture—As we discussed in Chapter 12, culture can be thought of as a social control system that drives behavior. For a change effort to be sustained, it is important that the new ways of behaving are reflected in the culture. This may include the creation of new positions that embody the change effort and send a very clear signal to employees about what is important. For example, the appointment of a “Chief Innovation Officer,” who is part of the top management team, can signal to the organization a new emphasis on innovation and change. Further, Human Resource functions can be a powerful way of communicating what is important during a change effort. Changes in how promotions and rewards are allocated are critical, and must be seen as aligning with change efforts. However, as also noted in Chapter 12, the big danger in a change effort is that the existing formal reward system (e.g., money and promotion) may not become aligned with the desired new behaviors. If through reward systems, leaders continue to reward the old way of doing things (i.e., the old culture), while professing to hope for changed behavior, the inconsistency will be glaring and demotivating.

In sum, Kotter’s eight-step model elaborates on the unfreeze-change-refreeze logic. The first three steps (sense of urgency, form a powerful coalition, and create a vision) suggest ways to unfreeze from the current state, and get people mobilized for change. Steps four to six (communicate the vision, remove obstacles, and create short-term wins) are ways that a leader can implement the change. The final two steps (build the change and anchor the change in the culture) are ways leaders can refreeze or sustain the change.

Both of these models emphasize the need for leaders of change to accomplish two important tasks. First, there is the need to help those who are affected by the change to understand why it is necessary and important that they support the change effort. This can occur by both creating a sense of anxiety (i.e., if change does not occur) and offering a positive sense of future opportunity (i.e., through new vision). Second, change leaders need to have a comprehensive plan for implementing the change that allows them to ensure that the change effort is systematic and controlled.

One thing that stands out in Figure 13.2 is the purple box. Although change needs to be anchored in the existing organizational culture, there is also the long-term reality that change is ongoing. So although leaders want change to “stick,” so to speak, they also want to keep their eyes on the future and the potential need for additional change. In other words, at least in the long-term, change should be viewed as an ongoing process.

A key reality in applying these models to real-life situations is that leaders need to acknowledge and deal with resistance to change.[10] Leaders of successful change efforts need to anticipate this reality and have a plan to overcome resistance. The following section will suggest a number of strategies and tactics that can help deal with resistance.

## Dealing With Resistance to Change

U.S. Army General Patricia McQuiston has said that, “People don’t hate change. They hate change that is forced upon them.”[11] In reality, there are various shades of perceptions toward change, ranging from eagerness to pursue it, to outright despise or fear of change.
