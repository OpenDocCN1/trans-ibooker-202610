
## 12.3 Aligning Culture With Vision, Mission, and Strategy

Is there such a thing as a “right” or best organizational culture? Asked another way, are there particular norms and values that will make any organization effective, and thus toward which a leader should direct his or her efforts? By and large, the answer is a “qualified” no. With that said, perhaps there are certain types of strongly shared norms and values that will prove to be effective for just about any firm. For example, one could argue that certainly over the long term, an organizational culture characterized by shared ethical norms and values is more likely to sustain competitive advantage, as compared to one that is less ethical.

Aside from ethical norms and values, organizational cultures can be characterized in terms of a number of dimensions. One popular characterization considers the following four dimensions and associated norms and values:

- 1. Order and consistency—adherence to rules and procedures, emphasis on stability rather than change, top-down command-and-control, reliance on proven approaches;
- 2. Achievement—being the best (e.g., leading in market share in an industry), aggressive action, emphasis on accomplishing “the numbers”, emphasis on personal initiative, competitiveness;
- 3. Clan—emphasis on building consensus and teamwork, equal status, being sensitive to the needs of others, having a friendly or “family” environment;
- 4. Adaptability—emphasis on experimentation, innovation, creativity, and risk-taking.[64]

Two issues should be especially noted regarding these dimensions. First, although some organizations will emphasize or have one of the above dimensions as predominant in its culture, other organizations may attempt to emphasize more than one of these dimensions simultaneously. Further, to some degree, an organization could have at least some of each of the above dimensions present in its culture. Second, there is some evidence that organizations with cultures that promote innovation (i.e., adaptability) and collaboration (i.e., clan) are more likely to perform better financially and have employees who are more positive about their work experience.[65] Conversely, a culture that is characterized by a high degree of order and consistency may become too rule-bound or bureaucratic, and thus, not be flexible in terms of changing environmental demands. With all that said, the real key is that there should be an alignment between the dimension(s) that is emphasized in a firm’s culture and the pursuit of competitive advantage. In other words, culture should be used as a mechanism to ensure the pursuit of vision, mission, and strategies that will achieve competitive advantage.

Some firms are quite large, making the task of building and shaping culture that much more difficult. For example, Walmart has 1.4 million employees in the United States. In a firm so large, how can any leader ensure that these employees are coordinated and doing the right things? The answer is that a fundamental task of any strategic leader is to coordinate the activities of large numbers of people pursuing interdependent activities. To accomplish this, strategic leaders design control systems that help employees understand what is important and how to behave. This means using both formal control systems (e.g., performance management systems, budgeting systems, inventory control systems, etc.) and social control systems (e.g., the culture of the organization)—as well as the alignment of formal and social control systems.

To be successful, leaders need to design control systems that support or are aligned with the firm’s mission and culture, as well as the leader’s vision and strategic goals. If one is a leader in an organization with a strategy of being a low-cost provider to its customers, then that leader will probably need control systems that allow him or her to monitor costs and expenses and, when necessary, act to reduce them. At least to some degree, the leader may need to build an order and consistency culture. If the leader’s strategy is to be an innovator, that person will need control systems and an *adaptability* culture that promote innovation (e.g., by providing resources for new products and measuring the sales of new versus old products). In addition, if innovation is the key strategic goal, "control" could start to resemble the paradox discussed in previous chapters. That is, leaders often gain control by giving up control. In any event, substantial research confirms that the alignment of strategy, control systems, and culture is associated with organizational performance.[16]

Finally, it should be noted that the four dimensions described above represent only one framework, or set of dimensions, for understanding organizational culture. For example, cultures can also be characterized in terms of the extent to which norms, values, and beliefs are moral or ethical. We will see an example of moral/ethical culture in the video case mentioned later in this chapter.

## Case Examples

### Southwest Airlines.

Since organizational culture can be thought of as a social control system, the same logic of aligning the culture with vision, mission, and strategy applies. If a leader’s strategy emphasizes low costs, does the organizational culture support this? To illustrate this point, think about Southwest Airlines, the biggest and most successful low-cost airline in the U.S. What culture has Southwest developed in order to ensure low costs? Airlines are a capital-intensive business, and one of the keys to success in any capital-intensive business (e.g., airlines, hotels, oil refineries, semiconductor manufacturing) is high asset utilization. In Southwest’s case, this means keeping the airplanes in the air. Part of this issue is handled through the formal control system and aspects of an *order and consistency culture* that efficiently schedules airplanes. But an equally, if not more important part is by turning around the airplanes quickly and being responsive to customers. Here, the key is a culture that promotes both flexibility and creativity (i.e., *adaptability culture*), as well as teamwork (i.e., *clan culture*), on the part of all employees.

An example of teamwork is how employees at Southwest do not strictly delineate their roles. For the purpose of turning around the airplanes quickly, pilots, ramp agents, and flight attendants typically work together to get a parked airplane ready for its next flight. In short, because employees care about each other and the company, they are more willing to pitch in, help each other out, and use their initiative to solve problems when they arise.[17]

Figure 12.2 depicts a "triangle of success" at Southwest with organizational culture as one point in the triangle. However, as also depicted in Figure 12.2, some key decisions or strategies have been executed throughout the firm’s history to implement its emphasis on combining customer satisfaction with the pursuit of low costs. *First*, customer satisfaction is not just a slogan, but has been implemented using a number of policies and strategies. For example, Southwest attempts to have point-to-point (or nonstop) flights between cities. Although not totally unique in the industry, this scheduling strategy stands in contrast to the hub-and-spoke model of large airlines like Delta and United, which rely on funneling passengers through one or more hub airports with connecting flights to destination cities. While somewhat efficient as a means of scheduling, the downside of a hub-and-spoke model is that it forces extra, connecting flights that are less than desirable for passengers. In addition, Southwest stresses on-time performance and encourages friendly service on the part of employees.

Second, Southwest emphasizes low costs and high asset utilization. Airlines represent a high fixed asset industry, so a key to success is to efficiently utilize assets. As an example, Gary Kelly, the current CEO of Southwest, was the architect for fuel hedging techniques that the firm has used over time to greatly reduce costs.[8] Indeed, fuel represents a major cost for airlines, and unique strategies or techniques for controlling such costs can represent a competitive advantage. Further, while on the ground, Southwest is able to turn around their airplanes quickly to get them ready to fly again. This is due in part to employee cooperation (e.g., pilots helping to clean cabins), which is stressed by the culture. It is also due to relatively low labor costs, as compared to industry norms.

What has been the result for Southwest Airlines over time? As shown in Figure 12.2, Southwest's culture is a key part of, although not the only reason for, their strategic success. That culture is interconnected to its ability to generate customer satisfaction, and maintain low costs while effectively utilizing assets. Throughout its history, strategic leaders at Southwest have made wise choices and implemented policies that continually align culture and strategy in order to achieve competitive advantage. It is interesting to note that since its inception decades ago, Southwest Airlines had never had an unprofitable quarter—that is, until the pandemic in 2020, when the bottom essentially fell out of the airline industry. Aside from 2020, the same can certainly not be said for its competitors, which have not only failed to achieve quarterly profits both prior and during the pandemic, but have also realized numerous bankruptcies over the years.[9]

### Zappos.

A similar illustration of how culture can be a source of competitive advantage can be seen in Zappos, the successful online shoe company. In 1998, Tony Hsieh sold his first internet company to Microsoft for $285 million. In 1999, he invested in a new company that was selling shoes online. Although initially skeptical, he soon realized that this was potentially a huge market. But to be successful it would require the company to do five things: (1) stock a huge inventory, (2) provide 24/7 access, (3) offer fast delivery, (4) provide customers with easy returns, and (5) most importantly, offer amazing customer service.

From the outset, Hsieh has emphasized the importance of culture: “Our whole belief is that if you get the culture right, most of the other stuff like delivering great customer service or building a long-term enduring brand will just happen naturally on its own.... Zappos is a service company that just happens to sell shoes.”[10] The Zappos culture is built on 10 values, some of which include delivering WOW service, embracing change, and creating fun and a little weirdness.
