### Learning Objectives

- 1. Describe how global leadership can be distinguished from more generic ways of thinking about leadership.
- 2. Identify how the American cultural context is somewhat unique as compared to other cultures, and implications for global leaders.
- 3. Explain the elements of a global leadership orientation and why they are important for a global leader's effectiveness.

- 5. Identify misinterpretations in cross-cultural situations and how to avoid them.

In Chapter 9, we addressed the role of diversity in leadership, with a special focus on women and men in leadership roles. In this chapter, diversity continues to take center stage. However, we now direct our attention more broadly to the global context in which leaders increasingly find themselves. Indeed, the global operations of firms have accelerated in recent times. For example, according to the World Trade Organization, global exports in 2014 were almost double that of 2005.[1] Coinciding with the global operations of firms, we see greater interaction among people from different cultures and societies. Accordingly, to achieve performance goals, global firms need to work effectively with a multitude of diverse stakeholders such as suppliers, customers, community members, governments, and non-governmental organizations.[2]

As a result, individuals at different levels in organizations need to assume global leader roles in order to ensure their own and their organizations' effectiveness. In so doing, they attempt to learn from the thinking of people in cultures other than their own. The opening quote of this chapter from President John Kennedy is emblematic of the thinking and broader perspective of a globally-oriented leader.

### 10.1 Who Are Global Leaders?

There are essentially two types of global leaders, and their distinction centers around one’s geographically-based, current living situation. First, an expatriate role represents a classic or traditional way of thinking about global leaders. An *expatriate* is essentially an individual who lives, typically with his or her family, in a country other than his or her origin, for at least six months at a time. So if an individual worked for a multi-national firm, was from the United States, but she did an extended assignment (e.g., one year) in Brazil, then that person would be considered an expatriate.

There can be many challenges for both expatriates and their firms. They include personal and family adjustments when living in other countries and additional expenses that are incurred by expatriates (e.g., education expenses for children of expatriates, moving expenses, and so forth).[3] Indeed, much research has been devoted to expatriates in terms of their ability to adjust to the requirements of living and working in a different country.[4]

A second approach to understanding global leader positions has grown in popularity in recent years. Specifically, global leader roles can be defined in terms of interactions with individuals from other parts of the world, regardless of whether one actually spends time living outside of his/her home country.[5] Given the advances in technology (e.g., teleconferencing) and international travel in recent years, this second approach to global leadership has become increasingly expedient and prominent. For example, a leader who is a “global domestic” is based in his or her own home country, but most of that person’s responsibilities or work-based interactions are with individuals in, or from, other countries.[6] While that person is likely to spend time traveling or interacting with stakeholders of his/her firm in other countries, the person remains based in his/her home country. And then there are the truly global leaders like Carlos Ghosn. Ghosn’s parents were Lebanese, but he was born in Brazil, lived as a child in Lebanon, went to college in France, was the CEO of the French tire company, Michelin, and went on to head up the car companies Nissan in Japan and Renault in France. He speaks fluent Portuguese, French, English, and Arabic, and spends much of his time on airplanes. Despite his qualities, Ghosn fell into trouble because of alleged gross misuse of funds at Nissan. As of early 2020, he was considered a fugitive from legal authorities in multiple countries.[7]

In sum, global leader roles reflect multiple types of internationally-based work contexts, which range from individuals who are fully embedded in a foreign country (e.g., expatriates) to those who live in their home or base country, but often engage with others from different cultures. A common denominator in global leader roles is a need to engage people from geographically diverse backgrounds or cultures. But what is this engagement all about, why is it important, and what life experiences tend to predict global leadership? We answer those questions in the sections that follow.

### 10.2 Understanding the American Cultural and Business Context

We begin with a simple principle: to understand and deal with other cultural or business contexts as a leader, one must first understand his or her own home context. In other words, the leader needs to first have a solid grasp on his or her own cultural context and ways of doing business and solving problems. From that cultural platform, the leader can then better understand and potentially adapt to other cultures that might be different. Below, to illustrate this principle, we highlight the example of the American cultural and business context. Although the American context is considered here, the same principle could be applied to leaders from any culture.

- 1. Cultural looseness.

Some cultures have strong social norms about what is the “right” behavior to show, and they also have strong means of sanctioning the wrong behavior—both through laws and social mechanisms of approval or disapproval.[8] For example, many Asian cultures tend to be characterized by cultural tightness. In contrast, American culture tends to be rather loose; for example, by fostering expectations that people will “do their own thing.” Part of the looseness stems from the existence of an array of sub-cultures that, to some degree, are even geographically-based (e.g., values and lifestyle in the Southern region of the United States are different from those of the North).
