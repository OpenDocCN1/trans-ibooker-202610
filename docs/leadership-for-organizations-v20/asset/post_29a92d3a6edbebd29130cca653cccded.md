
So instead of laying negative attributions on followers, what is the solution? That is not an easy question to answer, especially since as a general rule, people do not like to receive negative feedback. Nevertheless, negative feedback on the part of leaders is sometimes necessary to not only satisfy the goals of the organization, but to also help followers develop. But negative feedback need not be delivered in the form of judgments or attributions. As an alternative, leaders can specifically confront the negative behavior, report how that behavior affects them and the organization, and together problem solve as to a solution. Using the showing up late to class example mentioned above, a leader might:

- a. Describe the behavior to the follower (“I noticed that you showed up late to class today”).
- b. Describe how the behavior makes the leader feel (e.g., frustrated), or its effects on the organization (e.g., disturbing to the class process).
- c. Ask questions as to why the behavior may have occurred and together determine how the behavior can be prevented in the future. As phrased by Walt Whitman, “be curious, not judgmental.”

Notice how the above actions on the part of the leader involve no judgment or attribution of motive. But at the same time, the leader is feeding back his or her concerns, as well as listening to the follower. While there is no guarantee of behavioral change on the part of the follower, there is at least a better chance of success, while reducing the tendency toward a defensive reaction. In short, alternatives to attributional communication that are more precise, behavioral, and non-judgmental can be worthwhile when interacting with followers and co-workers, and even in personal relationships. Bill Campbell, the widely admired coach of Steve Jobs, Larry Page, Sergey Brin, and numerous other Silicon Valley CEOs, said it quite simply, “Be direct—not mean.”[13]

## One-on-One Coaching

Coaching is commonplace in many spheres of life, including sports and organizations. Our reason for introducing coaching here is that it constitutes a primary means by which leaders give feedback, and followers receive it. For the sake of describing leadership techniques, we partition coaching into one-on-one interactions between a leader and follower, versus the type of coaching that is shown by a leader to a team as a whole. This latter form of coaching is emphasized in the next chapter. Of course, in reality, these two forms of coaching can mix together in a fluid manner. For example, in one moment, a leader may provide coaching to a single follower, while shortly thereafter, the coaching is directed toward the overall team. The common denominator is that coaching in general is targeted toward performance improvement.

As shown in Table 7.1, one-on-one coaching may be viewed in terms of a logical sequencing of steps.[14] First, it is imperative that the coach establish performance goals or standards in his or her own mind, and then communicate them to the person being coached. This may seem intuitive, but it is quite possible that a coaching effort could turn into the proverbial “blind leading the blind” without a clear understanding by all parties concerned as to what effective performance is all about.

## TABLE 7.1 Key Steps of One-on-One Coaching

| Key Steps of One-on-One Coaching |
| :--- |
| 1. Establish and communicate performance goals or standards |
| 2. Collect performance data or make observations |
| 3. Analyze performance, including "gaps" |
| -preferred vs. actual performance |
| -self vs. other assessment |
| 4. Provide feedback, advice, and encouragement |
| -follow the "be here now" principle |
| -timely, specific, behavioral, balanced, and "owned" |
| -avoid negative attributions or judgments |
| 5. Get the follower's agreement that a problem exists |
| 6. Mutually devise and implement a development plan |

Second, effective coaching is based on good data. At a minimum, such data might consist of systematic, ongoing observations by the coach. However, it could also involve systematically-collected data from other sources. For example, below we will consider how 360-degree feedback processes can supply the data upon which coaches can provide their own feedback. As a caveat, one thing that can be detrimental is to rely on hearsay. When coaches base their feedback on hearsay or “I’ve heard it said that you ...,” defensive reactions are likely to ensue on the part of the person being coached. That person will naturally question the motives or validity associated with the hearsay evidence.

Third, the coach needs to process or analyze the data that have been gathered. An emphasis should be placed on potential gaps. The primary gap of concern is the difference between the desired or preferred performance that is identified in the first step, versus the actual performance that is suggested by the data that is gathered in step 2. However, a gap that is less obvious, but equally important, is the extent to which the person being coached perceives things differently than do others. For example, if an individual perceives that she is already performing at a high level on a particular dimension (e.g., quality of performance), but the coach or others do not perceive such a high level, then there is obviously a challenge for the coach to bring the individual’s perceptions more in line with those of others.

Fourth, based on the previous three steps, the key to coaching is to provide feedback, advice, and encouragement. In other words, this step gets at the heart of coaching, but on the other hand, it is not likely to be successful if the prior three steps are ignored. To successfully implement this step, the coach needs to follow the “be here now” principle that was emphasized in Chapter 1. That is, the coach needs to provide feedback and attend to cues from the person being coached with a minimum of distractions, and with full attention being shown toward that person (e.g., being in a listening mode, as well as feedback giving mode). Further, the feedback and advice needs to be timely, specific, behavioral, balanced, and “owned.” That is, feedback and advice should be: (a) as soon after any negative performance indicators are observed; (b) specific and behavioral in nature rather than vague and filled with general judgments or attributions; (c) balanced in terms of including positive feedback; and (d) “owned” in terms of the coach believing in the feedback, rather than simply relaying information from others. Regarding the latter point, even if much of the data that forms the basis of feedback is garnered from other individuals or sources, it is imperative for the coach to say that he or she believes in the validity of the data, rather than simply being a messenger.

The idea of balancing feedback is that the person being coached should not be overwhelmed with negative feedback. Getting back to the opening quote of this chapter, Mary Kay Ash was a champion of providing recognition and praise to her followers in order to make them feel valued and important. While negative feedback should not be avoided if warranted, the coach needs to ensure that the feedback recipient feels valued, and the best way to do that is to make sure that any negative feedback is balanced with positive reinforcement.

As noted above, it is important for the leader to listen, rather than just provide feedback. This may involve interspersing constructive questions that are directed toward the behavior of the person being coached, and then carefully listening to that person's answers. The overall goal is to make sure that the feedback being provided is understood.

Fifth, it is imperative that the coach gets a verbal acknowledgment or agreement that a performance problem exists. This is often a forgotten step, since coaching interactions often disintegrate into one-way communication exercises in which the coach talks, but the person being coached remains quiet. However, it is important that the feedback is not only understood, but that a problem is recognized by part of the person being coached; indeed, a problem that needs to be corrected.

Finally, to complete a coaching process, the coach and person being coached need to mutually agree on what actions will be taken after the coaching process is completed. Without this final step, it may not be clear as to the precise action steps to be taken to ensure performance improvement.

## 360-Degree Feedback

It is very common for employees of organizations to receive formal feedback regarding their performance through such mechanisms as performance appraisal systems. The typical process is that such feedback is received from one's supervisor formally on paper, as well as through a face-to-face meeting, which is done on an annual basis. A numeric evaluation is often applied (e.g., a 5-point scale, whereby 5 is the highest performance rating). As people move into leadership roles, performance (and thus, performance feedback) becomes more complex. That is, individuals increasingly have effects not only on objective performance indices (e.g., sales, productivity, etc.), but they also have an impact on a wide range of other people, such as the employees who they supervise. In addition, communication and feedback become even more important as people enter leadership roles. As mentioned earlier, while we typically think about leaders as communication givers, it is equally important to think about leaders as communication receivers. If leaders do not receive and absorb communication well through feedback, they are not likely to be successful—especially as they attempt to advance to higher level positions.

The practice of 360-degree feedback in organizations was conceived largely as a means of leader development.[15] As depicted in Figure 7.1, the underlying concept is that it is desirable for leaders to receive and learn from feedback from a variety of hierarchical and lateral sources (hence, the term “360-degree”) including bosses, subordinates, peers, and customers. Although some 360-degree feedback efforts include multiple sources, upward feedback is most common. Indeed, given the importance of leader-follower relationships, it is not surprising to see an emphasis on upward feedback.[16]
