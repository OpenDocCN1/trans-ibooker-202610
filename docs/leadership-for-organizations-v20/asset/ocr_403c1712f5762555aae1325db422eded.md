We fully realize that leaving is oftentimes easier said than done. After all, there may be few reasonable work alternatives for the individual. Nevertheless, alienation is not a healthy work situation for an individual, and if this represents the only way to ‘survive’ organizational change, alternatives need to be at least explored by the individual.

As a case example, one of the authors of this book previously served as department Chairperson in a relatively small business school. The management team of the school (i.e., the Dean, department chairpersons, and program directors) were considering the possibility of the school developing online programs of study. This consideration occurred at a point in time when online programs were in their early stage of development. The author continually pointed out problems with the development of online platforms, such as costs and the fact that other, bigger schools would be formidable competitors. He attempted to forcefully make his case in a series of meetings. But after some period of time, it became clear that most of the other people on the management team were leaning toward developing online programs. He made the choice of not attempting to obstruct the team’s efforts, but also not to quit his position. Instead, despite his doubts, he decided to support the greater team’s efforts in terms of this new direction.

# Two Caveats for Effective Followership

Sometimes it is not easy or straightforward to be an effective follower. There are a few caveats for effective followership that need to be mentioned. First, as stressed throughout Chapter 3, “it takes two to tango.” So just as effective followership is important to realize effective leadership, the reverse is also true—effective leadership is important for effective followership. If leaders do not encourage and reinforce independent, critical thinking and high levels of engagement on the part of followers, it is not likely to materialize. Instead, we are more likely to see either conformist or alienated followers.

We should further acknowledge that, in addition to the leader, organizational environments may or may not encourage effective followership. That is, aside from the leaders themselves, some contexts encourage independent thinking and engagement. In these environments, followers are likely to see other role models of effective followership, and they are likely to draw courage from others to challenge the status quo by being critical, independent thinkers. In short, it is easier to be an effective follower in some organizational contexts, as compared to others. Again, we return to a principle that was laid out in Chapter 1; specifically, that effective leadership processes involve three elements working in concert: the leader, the follower, and the context.

Second, loyalty can sometimes be taken too far—in terms of loyalty to either the leader, or the organization. Regarding both leaders and the greater organization, there may be times when excessive loyalty can be harmful, especially when moral values or even laws have been compromised. When this happens, the effective follower should no longer be blindly loyal, and instead, should speak up. A first step is to follow the maxim of “speaking truth to power.” The hope is that proper steps will be taken to change behavior or policies. As an example, Michael Woodford had been hired to be CEO by Olympus, the camera producer, several years ago. He discovered fraudulent behavior within the firm in order to cover up financial losses. He went to the Board of Directors, who essentially oversaw all actions and policies of the firm, including the CEO. However, the Board did nothing, except fire Woodford when he went public with the story. In the end, the Board ended up resigning, and several senior executives pleaded guilty to fraud.[6] In this particular instance, Woodford did not stay loyal to his superiors, nor to the organization that they were attempting to uphold. However, it could be argued that by “speaking truth to power,” he was defending the organization as it should be, rather than as it was.
