Americans. Although the Japanese negotiators cannot be clearly heard, it is obvious that they are speaking to one another in Japanese.

During the Japanese side-conference, the Americans also huddle and talk among themselves. One of the Americans speaks up and says, "You know these Japanese guys are really rude. For the most part, when I'm speaking, they won't even make eye contact with me. To add insult to injury, they can't even be honest with me. I know that sometimes they just want to say 'no' to my face, but they don't have the decency or maybe guts <big grin> to just come right out and say it." A second American speaks up and says, "Yeah man, I hear what you are saying. Look at them right now—all huddled up and whispering in Japanese. I'd bet that they're plotting some slick maneuver in these negotiations."

After about 5–10 minutes, the Japanese negotiators return to the table, and announce, "It is getting close to dinner time. We think that this might be a good time to take a break, and for all of us to go to dinner together. Perhaps we can find a restaurant and continue getting to know one another over food and drinks." One of the Americans then whispers in the ear of one of his colleagues, "These guys are just trying to stall. We had planned on wrapping this negotiation up today and being on a plane back to the U.S. tomorrow. And now these guys are just talking about eating, drinking, and schmoozing. Jeeezzz ..."

As mentioned in Chapter 7, people have a tendency to form quick judgments or attributions, oftentimes negative in nature, about the motives, behaviors, and personalities of others. This tendency can be even stronger in cross-cultural situations that provide a lot of uncertain or confusing information, such as the ones mentioned above. Indeed, we can clearly see these negative attributions or judgments in the words of the American negotiators who are attempting to make sense of the behavior of their Japanese counterparts.

In many instances, such attributions are wrong, and can lead to counterproductive behavior (e.g., negative accusations). For example, more often than not, when negotiators whose first language is not English (e.g., the Japanese individuals in our example) want to take a side-bar huddle, it is not because they are plotting some sneaky maneuver. Rather, it is simply because they do not understand some of the English that is being spoken, and are attempting to interpret it among themselves. One might wonder why they simply do not ask for clarification directly to the Americans. But in doing so, they might feel embarrassed or lose face.[28] In the Saudi example, it may simply be customary in his culture to hold hands with a male colleague, and it has no sexual connotations. It follows that any attribution of motives to the contrary on the part of the American would be a misinterpretation.
