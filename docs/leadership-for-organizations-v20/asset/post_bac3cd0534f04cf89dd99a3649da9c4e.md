## 8.1 Key Elements of Team-Oriented Coaching and Leadership

There are some basic elements involved in team leadership. The elements described below serve as the foundation for how leaders can develop or coach teams to achieve maximum performance. After first describing these elements, we will consider how they come into play throughout the stages of a team's development.

## Avoidance of Out-Group Relationships

In Chapter 3, we considered the potential problem of out-group relations between a leader and individual team members. If an effective team can be formed while essentially ignoring followers who have a poor relationship with the leader, then out-group membership might not be such a big issue for leaders who attempt to build teams. However, in reality, out-group membership is anathema to team leadership for several reasons. First, other team members often have problems with out-group members. Indeed, fairness issues can surface in that in-group members may start to believe that out-group members are getting a "free ride." If this happens, it will be difficult for a leader to form a unified team that is working collaboratively on common goals.

Second, even if fairness issues do not surface, the old adage "one bad apple can spoil the whole bunch" might come into play. In other words, an out-group member might spread his or her problems regarding the leader to other members of the team, essentially 'infecting' them with negative attitudes or feelings toward the leader and maybe the team as a whole. The upshot is that it will be difficult for the leader to form a unified team. Accordingly, as stressed earlier in this book, leaders should make attempts to avoid out-group formation in the first place. But if an out-group or a single "bad apple" somehow develops, the leader should take steps to remedy the situation.

## Shared Values, Goals, and Vision

"Everyone is on the same page." That's a metaphor for the foundation of what a team is all about. Whether the team is in sports, the business world, government, and so forth, if a collection of individuals is to function as a team, then everyone on that team must be on the same page. But what exactly does that mean? We argue that it means a shared sense of values, goals, and vision.

An example of a shared sense of values is the shared importance placed on playing defense in college basketball. Players, especially players who seek to eventually join the National Basketball Association (NBA), come out of high school wanting to demonstrate their ability to run, shoot, dunk—in other words, play offense. However, to win championships in college basketball, a team must also be excellent on the defensive side of the ball. John Calipari, coach of the University of Kentucky, has shown an ability to recruit high-level players, who often join the NBA after just one year of college play. However, through persuasion, reward, and discipline systems, he is also able to instill values and skills pertaining to defense.9

Likewise, in the business world, leaders need to instill values that they want to see in the teams that they lead. They can do so by leading-by-example, talking a lot (e.g., in team meetings) about shared values, and rewarding behavior that is in line with the desired values. Anne Stausboll was the CEO of one of the nation's largest retirement funds (CalPERS). She has said that "It's all about how you build the team, empower and appreciate. The more time you spend nurturing and developing, the more successful you'll be in your career."(4)

Take the example of customer service. If a leader wants to make customer service a core value of the team, the leader must show through his or her own actions the value and importance of customer service. Further, the leader needs to be consistent in terms of how customer service is rewarded at both the individual and team levels. So often, leaders and organizations talk a lot about the importance of one set of behaviors, and yet another set of behaviors gets rewarded. In other words, leaders may talk a lot about the importance of one set of behaviors or results, and yet a very different set of behaviors or results is actually rewarded.(5) For example, a leader might espouse that "customers and their needs should drive everything that we do." But in reality, the leader and the greater reward system in the organization simply provides rewards based on the amount of sales to customers. Such inconsistencies will not help the leader to build a cohesive and effective team.

Shared values form the basis of shared goals and vision. As will be described in a later chapter, goals are more short-term and tangible (e.g., increase customer satisfaction ratings by 20 percent over the next year). Vision tends to be less concrete and occurs over a longer time horizon (e.g., revolutionize the way that leaders are developed in the future through neurofeedback procedures). In the early 1980s, Steve Jobs took charge of the team that invented the Macintosh. Although Jobs was never viewed as a technical genius, he was seen by others and prided himself in being the "keeper of the vision."(6) In other words, on the various project teams that he led, he was instrumental at driving the formation of vision, and then maintaining vision even when conflict might erupt within a team, and deadlines were not met. The initial development of the Macintosh is a good example.(7)

## Dealing With the Paradox of Interdependence Versus Individuality

Team leaders have conflicting goals at times. On the one hand, they want team members to be somewhat interdependent, working collaboratively toward team goals and vision. But on the other hand, they want to encourage individuals to excel and show initiative. Indeed, although they would seem to conflict, these two goals are interdependent. In other words, it's hard to have a great overall team without individuals who excel. On the other hand, it's easier for individuals to excel in the context of a supportive and collaborative team. That said, how exactly can such a seeming contradiction be managed or led? There is no easy answer. For example, it might be natural for the leader to simply form strong bonds or relationships with those individuals who excel, while being more detached from other team members. However, as already noted, such differential treatment of followers tends to result in in-groups versus out-groups, which runs counter to team development.

In addition, the cross-cultural issues that are addressed later in Chapter 10 can come into play. For example, in a highly collectivist society (e.g., Japan), the leader of a team would tend to stress interdependence, rather than individual initiative. As such, the paradox of interdependence versus individuality becomes less of an issue. In contrast, in a cultural context like that of the United States, both individualism and collectivism are stressed. Accordingly, the paradox of interdependence versus individuality becomes more relevant and challenging as leaders attempt to maximize team effectiveness.

One possible solution to this paradox is for the leader to find ways for everyone on the team to excel. This means understanding each individual member, and what he or she brings to the table in terms of respective skills and motivations. Once this is known, the leader can work with each individual to excel, while simultaneously encouraging interdependence and collaboration toward common goals or vision. Phil Jackson is a controversial figure in the world of professional basketball. But most people agree that in his earlier years as a coach, he would find a way to deal with the prima donnas or superstars on his teams, and get them to work together as a team. Moreover, he would capitalize on the talents of players who were less than superstars in order to have them play important roles on a team. He said, "Good teams become great teams when the members trust each other enough to surrender the me for we."[8]

## Preventing or Dealing With Team "Illnesses"

- **groupthink**: When information that would run counter to established ways of thinking and acting in a team, and associated minority dissent, are essentially shut down or ignored by the majority.
- **free-riding/social loafing**: Phenomenon whereby some members of a team may work less diligently or with less effort toward team goals simply because they realize that there are others in the team who will pick up the slack.

There are systematic maladies or "illnesses" to which teams are especially vulnerable. Here we consider two main issues: (1) **groupthink**, and (2) **free-riding/social loafing**. Teams tend to have a desire to achieve harmony or a lack of conflict. But a key source of disharmony or conflict is information or thought processes that would run counter to a team's way of thinking about problems, or their norms. Groupthink occurs when information that would run counter to established ways of thinking and acting in a team, and associated minority dissent, are essentially shut down or ignored by the majority.[9] Groupthink tends to develop when the bulk of a team, oftentimes the in-group of the leader, becomes excessively cohesive. In doing so, it stops thinking creatively about problems, and it ignores information that might run counter to the team's way of doing things or solving problems. It follows that new challenges are not likely to be met successfully.

To begin to understand what norms are and how they operate to affect groupthink, let's consider a famous experiment that, at first glance, may seem puzzling. In this experiment, a group of college students were asked to estimate the length of lines projected on a screen. Watch "Asch Conformity Experiment". Unbeknownst to the real subject, the other participants were part of the experiment, and they gave what are clearly inaccurate estimates (too long or too short). When the naive subject was asked for his estimate, he or she just went along with the group.[10] The same thing happens with groupthink. Because of normative pressure, members tend to go along with the direction that a group is taking, even if there is evidence to the contrary. If such evidence is introduced, there is likely to be pressure to ignore it, and even shame the person who introduces it.
