That is, a particular team member(s) could be designated to be the devil's advocate for the team, and present information or opinions that run counter to the general flow of the team's thinking when dealing with issues and problems. Perhaps the chosen individual already has a reputation as being somewhat of a rebel, but is nevertheless a good employee who has the goals or mission of the organization at heart.

Groupthink is not the only potential problem facing teams, and it is not the only problem with which leaders should deal. Free-riding and social loafing are highly similar phenomena whereby some members of a team may work less diligently or with less effort toward team goals simply because they realize that there are others in the team who will pick up the slack.[11] One potential reason for free-riding/social loafing is what has been termed diffusion of responsibility. For years, diffusion of responsibility was examined in social contexts, whereby bystanders might not come to the aid of a person in need of assistance (e.g., a person being attacked on a street) because of the perception that others were observing the same event, which might cause a person to think, “why should I take action or assume responsibility; why not let others do so?”[12] However, the same thing could happen in teams in organizations as they pursue goals, whereby some team members might feel less responsibility to take actions simply because of their realization of having other team members who could take up the slack. The problems of free-riding/social loafing are twofold. First, when one or more members are not contributing, the overall performance of the team is likely to suffer. Second, perceptions of inequity or injustice are likely to occur, whereby highly performing members may start to feel like “suckers” who are being taken advantage of by the free riders or social loafers.

What can leaders do to prevent or deal with free-riding/social loafing? As is the case with groupthink, the first step is to simply recognize the likelihood of the phenomenon occurring. In other words, the leader should be on the lookout for free-riding/social loafing among team members. Other things that leaders can do is to make sure that individuals understand minimal standards, and then take steps to monitor to make sure that everyone in the team is contributing. Finally, this problem is related to out-group membership in that out-groupers are more likely to be free riders or social loafers. Accordingly, the potential damage of this phenomenon is just one more reason why leaders should also attempt to prevent or remedy out-group formation.

## 8.2 Leadership and the Stages of Team Development

Teams tend to progress in certain stages as they develop over time. The four primary stages are shown in Figure 8.1.[13] An additional stage, adjournment, has been recognized by some authors.[14] During this final stage, the team is essentially disbanding. It is perhaps most relevant to temporary project teams. In our analysis here, we will focus on the stages that are shown in Figure 8.1, since they deal directly with a team's performance and the accomplishment of its mission.

Three things should be noted about Figure 8.1. First, the goal of a team leader should be to move the team as expeditiously as possible toward the 4th stage of performing. In this latter stage, the team is fully functioning and productive. This is not to say that any stage can be overlooked or hurried through. For example, although it would seem that the 2nd stage of “storming” should be as quick as possible, or even ignored, it has its purpose in terms of airing differences and identifying underlying interpersonal problems.

Second, although there is a natural progression forward from stages 1 to 4, it is possible for a team to revert back to an earlier stage. For example, a team might be in either the norming or performing stage, and experience a major event that might cause conflict within the team (e.g., a new team member, who other team members do not like interpersonally). In this instance, the team could quickly revert back to the storming stage. As another example, a new leader might be introduced into a team, and he attempts to get rid of members (either voluntarily or involuntarily), who he perceives to be detrimental to the team. As a result of the turnover, the team might in essence be forced back to the forming stage of development.

Third, this latter example demonstrates the potential effect of leadership at the various stages of development. In the forming stage, the leader attempts to form ground rules of behavior. For example, in a relatively temporary problem-solving team, a leader might establish brainstorming as a ground rule, whereby virtually no idea for solving a problem would be dismissed out of hand. The leader might also put forth a vision of what the final product or solution of the team might look like. As mentioned later in this chapter, that vision could be initially crafted or honed through shared leadership processes with team members.

In the storming stage, attempts to surface underlying differences that could be causing conflict, while encouraging dialogue, rather than argumentative discussion. With dialogue, judgment and preconceived notions are suspended, people are asked to listen rather than just talk or push their own ideas, and all voices are heard. With that said, it is not realistic that all conflict in teams can be solved through dialogue, and in some instances involving deeply ingrained or personal problems, team members may need to be separated, transferred, or even fired. Unfortunately, in order to avoid confronting conflict, a leader might attempt to simply sweep that conflict under the table, so to speak. In other words, the leader might simply avoid a festering conflict, such as two team members who seem to be continually arguing or undermining each other. However, such avoidance behavior rarely works, and the conflict might spread to other team members, even to the point of forming separate factions within a team.

In the *norming* stage, the leader attempts to apply clarity to roles, norms, and values of the team. For example, the leader may help to clarify the different obligations and team needs that various members can help to satisfy. As a further example, the leader might attempt to stress the norm of showing up on time to meetings (i.e., either face-to-face or virtual).

Finally, in the *performing* stage, the leader works to help build the team's identity, especially if the team will be relatively ongoing over time. For example, team members might be encouraged to wear T-shirts to work (at least on certain days) displaying the team's logo or perhaps a nickname. Perhaps even more importantly, when a team is in the performing stage, it is important for the leader to monitor the team's environment for any changes that are occurring that could affect the team's goal or mission. If changes are needed, it is important for the leader to introduce new ideas or vision.

### When Leadership Is Lacking

Figure 8.1 also shows what can happen if leadership is lacking at any of the four stages of team development. In the *forming* stage, a lack of leadership could cause confusion and a lack of common direction or norms for the team. When the team is *storming*, a lack of leadership could engender distrust and the type of negative judgments and attributions between team members that was considered in Chapter 7 of this book. The communication of such judgments and attributions can cause conflict to be highly destructive, rather than fostering the constructive solution of problems. For example, if a leader observes a team member accusing another member as being lazy or irrational, the leader can insist that such accusations not be part of the team discourse, and instead, emphasize dialogue whereby specific problematic behaviors are considered and discussed in a respectful manner. If roles, norms, and values are not clarified by the leader in the *norming* stage, there could be a lack of teamwork toward common goals, and the team might even drift back to the *storming* stage.

Finally, if leadership is lacking in the *performing* stage, the team's goals and mission could become outdated in relation to the needs of its environment (e.g., customers). It is interesting that often if a team is clearly performing on all cylinders, so to speak, it can be tempting for a leader to take a hands-off or laissez-faire approach. The leader may go into more of a managerial or administrative mode, but not show the type of leadership that has been described in this chapter and elsewhere in the book. Unfortunately, this is a key reason why so many teams start to engage in groupthink, which so often occurs in the performing stage, and is likely to inevitably damage a team's viability.
