
## Leader-Member Exchange and Relationship-Building

> "Even the worst employee can make a difference. The test of leading is not with the easy-to-manage employee, but with the difficult-to-manage employee. Work with that person, set new goals and coach and encourage him or her to succeed." —Bob Nelson, author and management consultant

## Learning Objectives

- 2. Define in-group versus out-group relationships.

Leadership is largely about forming and maintaining relationships over time with individuals who the leader is attempting to influence. Indeed, relationships are key to the influence processes that are essential to leader effectiveness. In other words, leaders influence, and relationship-building is pivotal to being able to influence others. Relationships can be formed with a variety of individuals, including peers, superiors, customers, and perhaps most importantly, followers. In this chapter, we address all of these types of targeted individuals with a particular focus on followers. We begin by describing the nature of relationship-building with followers and its importance.

### 3.1 Leader-Member Exchange

A person in a leadership role can initially come into contact with a follower in one of two ways. First, the leader can be involved in selecting the follower into his or her team, perhaps in conjunction with others who might have input in the selection process (e.g., human resource officials). Second, the leader might be placed (e.g., through promotion, transfer, and so forth) into a context where followers already exist. In either instance, the leader will form a relationship with followers that evolves over time. There is some truth to the old adage, “love (or hate) at first sight,” which would suggest that the relationship between leader and follower gets carved in stone early on. Nevertheless, there is at least some tendency for relationships to change or evolve over time—sometimes to be more favorable, but unfortunately, sometimes to be less favorable.

### leader-member exchange (LMX)

The manner in which relationships between leader and followers forms or evolves; how a follower assumes roles; how the leader acts or responds towards the follower, and the “chemistry” that occurs.

How exactly do these relationships form or evolve? Much of the answer to this question lies in how a follower assumes roles, how the leader acts or responds towards the follower, and in the ‘chemistry’ that occurs. In other words, when it comes to leader-member relationships or what is known as leader-member exchange (LMX), it definitely “takes two to tango.” to quote an old adage. To some people, this may seem counterintuitive, since they might feel that leadership is all about the leader, and what he or she does in relation to followers. However, our premise in this chapter, as well as the next, is that followers also play a key role in LMX.

The main idea of LMX is that leader-follower relationships are based on exchanges. But what exactly do leaders and followers exchange? In other words, what are the “currencies” of exchange?[1] Essentially, there are two types of exchanges: (1) tangible, and (2) intangible. Tangible exchanges are in line with what we traditionally consider “currencies” to be all about. They are economic or productivity-based. The most basic exchange is one’s salary for satisfactory performance. However, tangible exchanges can be a bit more passive in the form of simply being left alone if one is performing satisfactorily. For example, a follower may simply prefer to be left alone or avoided by the boss if that follower is performing adequately, rather than being continually questioned, being the subject of abuse or ridicule, and so forth. In other words, such avoidance could be seen by some followers as a positive exchange—or at least a lack of negative exchange.

Tangible exchanges form the foundation of a relationship between leader and follower, and if missing or perceived to be unfair, could certainly cause problems. However, exchanges of this nature do not account for the full scope of possibilities with regard to leader-follower relationships, and they do not fully explain the long-term nature of relationships. Indeed, not all exchanges involve monetary currencies, or things that are readily translatable into monetary value. Intangible exchanges are also important to consider. An intangible exchange pertains to things like friendship (or friendliness), acceptance of mutual responsibility, loyalty, and trust. For example, trust is a powerful factor in any relationship, including those between leaders and followers. Without trust, the leader will feel a need to closely monitor the actions and behaviors of the follower, which takes a lot of time and tends to generate anxiety on the part of both leader and follower. Likewise, the follower will only put forth enough effort to satisfy the “contractual” nature of their relationship.[2] Moreover, the follower will not be motivated to be creative or find solutions to vexing problems, for fear of failure or reprimand. However, if trust exists, the relationship is more relaxed, and followers are likely to be motivated to put forth extra effort and creativity, and accept greater responsibility.

In terms of the characterizations of LMX, one of two basic directions are likely to occur. These different directions are commonly referred to as in-groups vs. out-groups, and they are depicted in Figure 3.1.[3] First, a follower could become part of a leader’s in-group, which is based largely on the type of intangible exchanges described above. Leaders embrace followers into their in-groups for different reasons, but one key reason is that a follower will demonstrate early on that he or she can perform well and take on increasing responsibility. This would obviously take pressure off of a leader to have to closely monitor the follower, which given the time demands of leaders, is always a welcomed relief. Alternatively, the leader might embrace a follower because he or she has similar values, interests, or background. For example, a leader who enjoys playing golf as a pastime will naturally take favorably to a follower who also plays golf. The two individuals will likely spend more time together, perhaps as compared to time spent by the leader with other followers, which then reinforces in-group membership. Keep in mind that such “membership” is informal, and not discussed or perhaps not even recognized by either party. The upshot of being in the leader’s in-group is that the follower will enjoy better work assignments or conditions (e.g., hours worked), receive positive coaching and feedback, and be able to provide a lot of input into decision-making and problem-solving.

The bottom line is that an in-group relationship between a leader and follower is based on trust. The leader trusts that the follower will assume increasing responsibility with little need for close supervision. The follower trusts that the leader will support him/her and look out for that person's best interests. While feelings of trust can potentially start to form early on in their relationship, in order to solidify, the leader and follower will continually seek cues from each other over time and observe each other's actions.

On the other hand, it is possible that an out-group relationship might form, and be based primarily on tangible exchanges. Out-groups can be characterized as the mirror opposite of in-groups. An out-group member is likely to be a poor performer or one that simply cannot or will not assume a higher level of responsibility. He or she often does just enough work to satisfactorily meet expectations. The out-group member may have a much different background (e.g., based on gender, age, or ethnicity) from the leader, and not share values or interests with the leader. One thing that can quickly and irrevocably put an individual into the out-group is if that individual publicly disrespects or undermines the leader. This can happen in many different ways. For example, a follower might show disrespect toward the leader in a team meeting or in their conversations with other leaders in the organization (e.g., the leader's boss). As another example, the follower may continually try to ignore or undermine attempts at organizational change or new directions that the leader is attempting to pursue. If these sorts of actions occur on the part of a follower, there will be a lack of trust on the part of the leader toward the follower.

One thing that should be made clear is that the in-group vs. out-group distinction regarding leader-member relationships is a pure, and perhaps sometimes artificial, distinction. As an alternative, three other possibilities come to mind. First, some followers may be in-between, i.e., neither in the in-group nor the out-group. These followers may be moderately valued and trusted by the leader, but not to the extent of in-group members. Second, it is possible that all followers are in the out-group. This obviously represents a toxic situation, and if it occurs, it is probably the result of poor leader behavior. Third, it is possible that all followers are in the in-group. As discussed below, this situation represents the ideal, and it is likely to require positive actions on the part of both the leader and all followers in order to be realized. Again, “it takes two to tango.”

### The LMX “Dilemma”

Although some degree of out-group membership is a naturally occurring phenomenon for leaders, in an ideal sense, it is not desirable. Out-group membership has the potential for much negative fallout. For example, since by definition, out-group members tend to be marginal performers, their presence is likely to negatively affect an organization's performance.
