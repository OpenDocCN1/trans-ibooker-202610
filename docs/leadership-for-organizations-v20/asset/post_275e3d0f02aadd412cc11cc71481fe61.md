People who are self-aware are highly in tune to their own emotions, and the effect of those emotions on their functioning. Thus, a self-aware person might recognize that she is highly anxious, and that this anxiety is noticed by others, such as followers. People who are socially aware can recognize and show empathy toward the emotions that are being felt by others. For example, a leader might pick up on the fact that followers are showing a lot of trepidation during times of change (e.g., an organizational restructuring), and thus try to be a calming force of reassurance for followers that change will end up being beneficial for all.

Regarding actions taken, again, emotional intelligence pertains to one's own emotions, as well as the emotions of others. First, there is self-management. An emotionally intelligent person is able to control his own emotions. For example, although a follower may make a seemingly careless mistake involving customers, the emotionally intelligent leader would not “blow up” when addressing or providing feedback to that follower. Scott McNealy, the founder and CEO of Sun Microsystems, said that “As a CEO, you have to attenuate, not amplify ... you can’t freak out.”[6] Although it may seem counterintuitive, a leader might also at times need to control more positive emotions. For example, when considering an ethically-charged problem in a team meeting, a leader may need to remain somewhat somber, thus avoiding excessive joy or excitement, which would not be an appropriate emotional response in that context. Second, there is relationship management, which involves the ability to connect with others emotionally through such actions as inspirational messages and the show of compassion. A leader may at times also need to deal with the strained emotions between followers, such as when followers are having disputes among themselves. In sum, emotional intelligence involves oneself as a leader, as well as connections with others. As we will see throughout this book, it is key to many aspects of leading, such as forming good relationships with others (e.g., followers, one’s boss, peers, and customers). One question that can be asked is what is more important: basic intelligence (i.e., IQ) or emotional intelligence? While the answer is not simple, basic intelligence may actually not be important as emotional intelligence for leaders.[10] But once again, as stressed in Chapter 1, context may come into play. Obviously, the relationship between IQ and leader effectiveness may be greater in contexts involving knowledge workers (e.g., scientists and engineers).

## Basic Needs Profile

All humans have needs. Some of those needs are quite basic, such as the need for sustenance through food and water. But beyond such basic needs, people have higher order needs that could pertain to their potential effectiveness as leaders. There are three key needs that are relevant to leaders:[11]

- 1. Need for achievement. This need involves the desire to accomplish, achieve mastery, and obtain individual recognition for achievements. While a moderate level of need for achievement is a good thing for leaders, if an individual has too much of it, she will not be able to allow others to "share the limelight". In other words, for the purpose of motivating followers, it is important that leaders share the recognition for accomplishments with followers.

- 2. Need for affiliation. People with a strong need for affiliation desire many close relationships and friendships, while wanting to avoid conflict. At first glance, it might seem like leaders need to have such strong needs. However, a strong need for affiliation could cause a leader to want to be close friends with followers, while avoiding tough decisions (e.g., reprimand or even dismissal of an employee) that may need to be made. Bob Sutton, a professor and author of the book The No Asshole Rule, captures this issue when he notes that "From a performance standpoint, liking the people you manage too much is a bigger problem than liking them too little."[12] Similarly, Spencer Rascoff, the CEO of Zillow (an online real estate company) has said that "I like to be liked, but sometimes managing people is not a popularity contest."[13] Accordingly, while leaders should certainly not seem aloof or unfriendly, a more moderate need for affiliation is typically best.

- 3. Need for power. This need involves a desire to influence or control others. Again, at first glance, it might seem like the most effective leaders have either a low, or at most moderate, need for power. But in reality, some of the best leaders have a strong need for power. The key to understanding this idea is to first realize that power can be either personalized, or alternatively, it can be socialized. Personalized power is all about the leader and his or her personal interests. So when using personalized power, the leader attempts to gain resources or manipulate situations for his or her own gain. Eventually followers will see through a leader with a personalized power motive, and they will lack trust in the leader and be uninspired by him or her. Kent Thiry, the CEO of DaVita, makes this point by observing that "Intentionality rules. People can smell your intentionality—whether your relationship is about a means for advancing your career or an end in and of itself."[14] In contrast, socialized power pertains to the garnering of power so that others can benefit. As an example, Martin Luther King used his power with various groups (e.g., government officials) in order to benefit his many constituents. Likewise, in the business world, leaders like Herb Kelleher, founder of Southwest Airlines, are notorious for how they use their power in order to benefit employees. In short, a strong need for socialized power is a good combination for leaders.

In sum, the best profile for leaders is likely to be moderate need for achievement, moderate need for affiliation, and strong need for socialized power. An effective leader should have a moderate need to achieve, but if taken too far, that need may ignore or overshadow similar needs of followers. Further, an effective leader needs to be a "people-person," but not get too close to those who he or she is leading. Finally, contrary to some people's intuition, effective leaders do have a strong need for power. However, they are able to channel that need toward using power to help and benefit others.

## Character

character
Moral excellence and firmness on the part of leaders.

Leaders need to have character. That statement may seem obvious, but what does it really mean? The dictionary defines character as moral excellence and firmness.[15] We will address ethical and moral leadership in more detail in Chapter 6. But for now, suffice it to say that there are several key characteristics that are associated with character on the part of leaders.

First, there is a good chance that the leader who you considered at the opening of this chapter displayed integrity, which is why you thought of him or her as a leader. Indeed, integrity is often times one of the first characteristics that people consider when they hear the term "leader." One interesting thing about integrity is that it involves both commission and omission. Commission is probably what we first consider when evaluating the integrity of an individual. For example, we think of words spoken, and whether those words match actions that are taken. When they do match, we say that the person is "walking the talk," and we are likely to conclude that the individual has integrity.
