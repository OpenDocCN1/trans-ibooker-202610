
4. Possible to attain and within a latitude of acceptance. There can be a fine line between vision and insanity. Vision extends one's perspective in ways that can seem far-fetched and risky. But if the vision goes too far, it can seem ridiculous and not worth considering. If President Kennedy had said that by the end of the 1960s, the United States should commit itself to putting a man on Mars, and returning him safely to earth, people would have thought that he was either joking or had lost his mind. In short, while a vision stretches people's imaginations, it is nevertheless viewed as possible, and thus acceptable. Interestingly, leaders such as Elon Musk in more recent times are espousing a vision that includes the colonization of Mars, and potentially returning Mars visitors safely back to earth.[19] Such vision, though still bold, is now seen as possible to attain and within a latitude of acceptance.

5. Shared widely, but pieced out individually. The idea of a vision being “shared” is a two-way street. That is, it is not only important for a leader to share or communicate a vision widely among followers, but it is also important that followers take the vision to heart, so to speak, and share it. In other words, if a vision is to ultimately be implemented, a wide swath of followers need to be emotionally committed to that vision. One way to make sure of such commitment is to not make it “the leader’s vision” per se. In other words, it is desirable for followers to co-create vision along with the leader, or at least have a voice in terms of how the vision is shaped. For example, a leader might “float” a new vision. Followers then work with the leader to hone or modify that vision. In addition, to realize shared vision, it is important that followers, or groups of followers, understand how the vision applies to their own work or their organizational units. In the case of President Kennedy’s vision, various units of NASA, the military (e.g., the U.S. Navy), and private organizations (e.g., contractors for NASA) each had their own parts to play to ensure the realization of the vision.

The previous elements outline the nature of effective leader vision. However, it is not just the words that matter, but also how the leader behaves in relation to the words. Visionary leaders communicate enthusiastically with both passion and energy. Moreover, they serve as role models for their visions. For example, consider a leader who introduces a new vision that includes greatly improved attention to customers and their needs. Followers will need to see actions or signals, as reflected in the leader’s own behavior, that attending to customers has truly taken on more significance, perhaps even at the expense of other, more short-term goals (e.g., achieving quarterly profit goals).

## How are Leader Values Connected to Inspiration/Vision?

Values pertain to strongly held beliefs that tend to guide the actions of individuals. Specifically with regard to leaders, values are relevant to decisions that are made. It should be apparent by now that there is a link between values and inspiration/vision, but what exactly is that link? Some research by Sully de Luque and colleagues has directly addressed that question.[20] The researchers examined over 500 companies spanning seventeen countries from several different continents. First, they asked CEOs of these firms how much importance should be attached to various considerations or groups/entities when these CEOs make strategic decisions. They labelled the importance of these considerations as decision-making values. One set of items dealt with stakeholders, including employees, customers, physical environment, and the greater community or nation in which the firm operates. These people or entities are referred to as stakeholders, since they all have a stake in how the firm operates or what it accomplishes—both good and potentially bad. CEOs who rated these items as highly important in their decision making were characterized by the researchers as strong on stakeholder-based values.

A second set of items pertained to economic concerns, such as the importance of considering profits, sales volume, and cost controls. When CEOs reported that such concerns should take predominant importance in their strategic decision-making, the researchers characterized those CEOs as strong on economic values. In rating both stakeholder and economic values, CEOs were asked which ones should be predominant, versus being more secondary. Before proceeding, try taking the survey in Section 7 to see how you personally view stakeholder vs. economic values.

The researchers also asked followers or subordinates of each CEO about that person's leadership. CEOs who were rated high on inspirational/visionary leadership were seen by followers as being energetic and enthused, and having good insight into what may happen in the future. In addition, these followers rated their CEOs on autocratic leadership. CEOs who were rated high were seen as telling subordinates what to do in a commanding manner and forcing their ideas on to others.

Results of this research are depicted in Figure 11.2. As shown in the figure, this research connects three questions:

- 1. What are the values that CEOs stress when they make strategic decisions? Are stakeholder values predominant for a particular CEO, or does the CEO put the most emphasis on economic values?
- 2. How do followers view the CEOs in terms of inspirational/visionary leadership, versus autocratic leadership?
- 3. What outcomes does the firm experience?

Several findings of the study help us to answer how a leader's values connect to inspiration/vision. First, leaders who had strong stakeholder values in their strategic decision-making were more likely to be seen as inspiring/visionary, and less likely to be viewed as autocratic by followers. In contrast, leaders with strong economic values were more likely to be seen as autocratic, and less likely to be viewed as inspirational/visionary. Second, organizations with inspirational/visionary CEOs tended to achieve more commitment and extra effort from employees, while those with autocratic CEOs achieved less. Third, as a bottom line outcome, organizations with inspirational/visionary CEOs (and those with stakeholder values) had a stronger chance of achieving better financial outcomes in terms of return on investment and sales growth relative to competitors.

What can we learn from this research? We see the following key takeaways:

- * Stakeholder values engender perceptions of better leadership. Leadership is largely in the eye of the beholder. In other words, what really matters is how others view an individual's leadership. Ask yourself the following question: In a leadership role, would you rather be viewed as inspiring/visionary, or would you rather be viewed as autocratic? Most people who want to learn more about and develop their own leadership would probably say the former—inspiring/visionary. It could be that on a day-to-day basis, a leader who shows that he or she attempts to stress a broad range of stakeholders when making decisions is demonstrating a long-term, visionary perspective that followers find to be inspiring. Conversely, when a leader seems to be more narrowly stressing profits, controlling costs, and so forth, there is a good chance that he or she is doing so in an autocratic manner and is less inspiring.

- * Stakeholder values are associated with inspiring core purpose and an integrator orientation to responsibility. We note two things here about stakeholder values. First, these values are very much in line with the socially-based, core purposes shown on the right side of Table 11.1, which individuals also find to be inspiring. Second, strong stakeholder values are in line with the type of integrator orientation to responsible leadership that was discussed in Chapter 6. In other words, a strong and broad sense of stakeholder-based values on the part of leaders aligns with an integrator orientation toward responsibility.

- * Stakeholder values and inspirational/visionary leadership are likely to result in good outcomes. The outcomes that these values and leadership are likely to achieve are not only in terms of direct effects on employees (i.e., commitment and extra effort), but also in terms of bottom-line financial outcomes of the firms of leaders.

- * Stakeholder values and inspiring/visionary leadership are global. One interesting aspect of this research is that it involved firms from many countries and continents (including Africa).
