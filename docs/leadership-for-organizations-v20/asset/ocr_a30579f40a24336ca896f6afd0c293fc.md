The common denominator of the hypothetical statements on the left side is that they focus on a particular product or profits. On the right side of Table 11.1, we see a different story or theme. All of these statements are broader in their own way. An example of an innovation theme can be seen with 3M. The GE mission was reshaped under the leadership of perhaps its most famous CEO, Jack Welsh. It clearly represents an excellence theme. A number of these themes are clearly social purposes in nature. For example, Mary Kay Ash established her firm for the core purpose of enriching the lives of women. "Enriching" to some degree took on a literal meaning in that she stressed how her employees could make money through their efforts, and thus achieve security. When her firm was first developing in the 1970s and 1980s, this was very important, since women had few options in the workforce for the purpose of making substantial money and having a sense of security through their work. It should be noted that sometimes a firm may wish to combine these themes, as is the case with the Motorola mission, which combines innovation and social purpose.

Three things need to be emphasized when comparing the potential (but hypothetical) missions on the left side of Table 11.1 with those on the right side. First, those on the right side are more compelling and inspiring. For example, Joel Peterson is the former CEO of a large real estate company, the current Chairman of JetBlue Airways, and a professor at Stanford. He has said, "People have values, dreams, and goals. If people believe that you can help them live this and do something meaningful, they'll follow you."[15] Brad Smith, the CEO of Intuit, echoes this notion by stating that "People want to be part of a cause greater than themselves. They want to solve important problems that matter to their mother and father, their friends . . . so have a cause, don't just be a product."[16] Kevin Johnson, the NBA all-star and former mayor of Sacramento, California, has said "You need to make people feel like they are doing something bigger than their individual work."[17] Indeed, potential employees, customers, and even investors tend to get more excited by the "bigger" statements on the right side. In the case of Liberty Mutual, it is mundane to be guided by a simple mission of selling insurance. On the other hand, since the overarching mission is actually to help people live safer and more secure lives, employees and other stakeholders are more likely to be inspired. Of course, this assumes that organizational leaders and practices (e.g. human resources practices) reinforce the mission, rather than treating it as simply a slogan.
