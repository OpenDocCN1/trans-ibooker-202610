## Decision-Making

More specific than day-to-day actions and behaviors (or generalized leader behaviors), leaders are inherently decision-makers. In other words, a specific, important behavior in which leaders engage is decision-making. In Chapter 1, we defined leadership in terms of influence processes. As such, in attempting to understand effective leadership, we seek to determine styles of leader decision-making rather than the actual nature or basis of decisions made. As an example, a leader could make a decision that emphasizes shareholder or production interests, over the interests of customers. While the nature of such decisions has implications for a leader's organization, they do not address leadership processes per se.

Four basic styles of leader decision-making have been identified.[25] First, a leader may approach a decision-making process in an _autocratic_ manner, whereby she makes the decision alone and simply announces it to followers. Second, a leader may first _consult_ with followers, get their ideas and suggestions, and then make the decision by herself. Third, the leader could use _participation/facilitation_, whereby she presents the nature of the problem to followers, possible boundaries in which a decision or solution might be derived, and then together the leader and followers come to consensus as to a decision. Fourth, the leader might _delegate_, which involves one or more followers deriving a decision pertaining to a problem on their own. While the leader would play no role in the decision-making process, she might serve in a supporting role to provide advice, encouragement, information, and resources to followers in their own decision-making. We will return to these styles in Chapter 5 as we consider the particular contexts or situations in which each is most beneficial.

## Leading With Love

If we assume that relations-oriented leadership is truly important, and perhaps traditionally undervalued by people in leadership roles, then the question becomes exactly how can one be more relations-oriented as a leader? One philosophy that has been put forth in recent times is the notion of leading with love.[26] In a metaphoric sort of way, **leading with love** can be likened to relations-oriented leadership 'on steroids' in order to show compassion, make work fun, and reduce fear in the workplace. When leading with love, relations are developed with followers by showing deep understanding, compassion, and concern for the individual. Leading with love involves acknowledging the value of others and not assuming negative motives on their part.

> **leading with love**
> Relations-oriented leadership "on steroids" in order to show compassion, make work fun, and reduce fear in the workplace.

The COVID-19 crisis that began in 2020 made the demonstration of compassion on the part of leaders even more relevant. Employees were faced with all sorts of new stressors in their lives, such as the homeschooling of children or actually catching the virus—themselves or significant others. Such circumstances made the need for leading with love even more relevant.

Another aspect of leading with love is attempting to instill fun and excitement into work. The expression “hump day” refers to Wednesdays. The idea is that the work week is halfway done, and at the end of the work day on Friday, fun can begin. The implicit notion is that work is work, and fun is fun, but the two will not cross paths. Leading with love involves breaking down such stereotypes about work vs. fun. The philosophy of leading with love is that the line between work and play should be blurred. Steve Denning is the Chairman of General Atlantic Partners, a $20 billion private equity firm. He says that “success without fun isn’t really success, and fun without success isn’t really fun.”[27] You need both.

To better understand leading with love, it is informative to think about its opposite—leading with fear, anxiety, and negative emotions. Leading with fear has either overtly or in a more passive way constituted the norm of influence practices in the past. Fear in organizations manifests itself in many ways, such as fear of failing to perform, fear of personal loss, fear of being judged, and so forth.

Why would one lead through fear, versus through leading with love? The answer to this question can be found in what has become known as Theory X versus Theory Y assumptions.[28] Leaders with Theory X assumptions believe that people tend to dislike work and want to avoid it. Accordingly, in order to get work accomplished, followers (or subordinates) need to be tightly controlled, which would include a sense of fear that if they perform or behave poorly, they will be strictly punished. On the other hand, leaders with Theory Y assumptions believe that the average person does not dislike work, will accept and even seek out more responsibility, and can be entrusted with setting their own direction or maintaining self-control. It should be apparent that a leader with a strong Theory X philosophy would be likely to lead through fear, while a leader with a strong Theory Y philosophy would be more prone to leading with love.

What are the likely outcomes of leading with love vs. fear? When one leads through fear, short-term production goals might be achieved, but in the long run, followers will be very hesitant to try new things or to innovate. As such, they will be very risk-averse and perform only up to minimal expectations. On the other hand, the likely results of leading with love include trust and commitment, emotional engagement, innovation, and sustained customer service.

Despite the likely benefits of leading with love, there are certainly skeptics and naysayers who might suggest that it reflects a “pie in the sky” mentality that does not take into account the reality of a workforce that oftentimes is not ready to be led with love. Such thinking would suggest that leading with love simply reflects what might be termed “unconditional positive regard.” But in reality, this is far from the truth. For example, leading with love does not imply that a leader would never disagree or provide critique to a follower. However, when he does so, it is not for the purpose of ridiculing or proving the follower to be wrong. Instead, the goal is to help the follower to grow, learn, and succeed.

### 2.3 Getting to the Roots: Neurological Underpinnings of Leader Traits and Behaviors

To a large extent, the brain is a primary source for leadership traits and behaviors. In other words, the brain underpins cognition and emotions, as well as the behaviors that leaders tend to show. Researchers, therapists, and physicians commonly measure physiological phenomena in the brain in terms of changes in blood flow and electrical activity. This activity occurs in one of two ways. First, the brain reacts physiologically to stimuli in our environments. For example, if a tiger were to walk into your immediate proximity, your brain would immediately engage in changes in blood flow and electrical activity that are related to fear and flight. Second, when your brain is in an alert but resting state (i.e., not being subjected to stimuli), it has what is known as a distinct, intrinsic electrical structure or pattern. Like height, weight, personality, and so forth, each of us have unique, intrinsic structures that can be assessed through what is known as quantitative electroencephalogram (qEEG).[29] Such neural structures differ between individuals, and they are relatively stable over time. However, in line with the concept of brain plasticity, these structures can potentially change over time due to life experiences, traumatic injury, and even systematic training.

In recent years, research has shown that intrinsic structures in the brain are associated with various aspects of effective leadership, such as visionary, ethical/moral, non-abusive, and complex reasoning forms of leadership.[30] In other words, we differ on those intrinsic structures, and these differences are associated with whether or not we will tend to be effective as leaders. What is not as yet clear are the practical implications of such research. One possibility is that a technique like neurofeedback could be used to help develop the potential for better leadership behavior. Through neurofeedback, the brain might be trained to be more conducive to effective leader cognition, emotions, and behavior.[31] While not a “magic bullet” for prospective leaders, perhaps neurofeedback might be paired with other leader development approaches, such as the 360-degree feedback processes considered in Chapter 7 of this book. Another possibility might be to actually use neurologically-based data to help form the basis for the selection or promotion of individuals into leadership positions. Indeed, the business world and media are starting to take note of these potential implications of neuroscience.[32]

Yet both of these potential implications raise ethical issues (e.g., privacy) that will most likely come into play in future years as this research moves forward. For example, is it ethical to change the electrical brain structures of individuals in an attempt to make them better leaders? Who says when and how brain-based data might be used to help select and promote leaders? Despite such difficult questions, one thing is for sure: the brain represents a new frontier for both understanding and developing leadership potential.
