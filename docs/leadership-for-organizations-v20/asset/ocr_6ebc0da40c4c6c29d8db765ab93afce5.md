# ## 13.3 The Role of the Leader in Change

Figure 13.1 provides a simple framework illustrating how these two types of change can interact. Typically, the most radical change involves proactive, discontinuous actions on the part of leaders and their firms. In other words, the change is fundamentally transformative. Given that people are largely leery of change, transformative change can be especially challenging for leaders. In Chapter 1, we made a fundamental distinction between _management_ and _leadership_, noting that managers work within existing structures to make systems operate effectively and deliver results, while leaders are responsible for setting a vision and helping the organization adapt to change. Based on Figure 13.1, a different way of saying this is that managers are most often responsible for incremental or reactive change, while true leaders are more often concerned with transformation and discontinuous change. While incremental change is important, it typically can be achieved through careful planning, measurement and feedback. On the other hand, discontinuous (both transformation and re-orientation) change is riskier, more important, and requires more involvement on the part of leaders.

For example, think about the leadership challenges associated with transforming a large organization like IBM. That firm began as a manufacturer of mechanical tabulating equipment, moved into computers and software (same customers but a completely different technology), leveraged these assets and capabilities to become a company that also provides business solutions and consulting, and is now trying to become a provider of artificial intelligence solutions. Each of these shifts required leaders to dramatically change how they did business and where they allocated their resources; in other words, major transformative change. In contrast, think about how the leaders of companies like Kodak or Polaroid who, when confronted with disruptive changes in their core business of film and instant photography, failed to help their organizations make the changes needed. What is it that differentiates the leaders of successful versus unsuccessful organizational change efforts?

To a large extent, we will attempt to answer that question in the remainder of this chapter. Undoubtedly, in a company as large as IBM or the former Kodak, one person alone cannot be responsible for the success or failure of the entire change process. However, people in leadership positions can play an especially large role. Let us take one of the larger-than-life icons of recent history in the business world, Steve Jobs, one of the founders of Apple. Jobs is known for many acts of leading and effecting change throughout a career that spanned approximately three decades. But one not-so-well known act was the use of a mouse on personal computers. Jobs came across the possibility of how a mouse could revolutionize personal computing around 1980 in the early stages of Macintosh development.[5] He and some colleagues at Apple witnessed a demonstration at a Xerox research and development laboratory (PARC). It featured an early prototype of a mouse on a computer that was originally meant to be for graphic design engineers.

In the middle of the demonstration, Jobs got up and started dancing around the room. Obviously, some people who were present thought that he was crazy, or at a minimum, a very strange individual. As his career went on, perhaps both opinions bore out some truth. Indeed, as noted earlier in this book, true leaders are oftentimes viewed as strange, different, or just “out there.” But what had happened in that particular moment is that unlike others who were present, Jobs was suddenly able to envision how this very early prototype could eventually become part of literally billions of personal computers (not just those associated with Apple) that would change billions of lives.
