However, such avoidance behavior rarely works, and the conflict might spread to other team members, even to the point of forming separate factions within a team.

In the *norming* stage, the leader attempts to apply clarity to roles, norms, and values of the team. For example, the leader may help to clarify the different obligations and team needs that various members can help to satisfy. As a further example, the leader might attempt to stress the norm of showing up on time to meetings (i.e., either face-to-face or virtual).

Finally, in the *performing* stage, the leader works to help build the team's identity, especially if the team will be relatively ongoing over time. For example, team members might be encouraged to wear T-shirts to work (at least on certain days) displaying the team's logo or perhaps a nickname. Perhaps even more importantly, when a team is in the performing stage, it is important for the leader to monitor the team's environment for any changes that are occurring that could affect the team's goal or mission. If changes are needed, it is important for the leader to introduce new ideas or vision.

# When Leadership Is Lacking

Figure 8.1 also shows what can happen if leadership is lacking at any of the four stages of team development. In the *forming* stage, a lack of leadership could cause confusion and a lack of common direction or norms for the team. When the team is *storming*, a lack of leadership could engender distrust and the type of negative judgments and attributions between team members that was considered in Chapter 7 of this book. The communication of such judgments and attributions can cause conflict to be highly destructive, rather than fostering the constructive solution of problems. For example, if a leader observes a team member accusing another member as being lazy or irrational, the leader can insist that such accusations not be part of the team discourse, and instead, emphasize dialogue whereby specific problematic behaviors are considered and discussed in a respectful manner. If roles, norms, and values are not clarified by the leader in the *norming* stage, there could be a lack of teamwork toward common goals, and the team might even drift back to the *storming* stage.

Finally, if leadership is lacking in the *performing* stage, the team's goals and mission could become outdated in relation to the needs of its environment (e.g., customers). It is interesting that often if a team is clearly performing on all cylinders, so to speak, it can be tempting for a leader to take a hands-off or laissez-faire approach. The leader may go into more of a managerial or administrative mode, but not show the type of leadership that has been described in this chapter and elsewhere in the book. Unfortunately, this is a key reason why so many teams start to engage in groupthink, which so often occurs in the performing stage, and is likely to inevitably damage a team's viability.
