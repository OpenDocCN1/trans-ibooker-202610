+   2. Need for affiliation. People with a strong need for achievement desire many close relationships and friendships, while wanting to avoid conflict. At first glance, it might seem like leaders need to have such strong needs. However, a strong need for affiliation could cause a leader to want to be close friends with followers, while avoiding tough decisions (e.g., reprimand or even dismissal of an employee) that may need to be made. Bob Sutton, a professor and author of the book The No Asshole Rule, captures this issue when he notes that "From a performance standpoint, liking the people you manage too much is a bigger problem than liking them too little."[12] Similarly, Spencer Rascoff, the CEO of Zillow (an online real estate company) has said that "I like to be liked, but sometimes managing people is not a popularity contest."[13] Accordingly, while leaders should certainly not seem aloof or unfriendly, a more moderate need for affiliation is typically best.

+   3. Need for power. This need involves a desire to influence or control others. Again, at first glance, it might seem like the most effective leaders have either a low, or at most moderate, need for power. But in reality, some of the best leaders have a strong need for power. The key to understanding this idea is to first realize that power can be either personalized, or alternatively, it can be socialized. Personalized power is all about the leader and his or her personal interests. So when using personalized power, the leader attempts to gain resources or manipulate situations for his or her own gain. Eventually followers will see through a leader with a personalized power motive, and they will lack trust in the leader and be uninspired by him or her. Kent Thiry, the CEO of DaVita, makes this point by observing that "Intentionality rules. People can smell your intentionality—whether your relationship is about a means for advancing your career or an end in and of itself."[14] In contrast, socialized power pertains to the garnering of power so that others can benefit. As an example, Martin Luther King used his power with various groups (e.g., government officials) in order to benefit his many constituents. Likewise, in the business world, leaders like Herb Kelleher, founder of Southwest Airlines, are notorious for how they use their power in order to benefit employees. In short, a strong need for socialized power is a good combination for leaders.

In sum, the best profile for leaders is likely to be moderate need for achievement, moderate need for affiliation, and strong need for socialized power. An effective leader should have a moderate need to achieve, but if taken too far, that need may ignore or overshadow similar needs of followers. Further, an effective leader needs to be a "people-person," but not get too close to those who he or she is leading. Finally, contrary to some people's intuition, effective leaders do have a strong need for power. However, they are able to channel that need toward using power to help and benefit others.

# Character

character
Moral excellence and firmness on the part of leaders.

Leaders need to have character. That statement may seem obvious, but what does it really mean? The dictionary defines character as moral excellence and firmness.[15] We will address ethical and moral leadership in more detail in Chapter 6. But for now, suffice it to say that there are several key characteristics that are associated with character on the part of leaders.

First, there is a good chance that the leader who you considered at the opening of this chapter displayed integrity, which is why you thought of him or her as a leader. Indeed, integrity is often times one of the first characteristics that people consider when they hear the term "leader." One interesting thing about integrity is that it involves both commission and omission. Commission is probably what we first consider when evaluating the integrity of an individual. For example, we think of words spoken, and whether those words match actions that are taken. When they do match, we say that the person is "walking the talk," and we are likely to conclude that the individual has integrity.
