The media has also criticized Crow for attempting to make ASU operate like a business corporation, although Crow might argue that this was necessary because of budgetary demands. Some additional controversial aspects are portrayed in Section 5.

*First*, generally when there is talk or writing about the new vision at ASU, it is seen as Crow's vision, rather than a common, shared vision that he has tried to form with existing ASU faculty and administrators. To be fair, many people have bought into his vision. But for the most part, Crow's strategy for vision formation and implementation has not been to try to co-create or build shared vision, or a guiding coalition of support, among existing personnel. Rather, he has brought in personnel (e.g., mid to high-level administrators) who largely “manage” their respective units in a top-down manner, rather than “lead” in a more collaborative manner (i.e., both within units and across units). For example, although Crow has met with existing faculty members, lower-level administrators, and students to share his vision, it is not as clear that their own aspirations for the future of ASU, or doubts regarding Crow's vision and its implementation, have truly been taken into account.

*Second,* Crow's relationship with students has been at best, shaky over the years. His early push for steadily increasing tuition may have had merit, but it has understandably caused some friction between himself and students. As another example, it seems pretty apparent that Crow has been determined to get rid of ASU's image as a party school. One action taken along those lines is to reduce the fraternity/sorority (i.e. “Greek”) presence on campus. Some students have argued that Crow's statements and actions toward Greek life have fostered a somewhat hostile climate toward students who maintain a Greek affiliation.[28] On the other hand, it could be argued that some actions on the part of Greek-affiliated students have warranted criticism, such as racially-distasteful behaviors at a Martin Luther King, Jr. theme party that was held by the Tau Kappa Epsilon fraternity in January of 2014. As a result of those behaviors, Crow directed the university to permanently sever ties with that fraternity.[29]

*Third,* Crow has shown a tendency to not share information about major, forthcoming changes until they actually have been planned out and formally announced to the media. Examples include the potential spinning off of the West campus to form a separate university in 2004, the Starbucks initiative, and the more recent acquisition of the Thunderbird School of Global Management. In each of these instances, press releases of the pending changes came before actual discussions with, and involvement of, faculty and students.

Crow might argue that information was only provided to individuals on a “need to know” basis, although in line with principles from Chapter 6, some people might question such thinking. To be fair, a counter-argument to early disclosure of these initiatives could be made. Specifically, such disclosure might have only led to extensive rumors, inaccurate media coverage, and political maneuvering. But at the same time, the lack of early communication to, and involvement of, people who must ultimately implement change (e.g., faculty and lower-level administrators) can leave them with feelings of distrust or a lack of empowerment to act. In such a case, changes might not be implemented as efficiently or timely as might otherwise occur.

*Fourth,* once a significant organizational or structural change is announced, Crow has shown a tendency to simply delegate the implementation to lower level administrators. This style has contributed to Crow being perceived as a leader who operates only at “30,000 feet.” While such delegation can make sense given the time demands of his job, it may also make sense to become personally involved at least to some degree in implementation efforts. This type of involvement would help to ensure that the implementation coincides with his vision of the university.

The continuing pervasiveness of bureaucracy at ASU is a case in point. Deans and other lower-level administrators could have taken more steps over the past twenty years to reduce the bureaucracy, but there is little apparent effort to lower the level of bureaucratic impediments for both students and faculty.
