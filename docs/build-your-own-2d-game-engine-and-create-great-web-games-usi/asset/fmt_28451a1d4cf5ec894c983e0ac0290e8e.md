
Notice the negative sign in front of the ![$$ \hat{\mathrm{N}} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq10.png) component. You can see in Figure [9-25](#334805_2_En_9_Chapter.xhtml#Fig25) that the ![$$ \hat{\mathrm{N}} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq11.png) component for vector ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_2 $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq12.png) points in the opposite direction to that of ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_1 $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq13.png) as a result of the collision. Additionally, notice that in the tangent direction ![$$ \hat{\mathrm{T}} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq14.png), ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_2 $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq15.png) continues to point in the same direction. This is because the tangent component is parallel to the wall and is unaffected by the collision. This analysis is true in general for any collisions in a perfect world with no friction and no loss of kinetic energy.

### Relative Velocity of Colliding Shapes

The decomposition of vectors into the normal and tangent directions of the collision can also be applied to the general case of when both of the colliding shapes are in motion. For example, Figure [9-26](#334805_2_En_9_Chapter.xhtml#Fig26) illustrates two traveling circle shapes, A and B, coming into a collision.

![Figure 9-26 ¶ Collision between two traveling circles](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Fig26_HTML.png)

In the case of Figure [9-26](#334805_2_En_9_Chapter.xhtml#Fig26), before the collision, object A is traveling with velocity ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}1} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq16.png), while object B with velocity ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{B}1} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq17.png). The normal direction of the collision, ![$$ \hat{\mathrm{N}} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq18.png), is defined to be the vector between the two circle centers, and the tangent direction of the collision, ![$$ \hat{\mathrm{T}} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq19.png), is the vector that is tangential to both of the circles at the point of collision. To resolve this collision, the velocities for objects A and B after the collision, ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}2} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq20.png) and ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{B}2} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq21.png), must be computed.

The post-collision velocities are determined based on the relative velocity between the two shapes. The relative velocity between shapes A and B is defined as follows:

*   ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{AB}1}={\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}1}-{\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{B}1} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq22.png)

The collision vector decomposition can now be applied to the normal and tangent directions of the relative velocity where the relative velocity after the collision is ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{AB}2} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq23.png):

*   ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{AB}2}\bullet \hat{\mathrm{N}}=-\mathrm{e}\left({\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{AB}1}\bullet \hat{\mathrm{N}}\right) $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq24.png) **(1)**
*   ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{AB}2}\bullet \hat{\mathrm{T}}=\mathrm{f}\left({\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{AB}1}\bullet \hat{\mathrm{T}}\right) $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq25.png) **(2)**

The restitution, e, and friction, f, coefficients model the real-world situation where some kinetic energy is changed to some other forms of energy during the collision. The negative sign of Equation (1) signifies that after the collision, objects will travel in the direction that is opposite to the initial collision normal direction. Equation (2) says that after the collision, friction will scale back the magnitude where objects will continue to travel in the same tangent direction only at a lower velocity. Notice that all variables on the right-hand side of Equations (1) and (2) are defined, as they are known at the time of collision. It is important to remember that

*   ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{AB}2}={\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}2}-{\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{B}2} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq26.png)

where the goal is to derive a solution for ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}2} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq27.png) and ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{B}2} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq28.png), the individual velocities of the colliding objects after a collision. You are now ready to model a solution to approximate ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}2} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq29.png) and ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{B}2} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq30.png).

**Note**

The restitution coefficient, e, describes bounciness or the proportion of the velocity that is retained after a collision. A restitution value of 1.0 would mean that speeds will be the same from before and after a collision. In contrast, friction is intuitively associated with the proportion lost or the slow down after a collision. For example, a friction coefficient of 1.0 would mean infinite friction where a velocity of zero will result from a collision. For consistency of the formulae, the coefficient f in Equation (2) is actually 1 minus the intuitive friction coefficient.

### The Impulse

Accurately describing a collision involves complex considerations including factors like energy-changing form, or frictions resulting from different material properties, etc. Without considering these advanced issues, a simplistic description of a collision that occurs on a shape is a constant mass object changing its velocity from ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}1} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq31.png) to ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}2} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq32.png) after contacting with another object. Conveniently, this is the definition of an impulse, as can be seen in the following:

*   ![$$ \overrightarrow{\mathrm{J}}={\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{A}}{\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}2}-{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{A}}{\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}1} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq33.png)

or when solving for ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}2} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq34.png)

*   ![$$ {\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}2}={\overrightarrow{\mathrm{V}}}_{\mathrm{A}1}+\frac{\overrightarrow{\mathrm{J}}}{{\mathrm{m}}_{\mathrm{A}}} $$](images/334805_2_En_9_Chapter/334805_2_En_9_Chapter_TeX_IEq35.png) **(3)**
