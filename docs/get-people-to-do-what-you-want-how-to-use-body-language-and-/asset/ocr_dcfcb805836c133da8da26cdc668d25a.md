One day in Kuwait, he was having a conversation with an American soldier, and in response to a question, Greg made a ticking noise with his mouth. “Did you just tick at me?” the soldier asked. It was a piece of nonverbal communication that Greg had picked up from his Arab associates.

Interrogators have only a “secret” clearance, as opposed to the higher “top secret” clearance, because they have a job requirement to understand the enemy's point of view. Interrogators talk with the enemy every day, for hours at a time. Greg knows this intimately because he interrogated many prisoners of war in their own language. By nature, interrogators have to be empathetic in order to get enemy combatants to talk, so they start feeling what the prisoners feel. They start to smell, talk, sound, and eat like the people they live with—the prisoners—because they mirror them, and there is a real danger of getting too close. It's possible to get drawn into a vortex and not be able to get out.

In everyday life, enculturation generally doesn't involve an enemy, but rather someone who is friendly yet represents something “foreign” or “strange.” For example, Greg currently works with an executive vice president who is Finnish. In one of his first meetings with him, Greg noticed that he had Japanese mannerisms. Greg asked, “Was he raised in Japan?” The vice president's colleague responded, “How do you know that?” The man's family had relocated to Japan for a period of time when he was young. The years he spent immersed in Japanese culture had affected the way he expressed himself.
