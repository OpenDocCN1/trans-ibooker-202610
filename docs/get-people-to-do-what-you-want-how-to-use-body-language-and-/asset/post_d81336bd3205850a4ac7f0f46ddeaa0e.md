
The second scenario is more complicated. It's the one that field operatives use to recruit agents, so you can look at the strategy in both a positive and a negative light. First, the negative.

Why do young people in the US military commonly become supporters of the Palestinian cause after they study Arabic at the Defense Language Institute? They are bombarded day in and day out with language and culture studies that differentiate them from other Americans who cannot understand Arabic and have no exposure to Arab rituals and traditions. It's natural to be drawn to a body of information, and even a population, when you know more about them than anyone else. You identify with them. It's a phenomenon that has played out time after time with CIA agents, such as former CIA officer Philip Agee defecting to Cuba on moral grounds. If you get too close to the enemy, you start to sympathize.

This is very different from Stockholm syndrome, in which someone who was kidnapped or taken hostage begins to sympathize with the captor. This is a phenomenon of enculturation. *Lawrence of Arabia* is a largely true story about the battlefield enculturation of British officer T. E. Lawrence. He became so entrenched in the priorities and values of Arabs rising up against the Ottomans that he held a central role in the Arab Revolt, provided advice on military strategy and became a vital liaison with the British stationed in the region. Even after the Turks put a high price on his head, no Arab would betray him because the Sharif of Mecca had bestowed him the status of “son.”

Greg's fluency in Arabic also brought him into close, daily contact with Arab cultures and habits, but his experience did not involve a “conversion” of any kind. It is possible to adopt mannerisms and habits of people whom you are with every day and still not absorb their values.

One day in Kuwait, he was having a conversation with an American soldier, and in response to a question, Greg made a ticking noise with his mouth. “Did you just tick at me?” the soldier asked. It was a piece of nonverbal communication that Greg had picked up from his Arab associates.

Interrogators have only a “secret” clearance, as opposed to the higher “top secret” clearance, because they have a job requirement to understand the enemy's point of view. Interrogators talk with the enemy every day, for hours at a time. Greg knows this intimately because he interrogated many prisoners of war in their own language. By nature, interrogators have to be empathetic in order to get enemy combatants to talk, so they start feeling what the prisoners feel. They start to smell, talk, sound, and eat like the people they live with—the prisoners—because they mirror them, and there is a real danger of getting too close. It's possible to get drawn into a vortex and not be able to get out.

In everyday life, enculturation generally doesn't involve an enemy, but rather someone who is friendly yet represents something “foreign” or “strange.” For example, Greg currently works with an executive vice president who is Finnish. In one of his first meetings with him, Greg noticed that he had Japanese mannerisms. Greg asked, “Was he raised in Japan?” The vice president's colleague responded, “How do you know that?” The man's family had relocated to Japan for a period of time when he was young. The years he spent immersed in Japanese culture had affected the way he expressed himself.

To varying degrees, a version of this plays out in businesses all the time. The most common is clever ad campaigns, which convince consumers to join the millions who would never use any deodorant but yours. Or you invite someone who does not want to make a deal with your company to do something with your group that provides a reason to bond. In the mid-to-late 1970s, this is exactly how Bergdorf Goodman CEO Ira Neimark wooed a string of top Italian designers to his store—on his terms. After staging extravagant shows for some designers, others wanted the leverage to have their designs featured in a similarly high-profile way. But they couldn't do it until they joined the Bergdorf “family,” which required exclusive relationships. They agreed and played a key role in Neimark's grand plan to put Bergdorf Goodman at the top of luxury retailing.

Regardless of the impetus, and regardless of whether you are bonding with a friend or an enemy, one fact reigns: The closer you bond, the harder it is to refuse to give someone what he wants. The more sensible the request, the more likely it will be granted.

These students at DLI sympathize with the Palestinians without a single request from Arabs to become sympathizers. The genesis of the bond is that their native Arabic speaking teachers have become friends, role models, mentors—and occupy super-typical status in the students' world.

In some cases, bonding with a group can also represent differentiation of some type. For example, the Arabic professor thinks Gary is the best student he has seen in years, and Gary truly understands his people, so Gary has huge incentives to move down the path to bonding with people who ostensibly have nothing in common with him in terms of culture, religion, or ethnicity.

The flip side of this means deliberately not bonding to a group, and in a business setting, that can be an equally effective way of getting people to do what you want. Greg uses this strategy in his current role as an executive effecting organizational change. He is an outsider at all times; he has to be. He has a mandate to make changes, and by nature, people who feel an affinity for a system or process are averse to changing it. If they saw him as an insider, changes would position him as a traitor. But because he maintains the posture and persona of an outsider, he can cause change without it being perceived as a personal attack.

## Peer-Group Manipulation

The DLI students who become supporters of the Palestinian cause arrive at that state on their own, but a journey like that results from deliberate action when you manage the process of making a person feel as though he belongs by becoming part of your group. You know that the affectation of belonging rests totally with you. But as soon as you pull away, then he has no group to which he belongs. You see high school kids do this unintentionally when they decide to hang around with the unpopular geek. The geek gets isolated from his own peer group by virtue of association with the “cool kids,” and he might even do favors for them unsolicited. But when that latter group gets tired of him or simply moves on, he's left alone after making the ultimate sacrifice: his former peer group.

If you don't feel as though you belong somewhere, somehow—if you don't attach to at least some of the people around you—you suffer from a crippling estrangement from civilized society. Gangs and jihadists prey on people in this situation, creating bonds that make people feel valued and necessary. As the process of bonding to the group draws them closer to its members, any remaining ties they have to family, schoolmates, and others in their day-to-day world are frayed until they snap. When the bonding is cemented and fracturing is complete, we can see the emergence of people who become perpetrators of terrorist attacks around the world.

With jihadists, there are elements of the bonding process that are treasured and vital parts of normal Muslim life. Similar to how Hitler used valued cultural rituals to create belonging, jihadists take the noble and usurp or pervert it. The connection that a young man or woman might feel to the jihadists is therefore ennobled by rituals and beliefs that are shared by at least 1.5 billion people. A good example is the salat, the obligatory Muslim prayers performed five times each day. This devotion sets the rhythm for a devout Muslim's daily life, and both spiritually and energetically reinforces the primary attachments in the person's life. For 1,400 years, the prayer ritual has linked Muslims around the world on a daily basis.

The shift toward radical behavior is not a small step; it is a journey. Like Charles Manson, the leaders reward what they want and punish what they do not want. If a sense of devotion can be maintained while someone is mentored and incentivized to go toward it, however, then the person being radicalized can continue to feel as though the effort—no matter how much violence it entails—allows her to maintain spiritual integrity.

Thomas Hegghammer, author of *Jihadi Culture: The Art and Social Practices of Militant Islamists*, explains that the militancy of brutal killers like Mohammed Emwaze (Jihadi John) “is about more than bombs and doctrines. It is also about rituals, customs, and dress codes. It is about music, films, and storytelling. It is about sports, jokes, and food.”¹⁴ He goes on to talk about their culture as a romantic one, “insofar as they see themselves as historical heroes, knights in shining armour, every one of them.”¹⁵
