
- Weaving the network by acting as liaisons between leaders, project teams, and the full network, including being available to hear and consider ideas and concerns raised by participants
- Representing the network in public settings
- Supporting the selection and onboarding of new participants
- Advancing efforts to resource the network

## Project Teams

Project teams (sometimes called *working groups, task forces,* and *action circles*) are organized by participants to advance a body of work related to the network’s purpose. When it comes to forming project teams, what participants think should happen is less important than what they will do. As Marvin Weisbord and Sandra Janoff advise, set priorities “based on willing actors rather than good ideas.”4

Early on in a network’s formation, project teams are most often focused internally to build the network itself. Internally focused project teams might be tasked with determining decision-making processes, securing funding, clarifying communications strategies, or developing an asset map. As the network evolves, project teams increasingly focus externally, advancing work in the community or broader system.

Project teams aim to break down the complexity that the network is trying to address into discrete, short-term tasks that can be deliberately planned and advanced. Accordingly, they are the perfect place to flex project management muscles. To balance deliberate and emergent strategy, project teams can develop plans of action only from one convening to the next, or over the course of a few months, whichever is shorter. Then, when they’ve completed their task, they can assess what they’ve learned, report their progress back to the network, and plan out the next phase of work, if necessary. This cyclical process allows project teams to assign responsibilities and make consistent progress, while also staying emergent and open to input and support from the network.

## Learning Circles

Learning circles form to combine knowledge, gather information, and generate learning on a specific topic of interest. They might aim to conduct research that can help the network identify critical points of leverage or simply foster conversation without a specific end goal in mind. For example, Sterling Network NYC has organized learning circles to support participants through professional transitions, to think creatively about how to reimagine and strengthen organized labor, and to engage in intimate conversations about race and identity using questions developed by the People’s Supper.⁵

Learning circles usually exist only for short time periods, disappearing after they share what they have learned with the rest of the network. In some cases, learning circles evolve into project teams as they shift from gathering information to taking action.

## Leading Network Teams

Like the network as a whole, teams benefit greatly from good leadership. Core teams are typically led by one of the network’s primary coordinators as part of their normal responsibilities, while project teams and learning circles are typically led by participants who step up into a leadership role.

Ideal team leaders are those who have a strong interest or stake in the outcome of the team. When self-interest and shared interest are aligned, leaders are better able to commit the time necessary to help move the work forward. Team leaders should not be expected to singlehandedly do the work of the group. Rather, they are facilitative leaders who support the team to advance its purpose. Following are a list of questions to consider when forming a new team, along with key responsibilities of team leaders.

### Questions to form a new team
Anytime a project team or learning circle is formed, it may begin by answering the following questions:

- What is the purpose of this team?
- Who is involved?
- Who will lead this team at least until the next convening?
- What tasks are required between now and the next convening?
- Who is taking responsibility for each task, and by when?
- What do we need from the network, if anything?

### Team leadership responsibilities
Those who step up to lead a project team or learning circle may provide the following types of support:

- Scheduling and facilitating conference calls or in-person meetings as needed
- Project management, including ensuring that each task is assigned to a specific person and with a specific due date
- Keeping network leaders and the core team updated with the team’s progress
- Reporting progress back to the network, sharing team needs and learnings, and providing opportunities for other participants to contribute

## Bounding Participation

Engaged participants are the lifeblood of networks. When participants are engaged, they are showing up, saying yes, and contributing to the network and its purpose.

Over the course of a network’s development, it is normal and expected that participants’ available time, energy, and attention will rise and fall with changing circumstances. Meanwhile, some new people may join the network, and others will choose to leave it entirely. By offering different ways to plug in, networks provide people with the flexibility to decide how and where they would like to participate.

We have found it useful to offer four primary levels of engagement to choose from: lead, partner, follow, and honor. These levels of participation can apply to the network as a whole, as well as to specific project teams:

- **Lead:** “I will take responsibility for making this network/team happen.”
- **Partner:** “I will actively work with the leaders to make this network/team happen.”
- **Follow:** “I would like to stay informed about how this network/team is progressing.”
- **Honor:** “Although I appreciate what you are proposing, this network/team is not of particular interest to me at this time.”

When a network is just getting started, we ask participants to share their preferred level of engagement with the network as a whole, to get a better sense of who would like to attend future convenings and be kept in the loop with network communications. *Leaders* are those who would like to be particularly engaged in the work of cultivating the network—for instance, by helping to coordinate the network, participating on the core team, or leading a project team. Network *partners* are actively engaged in the work of the network and committed to attending convenings and participating in network activities. *Followers* want to be kept in the loop but cannot be actively engaged at the present time. People who choose *honor* are respectfully declining to participate and are therefore removed from network communications. As the network evolves, we ask participants to clarify their intended level of engagement annually to help leaders with planning, coordination, and budgeting.

Be sure not to overlook the followers. Anyone who has seen Derek Sivers’s YouTube video “First Follower: Leadership Lessons from Dancing Guy” knows the power that followers have to inspire and advance the work of others. “If the leader is the flint,” he says, “the follower is the spark that really makes the fire.”⁶ Followers should be kept in the loop with updates on future opportunities to engage with and support the work of the network.

Over time, they may shift to become partners or leaders as their interests and capacity change—it's common for people to move between different levels of engagement over the course of a network's life cycle.

One of the responsibilities of network leaders is to provide many different opportunities for leadership, partnership, and followership to emerge, and to allow people to decline altogether. Trust that participants will self-organize to place their energy where they are most inspired to contribute and where they are most needed.

## Establishing Participation Agreements

Network participation is not a one-size-fits-all proposition. Participation in networks may be open (anyone can join), closed (participants agree to fulfill certain criteria to join and stay engaged), or tiered (the network provides multiple ways for participants to engage, each with distinct criteria and responsibilities). Networks will need to decide what participation agreements are necessary to maintain continuity as they evolve.

While it may be tempting to keep participation open to avoid complications around defining criteria and excluding some people, open participation comes with its own challenges. In The Art of Community, Charles Vogl identifies “clear boundaries” as one of the core principles of belonging. Vogl notes that for a strong community to develop, people often need to know that being a part of the community *means something* and that not just anyone can join in on a whim. In other words, boundaries are less about exclusion and more about fostering greater inclusion within the network.⁷

The Enspiral network learned this lesson the hard way. Enspiral is a community of more than 150 freelancers and activists located primarily in Europe and New Zealand who work collaboratively to create positive social impact through their ventures. At first, Enspiral was an open network, with few boundaries on participation. While this worked at first, over time the lack of boundaries created an unhealthy work environment when some people abused the high-trust atmosphere.
