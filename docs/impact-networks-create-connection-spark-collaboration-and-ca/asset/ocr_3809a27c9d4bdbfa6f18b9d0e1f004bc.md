If network coordinators are relatively tech savvy, they can make themselves available to assist those who are not comfortable with a particular tool—walking through it in detail until people feel at ease. We also encourage matching up network members as tech buddies, connecting the people who have high comfort with a given tool with people who need to learn. This way, participants can reach out to one another for support, rather than requiring one person to be responsible for providing tech support to the entire network.

# Practicing Reciprocity

People join networks because they feel they can make a contribution and think the network can help them do something they can’t do on their own. But no matter how much they care about the issue at hand, the altruistic commitment of participants alone is rarely enough to sustain a network over time. More than likely, networks will need to serve the personal or organizational priorities of those involved to sustain their engagement over time. This reflects the dynamic tension of self-interest and shared interest.

Taking the time to deliberately coordinate actions is a great way to serve the self-interests of participants. You can do so by providing space on calls and at convenings for people to share urgent needs and to connect with others who might be able to support their work. We call this activity Rapid Coordination, and we incorporate it into nearly every network convening we lead. The activity begins by inviting participants, one by one, to briefly share something they need help with or a potential collaboration they’d like to explore with others. After the first person shares what they need, others are invited to raise their hands if they believe they can help (by sharing information, having a follow-up conversation, or providing resources). The process continues until everyone has been given a chance to make a request.
