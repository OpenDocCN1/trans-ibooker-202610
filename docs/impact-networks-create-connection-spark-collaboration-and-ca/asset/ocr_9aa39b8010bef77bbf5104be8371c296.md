Coordinators are a critical hub in the middle of a network, helping to tie everything together. One way to think about coordinators is as air traffic controllers. Air traffic controllers see and understand how all the planes in a given airspace are flowing. They don’t fly the planes, but they are trusted to help each aircraft get to its destination. They regularly communicate with the pilots to understand their plans and needs, and they use that information to help guide the whole system.

Similarly, coordinators have their finger on the pulse of nearly everything that’s happening across the network. They work to integrate many parts into a unified whole. Because of their unique position, coordinators are well equipped to identify and frame emerging issues, collect agenda topics, and support the design of convenings and calls. Embedding evaluation practices, such as those described in chapter 10, helps coordinators to track the network’s development and identify emerging needs and opportunities.

# Establish and Maintain Network Operations

Coordinators are instrumental in setting up and maintaining a network’s online systems (explained in more detail below), guiding logistics for convenings and other events, tracking the network’s finances, and managing evaluation activities. Though coordinators may not lead bookkeeping or evaluation, they make sure these tasks are covered by others who know what they’re doing, and they ensure that important information is passed on to participants.

# Provide Knowledge Management

Coordinators act as knowledge managers who gather resources and learnings from network projects, organizing the information so that it can be easily accessed later. They also curate and share this information with participants through newsletters and other means. Coordinators make sure that people have the information they need to advance network activities while building on the work that came before.
