If performed with enough consistency, this activity helps create a culture of generalized reciprocity. Whereas reciprocity is the practice of exchanging value with another person, generalized reciprocity is the practice of exchanging value with the network. People who practice generalized reciprocity give their gifts without the expectation of immediate return; they give with a sense of trust that their actions will eventually be reciprocated in one form or another by others in the network, although it isn’t clear how or when.

When generalized reciprocity becomes a consistent, network-wide practice, participants give and receive each other’s gifts with generous intent. In turn, this creates a virtuous cycle: giving gifts builds trust, and trust facilitates the giving of gifts. The practice also creates a greater sense of belonging among participants. We need to give our gifts in order to feel like we’re contributing to something greater than ourselves. Having consistent opportunities to share and receive each other’s gifts is a big part of why people will continue to show up time and time again.

# Responding to Crisis

With a culture of reciprocity, along with a robust communication infrastructure, networks are able to effectively respond to chaotic situations. As described in the introduction, chaotic situations are turbulent and rapidly evolving, and they cannot be accurately predicted or controlled. When one is faced with a moment of crisis, the first necessary step is to provide an emergency response—to quickly coordinate actions and distribute resources to where they are needed most. This is something that networks are uniquely positioned to do.
