In hierarchical systems, decisions are typically made by those at the top and instructions for implementation are passed down. Not only does this structure minimize input, but it normalizes power imbalances that eliminate the potential for people to make decisions about things that directly affect their lives. Simply stated, “Power is participation in decision-making,” writes Cyndi Suarez in *The Power Manual.*¹⁰

In impact networks, decision-making—like leadership—is distributed. This does not mean that everyone will agree with every decision—in many cases, that simply isn’t realistic. It does mean, however, that all participants are invited to shape proposals and share perspectives, while demonstrating a genuine consideration of each other’s circumstances, benefits, and burdens. As long as decision-making is inclusive, transparent, and intended to serve the well-being of the whole, people are usually able to live with a decision, even if they disagree—and they are grateful for a process that results in forward motion.

In situations where the network needs to make a collective decision, such as defining its participation agreements, achieving full consensus demonstrates coherence within the system. Yet consensus decision-making has major limitations—it can be slow, it can drain the network’s energy when action is stalled, and it can restrict experimentation.

We prefer an alternative approach: consent-based decision-making, which allows actions in the best interest of the whole to proceed without unanimous agreement. This approach is rooted in the “range of tolerance” principle borrowed from ecosystem science: when organisms find themselves in contexts that are too hot or too cold, some parts of the system die because the temperature is out of their range of tolerance. With consent-based decision-making, we find a decision that everyone can live with, even if it’s not their top preference. As questions or objections arise, participants take the time to hear each other’s honest concerns, and they work together to refine the final proposal so that it lands within everyone’s range of tolerance.

## A Process for Consent-Based Decision-Making

The consent-based decision-making process has three phases: context, clarification, and call for consent:

**Context** frames the decision to be made: What are the relevant factors? What are the pros and cons that have been considered? What conversations have already taken place? And finally, what is the recommendation moving forward? Context is presented by those who took part in discussions to formulate the proposal.

**Clarification** allows for any further questions or clarity needed by network members prior to voting. Facilitators should help ensure that the conversation doesn’t veer toward evaluation of ideas but stays with clarity on what is being recommended. Participants are encouraged to consider whether proposals are consistent with the network’s purpose and principles, and to think in terms of what’s in the best interest of the network as a whole, as opposed to what’s in the best interest for “me” as an individual or as a representative of an organization or special interest.

**Call for consent** uses a 0 to 5 voting framework when the full group is ready to make a decision or assess the level of support and need for further discussion. First, the facilitator restates the proposal and asks participants to vote. Then, the participants respond with a number from 0 to 5 (using fingers, pen and paper, or other means) corresponding to their level of support. The process can also be completed digitally with polling tools if confidentiality is required, if voting with fingers or paper isn’t practical, or if detailed records need to be captured.

When voting, participants are invited to choose from one of the following options:

- **5—Lead:** The proposal serves our network well, and I would like to be one of the leaders in implementing it.
- **4—Partner:** The proposal serves our network well, and I will partner to support the leaders in implementing it.
- **3—Follow:** The proposal may serve our network, but I can’t offer any capacity toward implementation.
- **2—Concern:** I have concerns about the proposal and will be tracking as implementation moves forward.
- **1—Caution:** I have major concerns about the proposal creating harm and would like to discuss further.
- **0—Oppose:** The proposed course of action is outside a range of tolerance and requires changes before any action is taken.

The proposal goes forward as outlined if there are only 2s and above. If there are 0s or 1s, options include having real-time discussions to resolve objections or scheduling time for the 0s and 1s to meet with the 4s and 5s to resolve issues and find a way to move forward.

When we use consent-based decision-making, proposals are encouraged to proceed as long as they are consistent with the network’s purpose and principles and are unlikely to cause harm. Consequently, the process gives autonomy to those who volunteered to lead the implementation of an idea they’re excited about. This creates a bias toward action, with a responsibility to consider the impact of the whole. Consent thus encourages people across the network to step into leadership roles and pursue their varied, creative interests. This kind of diversity of action fosters a culture of experimentation and empowerment, and it makes the network a much more fun and innovative place to be.

## Embedding Evaluation

Evaluation is central to good network leadership. Any impact network will benefit from embedding evaluation practices, as the data collected will generate valuable learnings and help guide its evolution. Therefore, evaluation should be incorporated from the very start, not later after the network has begun to advance specific projects.¹¹

A good evaluation protocol provides regular assessments of the network’s health and functioning, including opportunities for participant feedback. This data helps network leaders highlight areas of strength and opportunities for improvement, guide collective decision-making, and inform the design of network activities and convenings. In addition, the data is likely to help make the case that the network is progressing, even before tangible project outcomes have been realized.

Given the emergent nature of networks, longer-term impacts often cannot be well defined at the beginning. In fact, determining long-term outcomes before participants have had a chance to think and work together is likely to stifle their ability to self-organize around what they think is most important. Therefore, efforts to assess long-term impacts require a thoughtful evaluation approach that allows for this flexibility over time; approaches that rely largely on predetermined and static metrics are generally not a good fit.

At the same time, healthy impact networks share a set of short-term and intermediate-term outcomes that are definable, including markers of network connectivity and demonstrated value for participants. These are important to document, in terms of actionable data to inform network development, as well as to demonstrate forward motion to funders and participants alike. Because the aspects of network health are relatively universal, they can be identified in advance and assessed with fairly standardized (yet customizable) tools. This is why we are able to offer the following structured evaluation approach, which will be useful for almost any impact network.
