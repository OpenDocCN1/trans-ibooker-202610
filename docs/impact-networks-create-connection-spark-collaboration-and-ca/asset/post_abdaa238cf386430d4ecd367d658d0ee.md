
## CHAPTER 6

## Convene the People

> The world doesn’t change one person at a time. It changes as networks of relationships form among people who discover they share a common cause and vision of what’s possible.

> —MARGARET WHEATLEY AND DEBORAH FRIEZE, “Using Emergence to Take Social Innovations to Scale”

Convening the people means bringing together whoever is needed to advance the network’s purpose. People often join a network because they are inspired by its purpose, but they stay because of who is involved. Who is part of a network may be just as important, if not more important, than why the network exists.

## Inviting Co-creation

When you bring people together in an impact network, you’re inviting them into co-creation—into the collective discovery of what the network is and what it can become. Networks are not owned by anyone; they are shared by everyone. Established members and newcomers alike are free to self-organize to advance the network’s purpose in alignment with its principles. As new people contribute, new possibilities emerge.

A network’s purpose and principles may be well defined by an initial group before new members arrive. This can be quite helpful—it gives people a sense of whether the network is something they’d like to contribute to, and it helps ensure that the network’s actions stay coherent as it grows. Then, purpose and principles are likely to be refined as new members join the conversation.

Convening people doesn’t mean inviting them into “your thing”; it means inviting them into co-creating what is possible, now that they are part of the group. Every person who joins an impact network has the opportunity, and the responsibility, to contribute to its development.

## Identifying Who to Convene

Who to invite into an impact network depends on its purpose, context, timing, and scope. When considering who to engage, we include people “for a lot of the wrong reasons—obligation, guilt, representation . . . even love,” notes adrienne maree brown. Instead, bring together those who

- are directly impacted by the issue,
- are doing compelling work on the issue, and
- can move the work forward.¹

Make it a priority to convene a diverse set of participants from the very beginning, not later after the effort has already been defined. Whoever is invited to join a network first is likely to significantly impact the makeup of the resulting group, as the social structures that are present at a group’s founding are likely to be retained over time.² This may require seeking out people who have different backgrounds, disciplines, ways of thinking, and experiences. A design team that includes people from many different parts of the system can play a central role in crafting a well-considered selection process or invite list for the launch of a new network.

Otherwise, if diversity is not prioritized, relationship-centered systems can easily become exclusive communities, as people are naturally drawn to connect only with those who look, think, and act like them, writes Vu Le, author of the popular Nonprofit AF blog. “Marginalized people and communities don’t have the same access to relationships: Relationships are not equitably distributed.”³ Therefore, one important step that network leaders—and catalysts in particular—can take to avoid replicating patterns of inequity is to center people who are affected by the issue at hand, as well as those who typically are excluded from networks—from the very beginning. This doesn’t mean “letting them participate,” it means listening and following *their* leadership. Because some people have more barriers than others when it comes to participating fully (such as time, money, distance, ability, and childcare responsibilities), deliberate efforts must also be made to ensure accessibility.

If your network has already launched, it’s not too late. The approach to inclusion that Food Solutions New England (FSNE) took is a good example. FSNE is an action network working to create a more equitable and resilient regional food system across six states. A short while after the network initially formed, participants realized that race was a significant predictor of food-related disparities in the region.⁴ They also realized that their predominantly white membership did not reflect the diversity of the communities impacted by its work.

To rectify this issue, the network appointed a group of participants—and provided them with stipends—to make meaningful connections with new partners. These “network ambassadors,” as they call them, have been instrumental in engaging under-represented communities in the network, including communities of color, young people, and food chain workers. Meanwhile, the network also created space for more racially diverse leadership and improved coordination between community efforts and the regional work of the network.

## Deciding How Many to Convene

Impact networks range drastically in size according to their purpose, context, and type. Some may assume that bigger is always better—that because more people mean more connections, it must also mean greater potential for impact. Indeed, “the network effect” tells us that networks become more valuable as more people are connected to them.⁵ But this phenomenon holds up only to a point. Human and biological networks (like the networks of neurons in our brains) in particular have been shown to have a “breakpoint,” above which the network effect no longer rings true. This is the point at which further growth is no longer beneficial and the network begins a pruning process, shrinking until it reaches an equilibrium—the ideal size for the network at that time. Ultimately, as Jeff Stibel, author of *Breakpoint*, attests, the quality of a network’s connections is much more important than its size.⁶

While size is less of a constraint in some learning networks, our experiences have taught us to start somewhere between twelve and twenty-four participants for the launch of most new action networks. We have found that most groups are able to hold a single conversation with up to about twenty-four people, though it requires a thoughtful design, well-planned room setup, and skilled facilitation. Other experts in the field agree: “12 is large enough to offer a diversity of opinion and large enough that it allows for a certain quotient of mystery and intrigue, of constructive unfamiliarity,” writes Priya Parker in *The Art of Gathering*.⁷ Meanwhile, adrienne maree brown uses a guideline that the amount of time needed for a meaningful full-group conversation is about five minutes per person.⁸ Given that the average person can only pay attention for about 90 to 120 minutes at a time before their eyes completely glaze over,⁹ a group of twenty-four people at five minutes each brings you right up to the upper limit.

Starting smaller initially helps create a group size that can more easily deliberate on issues critical to the network’s formation, with enough space for everyone to contribute. Then, as the network takes shape and gains traction, it can grow much larger if it needs to. The RE-AMP Network was started by fifteen organizations, including one foundation, and it has since grown to more than 140 organizations, including nearly two dozen foundations. 100Kin10 started with twenty-eight partner organizations and now has more than three hundred. On the other hand, if a network is delivering the impact you’re seeking, there may be no need for it to grow much larger.¹⁰ The Santa Cruz Mountains Stewardship Network started with nineteen organizations and has added only a few additional members over six years—even as it continues to expand its impact.

That’s not to say it isn’t possible to start with a larger group at the outset, but in doing so, recognize that there will be tradeoffs: the more people there are, the longer it will take them to build trusting relationships.
