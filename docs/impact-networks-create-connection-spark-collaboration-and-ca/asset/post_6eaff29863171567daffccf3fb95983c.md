## Making Networks Work

> > *There is immense power when a group of people with similar interests gets together to work toward the same goals.*
> >
> > —IDOWU KOYENIKAN, *Wealth for All*

Networks are more than just a way of seeing or thinking about the systems around us; they are a way of organizing to meet the complexity of our world. Impact networks are a viable approach when two conditions are met: (1) you face a complex issue that you can’t solve on your own, and (2) greater levels of connectivity are needed between individuals and/or organizations to address the issue.[^1]

Depending on their specific focus, impact networks may manifest in different forms, each with a distinct structure. As introduced in [chapter 1](#), the three primary forms of impact networks are learning networks, action networks, and movement networks. While we use these categories to distinguish important differences in focus and structure, in practice networks are so fluid and emergent that they rarely fall neatly into a single defined category. For instance, impact networks sometimes start out as a learning network but then evolve later into an action network as more structure is needed to collaborate around shared projects.

This is not to say that becoming an action or movement network has to be the endgame for all impact networks in order to effect social change. Quite the contrary—every form has its place, and each may be the best approach, depending on the network’s purpose and context. If you are not sure which form will best suit your network, start by adopting the simplest

structure—a minimum viable structure—that serves its current needs. A little structure goes a long way, and you can always develop more later if needed.

## Primary Forms of Impact Networks

Learning networks are focused on both connection and learning. Action networks are focused on connection, learning, and action. Movement networks connect multiple learning or action networks together to coordinate a broader movement. An overview of each form is provided below.

## Learning Networks

There are countless examples of learning networks around the globe, though many use different terms to describe themselves, such as “communities of practice” or “knowledge networks,” among others. Learning networks are formed to facilitate the exchange of information, spark innovation, increase coordination, and bolster members’ ability to adapt knowledge to local challenges.² Sometimes they are also formed to provide peer support and to promote the individual development of their participants, as is the case with leadership networks and fellowships. Learning networks referenced in this book include the Initiative for Multipurpose Prevention Technologies, the Justice in Motion Defender Network, and the Fire Adapted Communities Learning Network.

Going beyond the loose relational structure of a typical social network, as seen in figure 3.1, learning networks encourage the consistent flow of information between participants, as well as with outside groups, as seen in figure 3.2. Some learning networks are open, allowing anyone to join, but many are closed, only admitting participants who fit certain criteria (the process of determining participation criteria is known as bounding a network—see chapter 10 for more details). Participants may also form learning circles, gathering together to hold conversations, share knowledge, and collect information on a specific topic related to the network’s purpose.

While participants in a typical learning network do not go as far as collaborating together directly—that is, working in partnership and making

shared decisions—their individual efforts become more closely connected as they learn from one another and find opportunities to support each other’s work. As a result, promising practices spread, resources are more easily distributed to where they are needed most, messaging comes into greater alignment, and the actions of those involved begin to reinforce one another. In this way, building a robust learning network can itself serve as an effective strategy for creating change, even without an emphasis on collaborative action.

## Action Networks

Action networks bring together actors from across systems to connect, learn, and collaborate together to address complex issues. Examples of action networks referenced in this book include the Santa Cruz Mountains Stewardship Network, the Clean Electronics Production Network, the UCSF Health Coordination of Care Network, the RE-AMP Network, and 100Kin10, among others.

Action networks build on all the traits of learning networks by accelerating the flow of information in, out, and across the network, while also taking the next step to actively spark collaboration. One of the ways they foster collaboration is through project teams (sometimes called working groups or task forces), as seen in figure 3.3. Project teams exist to advance a specific body of work related to the network’s purpose. Some learning networks also form project teams—as noted earlier, the forms of impact networks are often fluid—but it is less common. The difference between learning and action networks is not so much in their structure as it is in the primary orientation of their focus: to facilitate the flow of

information and knowledge, as in a learning network, or to advance collaborative action, as in an action network.

Action networks may also develop additional participatory governance structures to guide collective activities. One such governance structure that most action networks (and some learning networks) establish is a *core team*. Core teams (sometimes called *leadership teams*, *steering committees*, or *governance councils*) consist of a group of participants who volunteer or are elected to guide the work and activities of the network. More details on the functions of core teams, project teams, and learning circles can be found in chapter 10.

Action networks have been called many things, including *alliances*, *coalitions*, *collective impact initiatives*, *consortiums*, *innovation networks*, and more. They may have varying degrees of structure: Some are loose collaboratives that bring groups together to champion a cause. Others, like collective impact initiatives, are highly structured efforts that emphasize a data-driven shared measurement system to ensure that activities are mutually reinforcing.

Their aims also can vary widely in scope. Coalitions, for instance, often seek to achieve a specific outcome, like passing a new policy. Once that outcome has been achieved, the coalition usually disbands. Most of the action networks we reference in this book, however, pursue many actions simultaneously to address complex, systemic issues that don’t have a definitive end point.

Meanwhile, the role of the core network is to facilitate information sharing, amplify and support the work of its members, and catalyze action on a broader scale. Like the core team of an action network, core networks provide a leadership role to steward the larger whole.

The Agribusiness Market Ecosystem Alliance (AMEA) is an example of a movement network working at both global and local levels to accelerate the development of professional farmer organizations, reaching millions of smallholder farms in eighty countries. AMEA’s core network consists of twenty-seven member organizations, including Heifer International, Mercy Corps, and the Rainforest Alliance, who are collaborating to create systemic change in the agricultural sector. Simultaneously, the core network also catalyzes and supports regional networks to create thriving local ecosystems for agribusiness stakeholders. AMEA currently includes five regional networks, embedded within the countries of Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, Ivory Coast, and Honduras, with plans to support the growth of additional networks in Latin America and West Africa in the years ahead. The regional networks are free to make decisions and take actions within their local context, provided they agree to a set of shared principles that guide the movement network as a whole.
