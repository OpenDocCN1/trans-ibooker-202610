## Resourcing Networks

The resources that support impact networks to launch and grow take many forms. Resources are needed to provide meeting space and food for convenings, to compensate network leaders, and to support projects emerging from the network. Impact networks are typically resourced through one or more of the following sources:

- Philanthropic funding
- Organizational resources
- In-kind contributions
- Participant dues
- Earned revenue from network projects

Each source is summarized in brief below.

**Philanthropic funding** has played a critical role in developing impact networks around the world. Foundations and philanthropists have resources to support the operational costs associated with collaborative efforts. With a systems-level view of complex issues, some are also well positioned to identify opportunities to catalyze new networks. The unique role of network funders is examined in more detail later in this chapter.

- Follow the network’s lead
- Engage as peers
- Take the long view

Each practice is described in more detail below.

## Follow the Network’s Lead

The primary challenge for funders is to support networks without restricting or controlling their path. On a conscious or subconscious level, some funders think they deserve more influence over the network’s direction than participants because they provided the money. Yet, by holding too tightly to control—by insisting on paying for a set of predefined outcomes, for instance—they risk stifling a network’s potential.

The key for funders is to let the network lead so that participants are free to create the future *they* want—not the future that funders want *for them*. “Foundations should not be the leaders, but instead provide support,” notes Liz Wilson, a senior executive at Small Foundation.¹⁶ According to Jennifer Husbands, a senior program officer at the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, this requires that funders “understand how to walk a tightrope between providing structure and encouraging organic connections to develop and flourish.”¹⁷ As a network funder, be clear on what you are supporting: you are not funding the fulfillment of a production order or provision of services to a specific number of people; rather, you are supporting the capacity of an ecosystem to engage a spectrum of perspectives, learn together, and self-determine optimal responses to local challenges.

## Engage as Peers

When funders play a prominent role in an impact network’s formation, they are in danger of reinforcing unhealthy power dynamics. A core lesson for network funders, writes David Nee, former president of the Graustein Memorial Fund, is to “share power with communities and other stakeholders, working alongside their peers as equal partners.”¹⁸

In particular, network funders should carefully consider their role in decision-making to avoid having outsized influence. For example, soon after catalyzing the RE-AMP Network, the Garfield Foundation ensured that its formal decision-making power was relegated to a single vote, just like every other network member. “Garfield realized it was important for us to pull back our central role,” recalls Ruth Rominger, director of the collaborative networks program at the foundation. “Funders have a tendency to want to own, but we decided we wanted to take more of a partnership role, while continuing to fund the network.”¹⁹

## Take the Long View

Many funders expect their grantees to show outcomes within just a few years. “Given the complexity of many issues that may have their origin in centuries’ worth of root causes, this expectation is just plain crazy,” writes Edgar Villanueva in *Decolonizing Wealth*.²⁰ The report titled *Embracing Complexity*, from Ashoka and others, echoes this sentiment, emphasizing the importance of preparing “for long-term engagement by being realistic about the time it takes to achieve systems change, acknowledging that the path of the initiatives will change along the way and encouraging realistic ambitions.”²¹

In the case of Sterling Network NYC, the outcomes and even the duration of the network are not fixed: participants recommit to the network on an annual basis, but the Robert Sterling Clark Foundation has committed to supporting the network for at least ten years. According to Li, who helped catalyze the network as president of the foundation, “One of the great gifts foundations can provide is to allow people to focus on experimenting and putting their heads to the issues that are being explored, rather than spending all their time finding funding to sustain the work.”²²

## Investing in the Future

Although many recognize the need for greater coordination between organizations, collaboration remains underresourced. As Susan Wolf Ditkoff and Abe Grindle write in *Harvard Business Review*, “So few philanthropists meaningfully support or engage in [collaboration], even though most are frustrated with the inefficient proliferation of siloed change efforts.”²³

Some funders have the impression that networks are especially expensive or difficult to fund, but the truth is, they don’t need to be—not if you’re investing in the right things, and not if you simply get out of the way and let the network get to work. To get started, “find a partner you trust who can help manage the network and hold a mirror up to you if you are impeding the network’s development,” suggests Jennifer Husbands.²⁴ We also encourage funders to focus on investing in dedicated network leadership, with all the roles it entails—including catalyzing, facilitation, weaving, and coordination. Under-resourced leadership is one of the primary factors we have seen that cause impact networks to fail to live up to their potential.

Ultimately, an investment in networks is an investment in our ability to work across silos and sectors to get things done. To tackle our most pressing challenges, we must collaborate—and impact networks provide the essential infrastructure that enables individuals and organizations to connect, learn, and work together for a better future. “Take a learning-by-doing approach, and take the first step,” encourages Liz Wilson.²⁵ It’s time for funders to embrace the way of the network.

Next, you and others become network weavers, fostering relationships of trust that will sustain far into the future. People become much more willing to share information, discuss failures, and extend help when support is needed. A culture of reciprocity is born.

As the network grows, a critical task of network leadership is to provide coordination to integrate the network’s many moving parts into a unified whole. With an enabling infrastructure in place, the network is able to respond quickly when crisis hits, distributing resources to where they’re needed most. Participants self-organize into teams to implement strategies for change, with more leadership opportunities available to serve as team leads. Gradually, the entire network connects with others working on similar issues to catalyze transformation at larger scales.

This is how change happens: one step at a time. At first, progress may seem small, resembling new connections, new conversations, or moments of service that provide help in a time of need. Yet, these are the building blocks of collaboration. Without these, we cannot effectively advance the larger-scale transformation needed to create systemic change.

If there’s one thing I hope you take away from this book, it is this: trust-based relationships enable everything else that a network aims to achieve. When people begin to connect and co-create in new ways—bound together by the strength of their relationships—magic happens. Conversations that were previously impossible become possible. Alliances form between groups that used to consider each other competitors. Audacious goals become attainable, and impact emerges at the system level. If at any point you feel unsure about how to proceed, invest in relationships. To be a network leader is first and foremost to weave the threads that tie us together. This is how a fragmented system can become a creative, adaptive, interconnected whole.

Throughout your journey as a network leader, you will inevitably face difficulties along with joy and satisfaction. The process of cultivating impact networks can never be fully understood or complete. There are many ways to go about this work, and our opportunity is to practice and learn in community with one another.
