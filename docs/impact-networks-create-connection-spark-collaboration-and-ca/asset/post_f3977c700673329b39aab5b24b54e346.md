
The process of gathering information into a network begins with a clear and consistent practice of collecting relevant resources and data from participants, as well as from outside sources. That information can then be organized and made available to participants through tools such as asset maps, searchable directories, and online databases. An interactive asset map, for instance, provides members with an easy way to identify the available resources, areas of expertise, and geographic regions present across the network—while also helping them to immediately connect with each other to follow through on what they learned. Specifically, the IMPT gathers information from its members through its online resource center. The resource center collects a wide range of resources from beyond the network, including articles, presentations, events, and funding opportunities related to the network’s purpose. Network coordinators help to organize the resources gathered from participants so they are up-to-date and easy to find. Coordinators also attend conferences, subscribe to listserv groups and alerts, and track all MPT products in development in order to collect information from the broader field in addition to its members.

## Information Out

Once collected, information is sent back out to participants through webinars, calls, emails, and other means of distribution. Electronic newsletters, when curated, can be effective at providing participants with a broad overview of what’s happening across the network in only a few minutes.

In addition to its monthly newsletter, full of information relevant to the field, the IMPT hosts regular webinars, presents at conferences, and provides opportunities for participants to share their work and answer questions. The network also hosts a website, theimpt.org, to make information about MPTs available to the public.

## Information Across

Impact networks also provide participants with multiple channels to connect with each other directly so that information can flow freely between them, unrestrained from the bottleneck of a central hub. Most networks set up an online communication system where members can share resources, opportunities, and requests. To create feedback loops for learning, it’s also good practice to establish simple network-wide practices for sharing real-time learnings related to the work that people are doing.

The IMPT enables the flow of information across the MPT field by weaving connections between people and organizations who should know each other but do not. They do so by identifying people who are conducting complementary research and then actively making formal introductions between them, going so far as helping to schedule an initial call. Coordinators also organize meetings ranging in size from small workshops to large conferences and regularly convene a collaborative group of funders to prioritize funding needs and fill funding gaps.

## Network Coordination

The people who specialize in developing the network’s ability to share information, capture learnings, and coordinate actions are called *network coordinators*. Coordinators provide the operational support that impact networks need to sustain their activities over an extended period of time. In particular, coordinators have four primary responsibilities:

- Keep the network on track
- Establish and maintain network operations
- Provide knowledge management
- Assist network teams

Each responsibility is summarized in brief below.

## Keep the Network on Track

Coordinators are a critical hub in the middle of a network, helping to tie everything together. One way to think about coordinators is as air traffic controllers. Air traffic controllers see and understand how all the planes in a given airspace are flowing. They don’t fly the planes, but they are trusted to help each aircraft get to its destination. They regularly communicate with the pilots to understand their plans and needs, and they use that information to help guide the whole system.

Similarly, coordinators have their finger on the pulse of nearly everything that’s happening across the network. They work to integrate many parts into a unified whole. Because of their unique position, coordinators are well equipped to identify and frame emerging issues, collect agenda topics, and support the design of convenings and calls. Embedding evaluation practices, such as those described in chapter 10, helps coordinators to track the network’s development and identify emerging needs and opportunities.

## Establish and Maintain Network Operations

Coordinators are instrumental in setting up and maintaining a network’s online systems (explained in more detail below), guiding logistics for convenings and other events, tracking the network’s finances, and managing evaluation activities. Though coordinators may not lead bookkeeping or evaluation, they make sure these tasks are covered by others who know what they’re doing, and they ensure that important information is passed on to participants.

## Provide Knowledge Management

Coordinators act as knowledge managers who gather resources and learnings from network projects, organizing the information so that it can be easily accessed later. They also curate and share this information with participants through newsletters and other means. Coordinators make sure that people have the information they need to advance network activities while building on the work that came before.

## Assist Network Teams

As networks develop, coordinators may also provide a wide range of support to network teams, including the core team, project teams, and learning circles (these teams were introduced in **chapter 3** and will be explained in more detail in **chapter 10**). Coordinators may assist network teams by offering thought partnership, helping with scheduling, and securing a meeting location.

Although coordinators can be a valuable asset for the network’s teams, this doesn’t mean they should be tasked with carrying out the project work of the network. Research has shown that networks in which the coordinators perceived themselves as primarily responsible for carrying out the network’s actions had the lowest levels of implemented results.⁴ Rather than doing the work of the network, coordinators cultivate the conditions that enable network members to do *what they want to do*, in service of the network’s purpose and in alignment with its principles.

## Tech Tools for Impact Networks

Most impact networks will benefit from an assortment of simple and effective software tools for tasks such as written communication, audio and video conferencing, polling and group decision-making, ideating and whiteboarding, collaborative document and spreadsheet creation, file sharing, and scheduling, as well as asset mapping and social network analysis. The right set of tools can drastically amplify the network’s ability to stay connected and self-organize. Choose the wrong tools and you might be stuck with steep learning curves and annoying tech issues. Technology is always changing, so rather than sharing specific tools in this book, we have compiled our favorites online in a continually updated list. Visit the Converge Network Toolkit to see our favorite Tech Tools for Impact Networks.

Regardless of the specific tools you choose, make sure they are easy to use. The specific use-case of each tool also needs to be clearly understood by the network. Tools are only good if people use them, and people will look for any reason not to use yet another tool.

It’s also important to take the time to orient participants simultaneously on the use of a new tool. Whenever we launch a new tech tool into a network, we take a few minutes during a convening or on a network call to introduce it, explain how it is intended to be used, and show the basics of what it can offer. If possible, we also allow a bit of hands-on time for participants to try it out.

If network coordinators are relatively tech savvy, they can make themselves available to assist those who are not comfortable with a particular tool—walking through it in detail until people feel at ease. We also encourage matching up network members as tech buddies, connecting the people who have high comfort with a given tool with people who need to learn. This way, participants can reach out to one another for support, rather than requiring one person to be responsible for providing tech support to the entire network.

## Practicing Reciprocity

People join networks because they feel they can make a contribution and think the network can help them do something they can’t do on their own. But no matter how much they care about the issue at hand, the altruistic commitment of participants alone is rarely enough to sustain a network over time. More than likely, networks will need to serve the personal or organizational priorities of those involved to sustain their engagement over time. This reflects the dynamic tension of self-interest and shared interest.

Taking the time to deliberately coordinate actions is a great way to serve the self-interests of participants. You can do so by providing space on calls and at convenings for people to share urgent needs and to connect with others who might be able to support their work. We call this activity Rapid Coordination, and we incorporate it into nearly every network convening we lead. The activity begins by inviting participants, one by one, to briefly share something they need help with or a potential collaboration they’d like to explore with others. After the first person shares what they need, others are invited to raise their hands if they believe they can help (by sharing information, having a follow-up conversation, or providing resources). The process continues until everyone has been given a chance to make a request.
