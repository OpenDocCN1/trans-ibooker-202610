## Network Connectivity

Network connectivity reflects how well relationships are forming across a network. As the effectiveness of any network depends fundamentally on the strength of the connections between the participants, network connectivity is often the primary means of assessing progress in the early days of its formation.

Social network analysis (SNA), also known as network mapping, is the most effective method currently available for visualizing and evaluating that connectivity. SNA offers an empirical way to represent the patterns of connection and disconnection among participants at a given moment in time. This provides important clues about where clusters are forming, how information is flowing, and how best to weave relationships.¹²

SNA can be used to capture the degree to which participants have formed relationships with one another as well as whether or not they are communicating (sharing information, ideas, or data), coordinating (their efforts are closely connected, but they maintain separate resources and responsibilities), or collaborating (working in partnership, sharing resources, and making shared decisions). We aim to gather this information before the first network convening to get baseline data, and then again after major convenings or at regular intervals (for instance, every six months) thereafter to assess change over time.

SNA can support a network’s development in several ways, such as the following:

- Assessing the network’s growth, as shown in figure 10.1
- Identifying key influencers and those who are least engaged
- Highlighting opportunities for intervention and weaving
- Revealing patterns of connection and disconnection

## Participant Experience

Evaluating network health involves assessing the degree to which the network is cohering around shared goals and developing a positive culture of collaboration. The key factors highlighted below are especially critical to the long-term success of impact networks and their ability to keep participants engaged.

A healthy network is one where participants feel they have clarified a shared purpose and a set of shared principles. The network includes a broad cross section of participants who are well suited to address the network’s purpose and actively contribute to its development. Those participants are developing relationships of trust, and they feel they are able to engage in and resolve conflicts with one another.

The network has defined whatever structures and agreements are necessary to organize the work and make good decisions. These structures and agreements should be flexible enough to allow the network to stay responsive to changes in the environment without feeling unnecessarily burdensome.

Healthy networks have ample *coordination* such that necessary operational responsibilities are well accounted for. Relevant *information* is easily accessible and freely shared. Participants also feel that the network has adequate *resources* to sustain its activities and that funders are supporting the network without controlling its path.

And last but not least, participants in healthy networks feel that they are receiving good *value* from the time they are investing. They feel that they are benefiting personally and professionally as individuals and that their organizations are benefiting from their involvement in the network as well.

Administering participant surveys at regular intervals provides a good assessment of the core indicators of network health. They offer a regular space for participants to provide feedback, supporting equity and ensuring that network leaders have a good understanding of participants’ perceptions of the network. The survey results help illuminate areas of strength as well as areas that would benefit from additional attention or support. Participant surveys can also help capture details related to a range of outcomes providing value for participants, such as growth in skills, knowledge, meaningful relationships, and professional effectiveness, as well as value for their respective organizations, such as amplified reach or impact. These outcomes enable members to justify their participation to their organizations, and they support the network as a whole to justify its work to funders. ## Emergent Activity

While some network outcomes are predictable (including increased connectivity and value from participation), many are not. The magic of networks lies in their emergent outcomes, which cannot be defined in advance, will not be the same across networks, and therefore cannot be measured with standardized tools. Although the precise nature of these outcomes cannot be predicted in advance, it is still valuable to track them as they emerge. Specifically, tracking the coordinated work and collaborative projects that are sparked among participants is essential to understanding how the network is impacting the broader system. This information will help network leaders and participants advance promising opportunities and potential innovations, as well as tell the story of the network’s influence on the broader system. It will also highlight any particularly exciting projects that could be the subject of a more detailed case study.

A critical purpose of collecting and compiling this information is to reflect the network’s actions and accomplishments back to participants in an efficient way. Doing so provides participants with real-time knowledge of what is happening in the network and supports their ability to find ways to plug in if they notice that a project is related to their work.

Given their role, network coordinators in particular should be aware of new developments, including projects and partnerships among participants. We advise coordinators to record this information and ensure that it is updated regularly. They may collect this information informally by talking with participants on a regular schedule, leading an interactive activity at a convening, or including questions about these activities on a regular survey. Once it is gathered, coordinators can compile the information into a dashboard or document that participants can access to stay updated on network activities and opportunities to engage.

## More Extensive Evaluation

Beyond these four fundamental evaluation components, networks that have the capacity and resources for more comprehensive evaluation activities may also consider organizing participant interviews or focus groups and conducting a qualitative analysis of this data. They may also assess the impacts of one or more collaborative projects undertaken by participants or analyze available regional or epidemiological data related to the network’s purpose to illustrate changes over time. More extensive evaluation practices can require substantial time and expertise and are often most effectively undertaken in close partnership with a professional evaluator.
