## Designing Meaningful Gatherings

Impact networks create many different ways for people to connect with one another. Communication platforms provide spaces for participants to hold conversations, share events and opportunities, make requests, and keep each other updated. Network leaders (weavers, in particular) connect people together directly by making introductions and setting the context for a conversation. And an assortment of in-person and online activities provide spaces for people to engage around shared interests, such as network calls and webinars, site visits, learning journeys, meals organized around a conversation topic, professional development opportunities, and peer assists. Network leaders are always looking for opportunities to facilitate connection and accelerate the flow of information.

Still, when it comes to cultivating impact networks, there is simply no substitute for convenings, whether they take place online or in person. Convenings bring all participants together to simultaneously build relationships, learn from one another, and make decisions for the future. They are milestone moments that can propel a network forward as both a tight-knit community and a vehicle for creating change. Specifically, convenings serve four primary functions:

**Convenings bring the whole system together.** They create unique opportunities for participants to recognize how their work is interconnected. When people hear each other’s varied perspectives on an issue, they start to develop a greater understanding of the *whole* system. This awareness of the big picture permanently shifts the way that people approach their work.

**Convenings provide space for relationships.** They bring people together to meet others they don’t yet know and to deepen trust with those they’ve met before. It’s easy to get caught up in daily responsibilities and forget to take the time to build relationships, even if it’s obvious conceptually that relationships are central to the work. Convenings encourage people to get away from their email inboxes and to focus instead on connecting with others, not just as organizational representatives but as human beings.

**Convenings advance learning and action.** They provide an opportunity for participants to share what they’ve learned from their recent work and to plan for the work ahead. The gaps between convenings (usually ranging from four to six months, or a year at most) may provide a good length of time to implement a project or test an idea in the real world. Learnings can then be shared—and plans revised—at the next convening. This cycle of planning and iteration allows networks to integrate both deliberate and emergent strategies for action.

**Convenings create energy.** They maintain momentum over the course of a network’s formation. Picture the network as a basketball spinning on a finger. The longer it spins without an infusion of energy, the more it starts to lose its center of gravity and risk falling over. We regularly see a spike in communication between participants just after convenings are held. This level of activity slowly dissipates as we get further and further away from the last time people were in a room together (virtual or in person). Convenings keep the ball spinning.

The cadence and format of convenings differ based on the type of network. We have found that learning networks convene one to two times per year on average, while action networks convene one to four times per year on average. Some networks may only ever convene remotely—because of distance, cost, and accessibility—while others mostly convene in person. Regardless of the specific cadence or format, effective network convenings tend to be fairly similar in their overall approach. Below is a set of key lessons we have learned from our collective experiences leading network convenings online and in person.

## Key Considerations for Designing Convenings

Facilitation often gets the bulk of the credit for a successful convening; facilitators are so visible in their role that they are not easily forgotten. But we are convinced that the design—including agenda and structure—is even more responsible for a convening’s ultimate success. Though every convening should be customized to fit the particular context of the network, we have learned a few key lessons that inform each one of our designs:

- Put relationships first
- Embrace the magic of small groups
- Focus on the framing questions
- Cultivate deeper experiences
- Incorporate space
- Infuse energy into the network

### Put Relationships First

Many believe that meetings are not worthwhile unless they result in tangible outcomes and plans for the future. But progress is about so much more than just defining strategies and clarifying next steps. In the world of impact networks, building relationships is the work.

Forward motion happens when people have conversations they don’t normally have, with people they don’t normally have them with. It happens when people learn more about their differences and find places where they agree. It also happens when participants foster a sense of belonging, together.

Far too many meetings are designed primarily around content. But the most valuable resource at any gathering is the people who attend. The rare opportunity of having people together at one time is too often wasted with presentations that could be recorded and broadcast online instead.

We recommend moving far away from the “sage on the stage” approach, where so-called experts are the only ones talking and everyone else is passively listening. Recognize that everyone in the room is an expert about something. Everyone has something to contribute. Draw on the wisdom of the crowd, and create spaces where people are regularly interacting with one another, learning from one another, and finding ways to support each other’s work.

### Embrace the Magic of Small Groups

For people to engage most fully, they need to be in small enough groups that they can see and interact with each other simultaneously. Small groups are uniquely powerful as sites of practice; they function as fractals, microcosms of the whole. As Peter Block writes, “The small group is the unit of transformation and the container for the experience of belonging.”¹²

Small groups give everyone a chance to speak and be heard, while also warming them up and making it easier for them to speak in the full group later. As a general guideline, think of the full group as a place for framing and summarizing, and use small groups to dig into content and discussions.

### Focus on the Framing Questions

Like the network itself, a convening design should provide just enough structure and no more. Getting the framing questions right is the key to this equation. The right questions help groups focus their attention on what matters most. Otherwise, conversations might range widely from group to group, creating a lack of cohesion across the convening. We spend as much time in our designs crafting thoughtful framing questions as anything else.

The best questions get to the heart of the matter. They invite people to bring their own meaning forward, based on their own experiences. They have an edge that creates palpable energy when people offer their response. Often the most important piece of the design is to get the questions right, put them in the right sequence, and then pair each question with a group size and amount of time to do it justice.

One of our favorite techniques is to rotate people through a series of quick pairs or small groups with a set of framing questions. At the beginning of a session, questions such as “Why does this work matter to you?” and “What are your hopes for our time today?” help to set the context for the work ahead.
