To get a p-value from the likelihood ratio, we rely on the fact that it’s a χ² statistic with one degree of freedom. (Recall from the last chapter that, for the violet example, G² = 137.19 and the p-value is practically 0.) In general, the smaller the p-value, the stronger the evidence against the null hypothesis: remember that a p-value is the probability, assuming that the null hypothesis is true, of observing a test statistic at least as large in magnitude as what we compute from our data. More plainly, think of the p-value as the chance of getting what you got if the null is true—just don’t ever write that in a book or paper. If this evidence against the null hypothesis is sufficiently strong, we reject it and can be confident that the response does depend on the predictor; on the other hand, if the evidence against the null is sufficiently weak, we fail to reject it and cannot be confident that the response depends on the predictor.

Using the likelihood ratio in this way is the likelihood ratio test, the preferred method for assessing whether β could be 0 in a simple logistic model. The Wald test, based on the Wald statistic just defined, is also commonly used and is often reported by statistical software, as in the summary of chasm_fit above. It’s therefore important to be comfortable with Wald statistics, too—and it’s just as important to understand why the likelihood ratio test is usually better.
