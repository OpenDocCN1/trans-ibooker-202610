## 3.5 Summary

- The regression line $\hat{y}=a+b\,x$ is the best approximation, using the available data, of the deterministic component of the theoretical model, $Y=\alpha+\beta\,x+\varepsilon$.
- The error term, $\varepsilon$, is always assumed to have mean 0 and constant variance.
- Linear regression does not require normality!
- Check the residuals to assess normality of the errors. If $\varepsilon \sim N(0,\sigma)$, inferences are straightforward:
  - The fitted model includes standard errors for the coefficients.
  - Confidence intervals can be computed for the coefficients and for mean responses.
  - Prediction intervals can be computed for individual respons s.

As we know from the last chapter, simple linear regression relates the expected value of a quantitative response to a predictor’s value. While this can be the ideal tool to use when the response has a continuous range of possible values, like measurements, it won’t work when the response is categorical. To see how to handle this kind of data, we’ll start with the simplest possible categorical response, a binary variable that has only two possible values. These values correspond to two disjoint categories— Yes or No, Agree or Disagree, True or False— and are usually coded numerically as either 0 or 1, with 0 interpreted generically as failure and 1 interpreted as success. These terms should not be interpreted literally, though; in some medical studies, for example, a response value of 1 denotes death or the presence of a disease, hardly a success from the patient’s point of view!

It’s no surprise that binary variables are ubiquitous in medical data— a patient either has or does not have a disease, lives or dies, relapses or not, and so on. Although much medical data is private, for obvious reasons, there are some excellent publicly available medical datasets, like the National Health and Nutrition Examination Study (NHANES) and the National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey (NAMCS). The NAMCS asks physicians for their patients' demographic data, their reasons for seeking medical care, and their diagnoses, treatments, and medications; the 2009 NAMCS, in particular, collected data from about 30,000 patient visits to more than 1,000 physicians' offices. Thanks to this abundance of data, we can explore the relationships between the NAMCS variables directly, without having to build a statistical model.
