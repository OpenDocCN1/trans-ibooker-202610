## 5.2.3 Residual analysis and model checking

Up to now, we’ve focused on fitting models with particular functional forms —null models with one parameter and simple logistic models with two parameters—to the available data, either packaged as individual binary responses or grouped into covariate classes. Once the form of the model and the structure of the data have been specified, we fit the model to the data by minimizing the deviance. The resulting value of the deviance therefore tells us *something* about the quality of the fitted model, with better fitting models typically having smaller deviances than poorly fitting ones; the likelihood ratio test’s comparison of nested models exemplifies this general principle. After using this test to choose between competing models, the next steps are to assess the overall fit of the selected model and then to investigate the fit in detail. The former is known, somewhat misleadingly, as checking the model’s *goodness of fit*, and the latter involves a careful analysis of residuals.

The deviance is a common goodness of fit metric, but using it for this purpose is fraught with challenges that should be better understood. First of all, when working with logistic models based on individual binary responses, the deviance tells us absolutely nothing about the agreement between the model’s predictions and observations. In fact, as you’ll work out in Exercise 7.2.2, the residual deviance in this case equals

$$-2\sum_{i}\left(\hat{\pi}_{i}\log\hat{\pi}_{i}+\left(1-\hat{\pi}_{i}\right)\log\left(1-\hat{\pi}_{i}\right)\right),$$

an expression that depends *only* on the predicted probabilities and omits any reference to the observed responses $Y_i$. Consequently, there’s no way for it to measure how well the logistic model fits the data! The deviance is certainly more informative when working with grouped responses, but we have to keep its definition in mind—the deviance compares the log-likelihoods of the fitted model and the saturated model, so it really measures how well the fitted model performs *relative to the saturated model*. This is why calling the deviance a goodness of fit statistic is a misnomer; it is more accurate to say that the deviance tells us whether we could improve the model significantly by adding parameters. To formalize this as a hypothesis test, we need to know the relevant distribution so that we can determine whether a given model’s deviance is small or large. Here’s the result, which we’ll investigate with simulations in Chapter 9: when the number $n$ of covariate classes is *fixed* and the covariate classes are all *large*, the deviance is roughly a $\chi^2_p$ statistic with $\left(n - p\right)$ degrees of freedom, where $p$ is the number of predictors in the model. This should make you wonder about several things:

- How good is this distributional approximation?
- How large do the covariate classes need to be for the approximation to be valid?
- How are covariate classes determined when the predictor is continuous?

The first two of these questions don’t have simple answers, but working through lots of examples provides the experience needed to deal with them. The third question is of a different sort. For the NAMCS data, for example, the covariate classes we’ve been using—patient ages—are quite natural, but we could just as reasonably organize the subjects into 5-year intervals, decades (which we did earlier), or groups like adolescents, young adults, middle-aged adults, and elderly patients. Since these options produce different numbers of covariate classes, the ensuing deviances would be $\chi^2$ statistics with very different numbers of degrees of freedom and could easily have very different $p$-values. What this means is that interpreting the deviance relies on a combination of theory, common sense, experience, and context. This means that the model could be improved dramatically by adding parameters, which makes perfect sense: we’re comparing a simple binomial logistic model with 2 parameters to a saturated model with 101 parameters, so it would be shocking if our tiny model were competitive with the larger model. Also, we know from our previous work that this simple binomial model does a good job of summarizing the relationship between ages and chronic conditions, so we shouldn’t interpret the deviance and its $p$-value in isolation. You should explore this further by grouping the responses in different ways, building various binomial logistic models, and seeing what happens with the deviances and $p$-values (see Exercise 7.1.2). As you play around with this and other examples, think carefully about the conventional wisdom that you should just compare the model’s deviance to $(n - p)$, the average value of the related $\chi^2$ distribution; there is a rule of thumb that a model with deviance less than $(n - p)$ fits well while a model with a larger deviance fits poorly, but context and common sense matter, too!

The other common overall measure of a logistic model’s goodness of fit is Pearson’s $X^2$ statistic, a direct comparison of predictions and observations. To define it, recall that we’re thinking of the $i^{th}$ covariate class as a binomial experiment with $n_i$ trials and a predicted probability $\hat{\Pi}_i$ of success on each trial; the expected number of successes is $n_i \hat{\Pi}_i$, the observed number of successes is $y_i$, and the variance of the number of successes is approximately $n_i \hat{\Pi}_i \left(1 - \hat{\Pi}_i\right)$. Normalizing the raw residual $\left(y_i - n_i \hat{\Pi}_i\right)$ by the estimated standard deviation yields the $i^{th}$ Pearson residual,

$$X_i = \frac{y_i - n_i \hat{\Pi}_i}{\sqrt{n_i \hat{\Pi}_i \left(1 - \hat{\Pi}_i\right)}},$$

a statistic that quantifies how well the model agrees with the observations in the $i^{th}$ covariate class.
