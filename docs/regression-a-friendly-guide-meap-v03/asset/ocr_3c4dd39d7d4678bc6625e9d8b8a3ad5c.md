realizations are the statistics $a$ and $b$. In the case of linear regression, assuming that the error $\mathcal{E}$ is normally distributed enables the calculation of explicit formulas for the sampling distributions of the regression coefficients; while neither the calculations nor the resulting formulas are trivial, they’re not too bad once you get the hang of working with random variables. The situation is quite different for logistic regression, and we will not even attempt to derive or state all of the relevant formulas. Instead, we’ll rely on a fact whose proof can be found elsewhere [15]: when working with large samples, the maximum likelihood estimates of the logistic regression coefficients are approximately unbiased and normally distributed. As long as this large sample assumption holds, we can therefore apply standard methods based on normal distributions to compute interval estimates and test hypotheses. What this means in practice is that we need the standard errors for the coefficients, which are the best estimates based on our data of the standard deviations of the sampling distributions.
