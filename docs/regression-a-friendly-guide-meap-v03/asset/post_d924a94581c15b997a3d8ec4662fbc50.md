
since the trials are independent, the overall probability is the product of the probabilities of the individual outcomes:

P(Y = 0) ≈ (1 − Π)^n   and   P(Y = n) ≈ Π^n.

Similarly, the probability of having successes on the first k attempts followed by failures on the remaining n − k attempts is Π^k * (1 − Π)^(n − k). In fact, this is the probability of any specific ordering of k successes and n − k failures; what remains is to determine the number of ways that these successes and failures can be rearranged. Think of this set of successes and failures as n slips of paper, k of which have a 1 written on them and n − k of which have a 0 written on them. Ignoring the 0s and 1s, there are n * (n − 1) * (n − 2) * ... * 2 * 1 = n! ways to arrange these slips of paper, as there are n ways to select the first slip, n − 1 ways to select the second slip, and so on. For any specific arrangement of the slips of paper, rearranging the ones or zeroes won’t really change anything, so we have to account for lots of identical arrangements. Since there are k! permutations of the 1s and (n − k)! permutations of the 0s for a specific arrangement of slips of paper, the total number of distinct arrangements of successes and failures is

$$\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\,.$$

This quantity is the *binomial coefficient* $\binom{n}{k}$; by convention,

$0! = 1$, so $\binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} = 1$. Putting this all together, we find that

$P(Y = k) = \binom{n}{k} \pi^{k} (1 - \pi)^{n - k}, \quad \text{for } 0 \le k \le n.$

This *probability distribution* defines the binomial random variable $Y \sim B(n, p)$ and is, not surprisingly, known as the

*binomial distribution* with parameters $n$ and $p$. When there’s only one trial, $n = 1$ and we get a Bernoulli random variable with probability $p$ of success.

Whenever you meet a new probability distribution, you want to get to know it better—specifically, you want to know its average (i.e., its expected value) and its variance. Brute force is one way to compute the average of a binomial random variable, but that is unnecessarily difficult and ugly. Instead, we’ll exploit a clever hack: the binomial random variable $Y \sim B(n, p)$ is the sum of $n$ independent

Bernoulli random variables, each of which corresponds to one of the trials in the experiment. Consequently, the average of $Y$ is just the sum of the averages of the Bernoulli variables, so that $E(Y)=n\pi$. Using the same trick, the variance of $Y$ is $Var(Y)=n\pi(1-\pi)$, making its standard deviation $\sqrt{n\pi(1-\pi)}$. When the probability $p$ is close to 0.5 or the number $n$ of trials is large, the binomial distribution is roughly normal, so that $B(n,p)\approx N\left(n\pi,\sqrt{n\pi(1-\pi)}\right)$. This is easy to see when we plot $B(20,.5)$ and $B(250,.1)$, for example, overlaying the approximating normal distributions on each (in magenta):
