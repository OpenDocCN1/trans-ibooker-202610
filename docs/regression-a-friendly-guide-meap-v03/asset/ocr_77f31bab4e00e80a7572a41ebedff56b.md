First, suppose that we have a nice, clean dataset with thousands and thousands of rows and just a few columns. With all of this data at our disposal, we don’t need to figure out an appropriate statistical model and fit it to the data— we can work directly with summary statistics and carefully explore how they depend on the available predictors. We might have an extensive database of information about software developers and their salaries, for example; we could organize the developers by experience and industry and then compute the average salary for each combination, thereby discovering how the average response depends on the predictors. We wouldn’t need to assume a particular functional form for the relationship between the response and the predictors, nor would we be restricted to working with the usual average— it might make more sense to investigate how median salaries depend on experience and industry, and working with the median would be no harder than working with the mean. If you can get plenty of data, start with a simple exploratory approach like this before doing something complicated.

Having lots of data can be great, but it’s possible to have way more data than you can handle— this is what happens in a true Big Data problem, when there are, say, millions and millions of rows and hundreds or thousands of possible explanatory variables. Regression might not help much in this situation, since there’s too much data for people to process in anything but a haphazard way. This is when machine learning methods like dimension reduction and deep learning shine, as algorithms can wrestle signals from the noise systematically, and models have access to the generous training sets that they need.

Big Data gets a lot of attention these days, for some very good reasons: the technical problems are interesting and challenging, and the people solving these problems get paid handsomely by some of the most influential tech companies in the world.
