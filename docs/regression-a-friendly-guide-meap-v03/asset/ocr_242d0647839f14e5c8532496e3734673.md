predictor equals x. Taking k = 2, Chebyshev’s inequality says that there is at least a 75% chance that

α + β x − 2σ ≤ Y ≤ α + β x + 2σ .

Of course, we don’t know α, β, or σ! Combining estimates based on the available data with Chebyshev’s inequality, we can be confident that most of the response values will be within 2 RSEs of the regression line. This is the most practical use of Chebyshev’s inequality— letting k = 1 just says that the probability is nonnegative, which is not helpful!

An important consequence of the three assumptions about ε is that the least squares estimates of the model parameters are optimal, in two ways:

+   - a and b are unbiased estimates of α and β, and

+   - this approach to estimating the parameters has minimal variance.

We’ll define the term unbiased in the next section; see the Gauss-Markov Theorem in the Appendix for a precise statement and a proof of these two facts. The practical takeaway is that we’re getting optimal results as long as the model’s assumptions hold. To determine whether they do, we analyze the residuals, as they provide estimates of ε's contributions to the observations. For the UNICEF IMR data, for instance, plotting the residuals versus literacy rates yields the following:

```
>>> africa_rse = np.sqrt( africa_fit.scale )
>>> sns.residplot(data=africa, x="Literacy", y="IMR", line_kws={"
>>> plt.axhline( 2*africa_rse, linestyle=":", color="black")
>>> plt.axhline( -2*africa_rse, linestyle=":", color="black")
>>> plt.title("IMR versus Literacy residuals")
```
