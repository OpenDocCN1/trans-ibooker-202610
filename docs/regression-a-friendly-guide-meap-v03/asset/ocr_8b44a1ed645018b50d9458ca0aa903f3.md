r~ = ~ 1/(n-1) Σ_{x,y} ((x - x̄)/s_x)((y - ȳ)/s_y).

In this formula, the sum includes all of the data points (x, y),

+   - x̄ is the average of the predictor variable,
+   - ȳ is the average of the response variable,
+   - s_x is the standard deviation of the predictor variable, and
+   - s_y is the standard deviation of the response variable.

Observe how the correlation coefficient averages the product of the z-scores of the coordinates of all of the data points— this can be a handy way to remember and think about this formula.

# 2.2.2 The null model

We’ve taken care of the first two steps in the analysis of the UNICEF data: we observed a negative linear association in the scatterplot, and we found that the correlation between Literacy and IMR is about -0.62, further evidence of a linear relationship between these variables. Since the infant mortality rates for these countries seem to depend roughly linearly on the proportions of literate young women, a linear regression model is appropriate. We can’t obtain the line of averages as we did with the heights data, however, since there aren’t enough observations to organize countries into groups and then compute average infant mortality rates for the groups. Instead, we have to fit a line to the data so that estimates and predictions based on it are as accurate as possible.

The simplest way to predict the infant mortality rate for one of these countries is just to guess the average of all of the observed values of IMR, ignoring Literacy entirely. This constant prediction is the null model, a horizontal line that provides a baseline for judging other predictive models:

>>> plt.axhline( y_bar, color="black")
>>> plt.title("IMR vs Literacy: Null model")
