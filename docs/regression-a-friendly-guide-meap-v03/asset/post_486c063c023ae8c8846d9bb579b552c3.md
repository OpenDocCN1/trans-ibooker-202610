## 5.2 Binomial models for grouped responses

When we introduced the NAMCS data, we saw that plotting the individual responses was useless. On the other hand, grouping the responses by age or by decade and then plotting the condition rates for the groups proved very effective and informative. When grouping the observed responses is a reasonable option, doing so can provide a more comprehensive assessment than building a crude binary decision rule can. After developing some relevant tools and terminology, we’ll explore all of this thoroughly.

### 5.2.1 Binomial random variables and distributions

Many problems involving binary data are naturally structured as collections of *trials*: someone tries something over and over again, with the same theoretical probability of *success* on each attempt. Two standard examples are flipping a fair coin and rolling a fair 6-sided die repeatedly; as mentioned earlier, the probability of getting heads when flipping a fair coin is $\frac{1}{2}$, and the probability of rolling a 1 with a fair 6-sided die is $\frac{1}{6}$. We’ve already seen that a Bernoulli random variable models a single trial, consisting of just one attempt, so our next task is to model what happens when there are multiple trials. For starters, we have to specify the probability, $\Pi$, of success on each trial and the number, $n$, of trials. We also require that attempts are *independent*: the outcome of one trial has no effect on the outcome of any other. This setup is called a *binomial experiment*, and the associated *binomial random variable* $Y$ keeps track of the overall number of successes in the experiment. In compact notation, we summarize all of this by writing $Y \sim B(n, \Pi)$: $Y$ is a binomial random variable with $n$ trials and probability $\Pi$ of success on each trial.
