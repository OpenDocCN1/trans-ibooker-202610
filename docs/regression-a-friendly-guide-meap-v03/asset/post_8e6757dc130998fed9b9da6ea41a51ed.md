## 4.1 The curve of chance

### 4.1.1 The incidence of chronic conditions

Of the dozens of variables available in the 2009 NAMCS dataset, we’ll focus on Age, a quantitative variable providing a patient’s age in years (surprise!), and Condition, a binary variable indicating whether a patient has at least one of the following chronic conditions: arthritis, asthma, COPD, cancer, depression, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, obesity, osteoporosis, cerebrovascular disease, chronic renal failure, congestive heart failure, or ischemic heart disease. | Number | Proportion |
|--------|------------|
| Condition |  |
| 0 | 13303 | 0.423218 |
| 1 | 18130 | 0.576782 |

This gives us the simplest version of the *null model* in the present context: based on this data, a randomly chosen individual from the relevant population has a 57.7% chance of having at least one of the 14 chronic conditions listed earlier. To draw reasonable conclusions from this data and from models based on it, it’s important to specify this population carefully— it consists of people who sought and received care from doctors in the United States in 2009, since the NAMCS collects data from American physicians about the patients who visited them. People who did not participate in the American health care system that year, either by choice or due to lack of access, are not represented; chronic conditions may or may not have occurred at the same rate among those people, but the data only tell us about Americans who visited their doctors in 2009. This *sample proportion* of 57.7% is likely very close to the actual *population proportion*, which can never be known exactly; thanks to the large sample size of 31,433 people, we can be confident that the population proportion is between 57.1% and 58.3% (the standard error is about 0.3%).

### 4.1.2 Chronic conditions and age

The sample proportion is the sum of all of the values of Condition divided by the number of patients in the dataset— in other words, 57.7% is just the average of this variable. Just as with simple linear regression, the null model uses the average of the observed responses to predict all future responses; the response being predicted here is the chance that a randomly selected person has at least one chronic condition. This ignores any predictor variables that might be available, so including other information like Age should yield much better predictions. To confirm this suspicion, let’s plot the percentage of patients of each age who have a chronic condition:

```
cohorts = (chronic.groupby("Age", as_index=False)

What we observed in the first 10 rows of the dataset turns out to be true, as we would have guessed based on our own experiences and common sense: chronic conditions are indeed more likely as people get older. This plot is a bit noisy, though, and there are far too many rows in this table to digest at a glance— the ages range from 0 to 100, so cohorts has 101 rows. To get a smoother plot with a concise tabular summary, let’s organize the patients by decade and compute the percentage in each age group with a chronic condition:

```
chronic["Decade"] = 10*round(chronic.Age / 10)
decades = chronic.groupby("Decade", as_index=False).agg(
    Total=("Condition","size"),
    Afflicted=("Condition","sum"),
    Rate=("Condition","mean"))
decades
```

Summarized in this way, the incidence rates essentially increase monotonically from one age group to the next older age group. These cohort-dependent percentages, which are just the averages of Condition for each group, are empirical probabilities, and they clearly show how terrible the null model is for this data. Someone under 30 has less than a 30% chance of having a chronic condition and someone over 60 has more than a 75% chance of having a chronic condition, yet the null model predicts the chance for each of these people to be 57.7%. Knowing and using a patient’s age is much better than ignoring it! In contrast to plotting the raw 0’s and 1’s, plotting these age group percentages is very useful:

```
sns.scatterplot(data=decades, x="Decade", y="Rate")

There are several things to notice about the plot in Figure 4.3. First, it does not seem reasonable to try to fit a straight line to these points, as we did with simple linear regression; we’ll see in a moment that most of these points fall on a special nonlinear curve, the logistic curve, that gives logistic regression its name. Second, there appears to be an important transition around age 40: people older than 40 are more likely than not to have a chronic condition, while people under 40 are less likely to have a chronic condition.
